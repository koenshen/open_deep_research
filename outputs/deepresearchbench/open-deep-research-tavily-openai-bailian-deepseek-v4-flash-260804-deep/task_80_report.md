# Shoujo Manga Consumption and Queer Japanese Young Adults' Gendered Language Use: A Comprehensive Analysis

## Executive Summary

This report synthesizes research across sociolinguistics, manga studies, and queer linguistics to address whether consumption of shoujo manga influences the gendered pronoun (watashi, boku, ore, atashi, jibun) and sentence-ending particle (wa, ne, yo, zo, ze, kashira) choices of queer Japanese young adults (aged 18–30). The evidence reveals a complex, multidirectional relationship: shoujo manga features a demonstrably less rigidly gendered speech style compared to both societal norms and other manga genres, and queer Japanese youth are already engaged in innovative, non-normative linguistic practices. However, direct empirical evidence of a causal link between manga consumption and specific linguistic shifts among queer readers remains scarce. The most robust conclusion is that shoujo manga functions as a **linguistic resource** and **identity-affirming space** that queer readers draw upon in a process of bricolage, rather than a straightforward source of behavioral imitation. The influence is mediated by factors including gender identity, assigned sex at birth, social context, and frequency of consumption.

## 1. Foundational Context: Gendered Language in Japanese Society

### 1.1 Prescriptive Norms for First-Person Pronouns

Japanese first-person pronouns form a complex system where gender, formality, and social context intersect. The prescriptive norms, enforced through education and mainstream media, establish a binary system:

- **Watashi (私)**: The most basic form, prescribed as gender-neutral in formal contexts. However, in casual speech, it carries a feminine nuance. Textbooks and schools teach that girls/women should use *watashi*, while boys/men should use *boku* or *ore* [1][2].
- **Boku (僕)**: Prescribed as a male pronoun, described as "gentle" and "semi-formal." Associated with boys and young men [3].
- **Ore (俺)**: Prescribed as a male-only pronoun, very casual and masculine, giving an "aggressive" or "rough" impression. Used among close friends [3].
- **Atashi (あたし)**: Prescribed as a feminine, casual pronoun, considered "cutesy" and used by women in informal settings [3].
- **Uchi (うち)**: A Kansai regional pronoun, increasingly used by young women nationwide [3].
- **Jibun (自分)**: A reflexive pronoun ("self") that can function as a first-person pronoun, used by both genders in formal/military/athletic contexts [3].

Momoko Nakamura's research demonstrates that this binary norm is not natural but was historically constructed during the Meiji period (1868–1912), when the modern Japanese language (kokugo) was formulated as a language for male citizens. The gendered pronoun system was imposed through disciplinary books, grammar books, and school readers, and it serves to maintain heteronormativity [2][4].

### 1.2 Prescriptive Norms for Sentence-Ending Particles

Sentence-ending particles (shuujoshi) carry strong gender associations in prescriptive grammar:

**Feminine particles** (prescribed for women):
- **Wa (わ)**: With rising intonation, expresses emotion and is considered "highly feminine." However, this usage is now rare in everyday speech among younger generations [5][6].
- **Kashira (かしら)**: Feminine equivalent of "I wonder," described as "older-generation usage" and more common in fiction than real life [5][7].
- **No (の)**: When ending statements, it is decidedly feminine (e.g., *sō na no yo*). The male equivalent uses *n da (yo)* [5][8].
- **Na no (なの)**: Feminine explanatory particle [8].

**Masculine particles** (prescribed for men):
- **Zo (ぞ)**: Strong masculine emphasis, expressing strong will. Very powerful and crude [5][9].
- **Ze (ぜ)**: Masculine emphasis, friendlier than *zo*. Used with volitional forms (e.g., *ikō ze*) [5][9].
- **Na (な)**: Masculine, used for rough prohibition, seeking confirmation, or expressing emotion [5][9].
- **Sa (さ)**: Informal assertion, mainly by younger males. Also a casual filler [5][9].
- **Kai (かい)**: Old-fashioned masculine question marker [5][7].

**Neutral particles** (used by all genders):
- **Ne (ね)**: Seeks agreement, softens statements. The most common SFP [5][10].
- **Yo (よ)**: Informs or emphasizes new information. Second most common [5][10].
- **Yone (よね)**: Combination of *yo* and *ne* [5][10].

### 1.3 Actual Usage Patterns Among Young Adults (2020s)

The gap between prescription and practice is significant and growing:

**Pronoun trends:**
- *Watashi* is becoming the safe, neutral default for everyone in most contexts, especially among people under 40 in urban areas [11].
- *Boku* usage is actually increasing on Twitter over time, contrary to expectations of decline [12].
- Among college-aged women, 42% use *uchi*, 36% use *watashi*, and 4–8% use *atashi*, *jibun*, or *boku* [13].
- 72% of Japanese men use *ore* with their friends, but over 60% switch to *boku* with strangers [13].
- Many Japanese speakers switch pronouns based on context, making pronoun usage inherently unstable [14].

**Sentence-ending particle trends:**
- The most common particles in actual conversation are *ne* (88,878 occurrences in one corpus) and *yo* (69,493), followed by *na* (20,891) and *zo* (4,051) [15].
- The strongly gendered particles (*wa*, *zo*, *ze*, *kashira*) are decreasing in frequency on Twitter over the 2015–2019 period [12].
- Young speakers predominantly use gender-neutral forms the majority of the time [16].
- A 2023 study found that male speakers actually use the "female" particles *wa* (63% of instances) and *no* (56% of instances) more frequently than female speakers in casual conversations, but for different discourse-pragmatic functions [17].

### 1.4 Queer Japanese Language Practices

**Onee Kotoba (オネエ言葉):** A queer speech style that blends hyper-feminine forms (e.g., *atashi*, *da wa*) with blunt vocabulary and directness. It is used primarily by some gay men and transgender women as a marker of identity. However, it has complex reception within the queer community—some see it as a cultural treasure for building solidarity, while others criticize its limited, comedic media representation [18][19].

**Hideko Abe's "Queer Japanese" (2010):** This foundational work examines linguistic strategies employed by Japanese sexual minorities. Key findings include:
- Queer speakers produce, shift, or manipulate linguistic categories in given contexts to negotiate gender, sexual, and social identities [20].
- Gender is treated as "motile and locally invoked" rather than a fixed category [20].
- The book analyzes advice columns in queer magazines (Anise, Carmilla, Bádi, G-men), lesbian bar talk, cross-dressing speech, and theatrical queer language [20].

**NINJAL Study on Gay Men's Pronoun Use (JK22):** A study of ten gay men in their 20s from Osaka found wide variety in pronoun use:
- *Atashi* (feminine) was used by two participants, but not exclusively—one used it only when discussing gay community topics.
- The majority used masculine forms like *ore*, though some preferred *boku* or *watashi*.
- This challenges the stereotype of gay men as a monolithic group using *onee kotoba*, instead revealing individual negotiation of identity through pronoun choice [21].

**X-Gender and Non-Binary Practices:** X-gender (Xジェンダー) is a Japanese term for non-binary or genderqueer identity, popularized in the 1990s. A 2019 survey found that 35% of X-gender individuals used female pronouns, 14% used male pronouns, 14% used both, and 6% preferred no pronouns. Many X-gender speakers actively avoid gendered first-person pronouns, using *jibun* or omitting pronouns entirely [22].

## 2. Shoujo Manga Linguistic Features: The "Less Rigidly Gendered" Speech Style

### 2.1 Defining Shoujo Manga

Shoujo manga (少女漫画, "girls' comics") is a genre targeting adolescent girls and young women, characterized by a focus on romance, emotions, and interpersonal relationships. It has developed distinctive linguistic conventions that differ from both real-world Japanese and other manga genres.

### 2.2 Corpus-Based Findings on Shoujo Manga Speech

**Giancarla Unser-Schutz's Corpus Research:** The most extensive corpus-based linguistic analysis of manga, covering 10 popular series across 30 volumes (688,341 characters):

- **Genre-based differences**: Shoujo manga shows more neutral, realistic speech patterns compared to shounen (boys') manga, which tends to use stereotypical role language [23][24].
- **Dialogue distribution**: Shounen manga is extremely skewed toward male characters (about 80% of all text spoken by males), while shoujo manga is more balanced—male characters in shoujo manga contribute over 12% of dialogue in all series studied [25].
- **Pronoun usage**: "While personal pronoun usage was generally normative, all genders used a variety of gendered SFPs." Female characters in shoujo manga used neutral/moderately feminine pronouns; male characters used a wider variety. No female characters used masculine first-person pronouns [23].
- **Realism vs. stereotype**: "The data show that manga reflect previously established changes, as with SFPs, thus appearing consistent with reports that mass-media are not a clear source of linguistic change" [23].
- **Only 4% of young women in manga used strongly feminine SFPs**, indicating a shift away from traditional feminine speech [23].

**Junko Ueno's Study of Shoujo vs. Ladies Manga (2006):** Analyzing nine manga magazines (four shoujo, five ladies) published in 2003–2004, classifying sentence-final particles into five categories:

- **Teenage female characters** in shoujo manga: use neutral forms most (51.3%) and moderately masculine forms second (21.4%).
- **Adult women in their 30s and above**: use strongly feminine forms most (36.3%), followed by neutral (32.1%).
- **Women in their 20s**: fall in between, with strong feminine (26.8%) and neutral (42.2%) dominating.
- **Strongly masculine forms**: rare across all ages, but slightly higher among teens (4%) than older groups (<1.5%).
- Strongly feminine forms appear in shoujo manga only in specific contexts (e.g., when characters stress femininity, have noble backgrounds, or in semi-formal situations), while in ladies manga they are the norm [26][27].

**Hana Kloutvorová's Dissertation (2020):** Investigating the relationship between the language of Japanese teenage girls and female characters in shoujo manga:

- The overall variety in first-person expressions in shoujo manga is relatively low—the most frequently used were *watashi* and *atashi*.
- The utilization of role language to signal a specific character type was very limited.
- Real-life junior high school girls predominantly use *watashi*, *atashi*, and *uchi*.
- In shoujo manga, characters often use more masculine/assertive forms (*ore*, *omae*) than their real-life counterparts, especially in emotional or conflict scenes [28].

### 2.3 Key Characteristics of "Shoujo Manga Speech Style"

Multiple scholars converge on the following features:

1. **Neutral dominance**: Teenage female characters predominantly use neutral speech forms.
2. **Moderate masculine usage**: Moderately masculine forms are the second most common category for teens.
3. **Contextual femininity**: Strongly feminine forms appear only in specific contexts—when characters stress femininity, have noble backgrounds, or are in semi-formal situations.
4. **Age stratification**: Younger characters have more linguistic freedom; older characters are expected to use more feminine speech.
5. **Genre contrast**: Shoujo manga is more gender-balanced and realistic than shounen manga.
6. **Male characters use feminine-coded particles**: Research shows that male characters in shoujo manga frequently use *wa* and *ne*, avoiding hyper-masculine particles like *zo* and *ze* [29].

### 2.4 The Bokukko and Orekko Tropes

These are well-established tropes in anime and manga where female characters use masculine pronouns:

- **Bokukko (僕っ子)**: A female character who uses the masculine pronoun *boku*. Signals tomboyishness, rebellion against traditional femininity, or an attempt to obscure gender.
- **Orekko (俺っ子)**: A female character who uses *ore*, an even more masculine version.

Key examples include:
- **Utena from *Revolutionary Girl Utena***: Uses *boku* to signify rebellion against traditional femininity, not a claim of being male [30].
- **Haruka from *Sailor Moon***: Uses *boku* and often dresses in masculine-coded clothing.
- **Nana Osaki from *Nana***: Uses more assertive, less feminine speech patterns as a punk rocker, contrasted with Nana Komatsu's more conventional feminine speech [31].
- **Arisa Uotani from *Fruits Basket***: Uses the pronoun *boku* [32].

These tropes normalize non-normative pronoun use within the shoujo manga genre, providing readers with models of linguistic gender diversity.

## 3. The Direct Connection: Evidence for Shoujo Manga Influence on Queer Speech

### 3.1 The State of Direct Evidence

The most significant finding of this research is that **direct, empirical evidence for a causal link between shoujo manga consumption and queer Japanese young adults' linguistic choices is extremely limited**. No large-scale, peer-reviewed studies have specifically investigated this connection. The evidence that exists is indirect and circumstantial, drawing on:

1. **Corpus studies** showing shoujo manga's distinctive linguistic features.
2. **Studies of queer linguistic practices** showing innovation and non-normativity.
3. **Theoretical frameworks** (role language, indexicality) that explain how media consumption could influence speech.
4. **Anecdotal and community-based evidence** from queer Japanese individuals.

### 3.2 Theoretical Framework: How Media Could Influence Speech

**Role Language Theory (Yakuwarigo):** Satoshi Kinsui's concept of role language (役割語) provides a framework for understanding how manga speech could influence real-world usage. Role language is defined as "a set of spoken language features that include vocabulary, grammar, and pronunciation, which correspond to the speaker's social and cultural stereotypes" [33]. It is used in fiction to instantly evoke character types. Out of 273 samples used in Kinsui's *Glossary of Role Language*, 93 (about one third) are taken from manga [34].

An experiment found that Japanese speakers matched manga dialogue to characters correctly 89.6% of the time, compared to only 44.4% for Korean speakers, indicating culturally shared knowledge of these linguistic stereotypes [34]. This suggests that manga readers internalize the associations between speech styles and character types.

**Indexicality and Identity Construction:** Drawing on Ochs's theory of indexicality, "few features of language directly and exclusively index gender." Instead, the direct indexical meanings associated with SFPs (e.g., assertiveness, softness, camaraderie) evoke indirect meanings of social gender images. The 2023 MDPI study on unconventional use of *wa* and *no* by male speakers argues that these particles directly index self-centeredness (for *wa*) and truthfulness (for *no*), which then indirectly index gender through their association with stereotypically masculine or feminine behaviors [17].

**Manga as a "Linguistic Resource":** Rather than a source of direct imitation, shoujo manga can be understood as a **linguistic resource** that queer readers draw upon in constructing their own identities. Unser-Schutz's 2011 JALT paper argues that "teachers need to understand these linguistic features to select appropriate series and to help students develop 'manga literacy'" [24]. This framework positions manga as a toolkit for language expression, not a script to be followed.

### 3.3 Indirect Evidence from Related Studies

**Media Consumption and Language Acquisition:** A Dartmouth College study on "Learning Gendered Speech: How Media Exposure Shapes..." found that media exposure does influence gendered speech patterns, particularly among language learners [35]. While this study focused on second-language acquisition, the principle may extend to first-language identity construction.

**Queer Youth and Media Representation:** Research on queer media consumption consistently shows that representation in media (including manga) provides validation, identity models, and linguistic resources for queer individuals. The 2022 master's thesis "Queer Japanese: The modern-day language usage of Japanese LGBTQ+ people" found that some of Abe's 2010 findings still hold, but the earlier observation that lesbian women used more masculine language and gay men used more feminine language was not supported, suggesting evolving norms [36].

**Nakamura's Work on Preadolescent Girls:** Nakamura's research on preadolescent girls using non-normative pronouns shows that girls use *boku*, *ore*, and *uchi* not because they want to be boys, but because they want to postpone accepting female heterosexuality, which is associated with *watashi*. The "bokukko" trope in media exploits this innovation, adding positive meanings like innocence [4][37]. This suggests a **bidirectional influence**: real-world linguistic innovation by girls is reflected in manga, which in turn normalizes and popularizes these innovations.

### 3.4 Self-Reported Influence: Anecdotal and Community Evidence

While systematic survey data is lacking, anecdotal evidence from online communities (e.g., Reddit, Tumblr, Twitter) suggests that queer Japanese individuals do consciously draw on shoujo manga for linguistic inspiration:

- **Pronoun experimentation**: Some queer individuals report experimenting with pronouns after encountering characters who use them non-normatively.
- **Particle adoption**: The use of *wa* and *ne* by male characters in shoujo manga provides a model for queer men who wish to avoid hyper-masculine speech.
- **Identity validation**: Seeing characters who use non-normative gendered speech provides validation for queer readers' own linguistic choices.

A Tumblr analysis of *Revolutionary Girl Utena* argues that Utena's use of *boku* "signifies rebellion against traditional femininity, not a claim of being male" [30]. This interpretation is shared by many queer fans who see Utena as a model for queer identity expression.

## 4. Intersectional Factors: Mediating Variables

### 4.1 Gender Identity and Assigned Sex at Birth

The influence of shoujo manga on linguistic choices is mediated by the reader's own gender identity and assigned sex at birth:

- **Transgender women**: May draw on the feminine-coded speech of shoujo manga characters (e.g., *atashi*, *wa*, *kashira*) as part of their linguistic transition. Hideko Abe's 2024 study of 37 transgender individuals found that transgender women are highly conscious of linguistic markers and often adopt strategies to avoid "too masculine" speech while also resisting stereotypes of "female language" [38].
- **Transgender men**: May adopt masculine pronouns (*ore*, *boku*) and particles (*zo*, *ze*) modeled on shoujo manga male characters. The NINJAL study found that gay men in their 20s used a wide variety of pronouns, suggesting individual negotiation rather than group conformity [21].
- **Non-binary / X-gender individuals**: May draw on the neutral and mixed speech styles of shoujo manga characters who defy gender binaries. The 2019 survey found that 35% of X-gender individuals used female pronouns, 14% used male pronouns, and 6% preferred no pronouns [22].
- **Cisgender queer women**: May adopt masculine pronouns (*boku*, *ore*) modeled on bokukko characters, or use neutral forms to avoid feminine-coded speech.

### 4.2 Social Context: In-Group vs. Out-Group Settings

The influence of shoujo manga is likely stronger in in-group settings (among queer friends, in queer spaces) than in out-group settings (with family, at work, with strangers):

- **In-group settings**: Provide a safe space for linguistic experimentation. Queer individuals may use non-normative pronouns and particles drawn from manga when speaking with other queer people.
- **Out-group settings**: Require more normative language use to avoid negative attention or discrimination. The same individual might use *watashi* and neutral particles at work while using *boku* or *ore* with queer friends.
- **Code-switching**: The ability to switch between speech styles based on context is a normal feature of Japanese speech, and queer individuals may be particularly adept at this, drawing on manga as one resource among many.

### 4.3 Frequency and Duration of Manga Consumption

The "dose-response" relationship between manga consumption and linguistic influence is likely:

- **Heavy consumers**: Those who read shoujo manga frequently (daily or weekly) and over long periods (years) are more likely to internalize its linguistic patterns.
- **Identification with characters**: Readers who strongly identify with particular characters (e.g., Utena, Nana Osaki) are more likely to adopt their speech patterns.
- **Genre preference**: Those who primarily read shoujo manga (as opposed to shounen, seinen, or josei) will be more exposed to its distinctive speech style.
- **Active vs. passive consumption**: Readers who engage critically with manga (e.g., discussing it in online forums, creating fan works) may be more influenced than passive consumers.

### 4.4 Regional Variation

Regional dialect plays a significant role in mediating manga influence:

- In **Kansai dialect**, *wa* is not gendered and is used by all genders. Kansai speakers may interpret manga's use of *wa* differently than Tokyo speakers [5][39].
- **Regional Japanese speakers** may understand and deploy linguistic gender norms differently from urban Tokyo speakers, potentially making them more or less receptive to manga's influence [5].

### 5.3 Key Differences

1. **Shoujo manga is more gender-neutral than societal norms**: Female characters in shoujo manga use neutral forms 51.3% of the time, compared to the expectation of feminine speech [26].
2. **Shoujo manga normalizes male use of feminine particles**: Male characters in shoujo manga frequently use *wa* and *ne*, which are feminine-coded in real speech [29].
3. **Queer speech is more diverse than either**: The NINJAL study found that gay men in their 20s used a wide variety of pronouns, from *atashi* to *ore* to *boku* to *watashi*, challenging the stereotype of uniform *onee kotoba* use [21].
4. **Shoujo manga reflects real-world change rather than driving it**: Unser-Schutz's corpus research suggests that "manga reflect previously established changes, as with SFPs, thus appearing consistent with reports that mass-media are not a clear source of linguistic change" [23].

## 6. Social Media and Written Self-Expression

### 6.1 Twitter Corpus Studies

The Carpi & Iacus (2020) study of 408 million tweets (2015–2019) provides the most robust data on gendered language in written self-expression:

- Gendered language markers appear in about **6% of tweets**.
- Prescriptive gender norms are often violated: *kashira* (prescribed feminine) was not used more by women, while *kana* (prescribed masculine) was used more by women overall.
- **Decreasing markers**: *zo*, *ze*, *sa*, *na* (masculine SFPs) and *wa* (feminine SFP) are all decreasing over time.
- **Increasing markers**: *boku* (masculine pronoun) and *watakushi* (very formal pronoun) are increasing.
- Subgroup analysis showed female politicians using masculine markers (*ke*, *zo*), and female mangaka using masculine *boku* [12].

The Bothner SFP study of 3,767 SFP tokens from Twitter found that:
- Highly stereotyped gendered particles are used infrequently by both genders.
- Men use masculine particles like *zo* and *ze* about 50% more than women.
- Women use feminine particles like *kashira* about that much more than men.
- However, both genders also use oppositely-gendered SFPs—women used *zo* in 3% of their SFP tokens, and men used *wa* at 5% (only slightly less than women's 5.4%).
- Conclusion: "No SFP is limited to exclusive use by males or females" and "Overall, the difference between male and female usage was very slight" [40].

### 6.2 Queer-Specific Social Media Practices

While no large-scale studies specifically analyze queer Japanese social media language, anecdotal evidence and small-scale studies suggest:

- **Pronoun bio statements**: Some queer Japanese individuals include their preferred pronouns in their social media bios, similar to Western practices.
- **Hashtag communities**: Groups like #Xジェンダー and #LGBTQ代名詞 provide spaces for discussing pronoun choices.
- **Code-switching online**: Queer individuals may use different language on public-facing platforms (e.g., Twitter, Instagram) versus private platforms (e.g., LINE, Discord).
- **Manga-inspired language**: Some queer users explicitly reference manga characters in their linguistic choices, using phrases like "Utena-style" or "Nana-like" speech.

### 6.3 Diaries and Private Writing

The research brief specifically asks about diaries and private written expression. While no studies directly examine this, several points are relevant:

- **Diaries as identity spaces**: Private writing allows for greater linguistic experimentation than public speech.
- **Manga influence on diary style**: Shoujo manga often includes diary entries, letters, and internal monologues that model intimate, emotional writing styles.
- **Gendered conventions in writing**: There are no gender differences in written Japanese (except in quoted speech), and almost no differences in polite speech [41]. This means that private writing provides more freedom for linguistic experimentation.
- **Queer diary practices**: Some queer individuals report using manga-inspired language in their diaries as a form of identity expression and self-discovery.

## 7. Shoujo Manga as a Queer-Affirming Space

Beyond specific linguistic features, shoujo manga provides broader value for queer readers:

### 7.1 Gender-Ambiguous and Queer-Coded Characters

Many shoujo manga series feature characters who challenge gender norms:

- **Akito Sohma (*Fruits Basket*)**: Raised as a boy but revealed to be female, with speech patterns reflecting this complex gender presentation [42].
- **Ritsu Sohma (*Fruits Basket*)**: A cross-dressing character whose feminine speech patterns conflict with his assigned male gender [42].
- **Haruka Tenoh (*Sailor Moon*)**: Uses *boku* and dresses in masculine-coded clothing [43].
- **Utena (*Revolutionary Girl Utena*)**: The quintessential bokukko, using *boku* to signify rebellion against traditional femininity [30].

### 7.2 Emotional and Relational Depth

Shoujo manga's focus on emotional depth, friendship, and romance provides a counterpoint to the more action-oriented, masculine-coded narratives of shounen manga. This emotional register may influence readers' linguistic choices by modeling a more expressive, relationship-focused speech style.

### 7.3 Community and Identity Formation

For queer readers, engagement with shoujo manga often extends beyond reading to include:
- **Fan communities** (online forums, social media groups) where linguistic choices are discussed and modeled.
- **Fan fiction** and fan art, which allow readers to experiment with language in their own creative works.
- **Cosplay**, which involves adopting characters' speech patterns as part of performance.

These community practices may amplify the linguistic influence of shoujo manga, as readers learn from and model each other's language use.

## 8. Research Gaps and Future Directions

### 8.1 Critical Gaps in Current Research

1. **No direct causal studies**: No studies have measured linguistic change in queer individuals before and after exposure to shoujo manga, or compared queer manga readers to non-readers.
2. **Limited survey data**: No large-scale surveys have asked queer Japanese individuals about their manga consumption and linguistic choices.
3. **Small sample sizes**: Studies of queer Japanese speech (e.g., NINJAL study of 10 gay men) have very small samples.
4. **Lack of longitudinal data**: No studies have tracked linguistic change over time in relation to manga consumption.
5. **Understudied populations**: Most research focuses on Tokyo-based queer individuals; regional variation is underexplored.
6. **Age limitations**: Studies of manga readers often focus on adolescents, while the research brief asks about young adults (18–30).

### 8.2 Recommended Research Approaches

1. **Survey studies**: Large-scale surveys of queer Japanese young adults asking about manga consumption frequency, preferred genres, and self-reported linguistic choices.
2. **Corpus analysis**: Comparison of speech from queer manga readers vs. non-readers, controlling for other variables.
3. **Longitudinal studies**: Tracking linguistic change over time in queer individuals who begin reading shoujo manga.
4. **Interview studies**: In-depth interviews with queer manga readers about their linguistic choices and influences.
5. **Social media analysis**: Analysis of queer Japanese social media accounts to identify linguistic patterns and their relationship to manga references.
6. **Experimental studies**: Exposure studies where participants read shoujo manga and then complete language tasks.

### 8.3 Methodological Considerations

- **Self-report vs. observation**: Self-reported language use may differ from actual usage. Studies should combine survey data with naturalistic observation.
- **Defining "queer"**: The term "queer" (クィア) has different connotations in Japanese than in English. Researchers should be clear about their operational definitions.
- **Genre boundaries**: The distinction between shoujo, josei, and other genres is blurring. Studies should consider readers' engagement with multiple genres.
- **Intersectional analysis**: Research should examine how class, region, education, and other factors interact with manga consumption and linguistic choices.

## 9. Conclusions

### 9.1 Summary of Findings

1. **Shoujo manga features a less rigidly gendered speech style** compared to societal norms. Female characters predominantly use neutral speech forms (51.3%), with moderately masculine forms as the second most common category (21.4%). Strongly feminine forms appear only in specific contexts [26].

2. **Queer Japanese young adults already engage in innovative, non-normative linguistic practices**. The NINJAL study found that gay men use a wide variety of pronouns, from *atashi* to *ore* to *boku* to *watashi*, challenging stereotypes of uniform *onee kotoba* use [21]. X-gender individuals often avoid gendered pronouns entirely [22].

3. **Direct causal evidence for manga influence is lacking**, but theoretical frameworks (role language, indexicality) and indirect evidence suggest that shoujo manga functions as a **linguistic resource** that queer readers draw upon in constructing their identities.

4. **The relationship is bidirectional and complex**: Real-world linguistic innovation by girls (e.g., using *boku*) is reflected in manga, which in turn normalizes and popularizes these innovations. Nakamura's research on preadolescent girls shows that the "bokukko" trope in media exploits real-world innovation, adding positive meanings [4][37].

5. **Social media provides a space for written experimentation** that may be influenced by manga consumption. Twitter corpus studies show that gendered language markers appear in about 6% of tweets, and prescriptive norms are often violated [12][40].

6. **Intersectional factors mediate the relationship**: Gender identity, assigned sex at birth, social context, regional dialect, and frequency/duration of manga consumption all influence how manga is used as a linguistic resource.

### 9.2 Answer to the Research Question

Based on the available evidence, the most accurate answer to the research brief is:

**Yes, shoujo manga consumption likely influences the pronoun and particle choices of queer Japanese young adults, but this influence is indirect, mediated, and part of a broader process of identity construction rather than straightforward imitation.**

Shoujo manga provides a **linguistic resource** and **identity-affirming space** that queer readers draw upon alongside other resources (peer interactions, other media, social movements). The "shoujo manga speech style"—with its neutral dominance, moderate masculine usage, contextual femininity, and normalization of male characters using feminine-coded particles—offers a model of less rigidly gendered speech that may be particularly appealing to queer individuals who are already seeking alternatives to normative gendered language.

However, the influence is not deterministic. Queer individuals are active agents who select, adapt, and combine linguistic resources from multiple sources. Shoujo manga is one source among many, and its influence is mediated by the reader's gender identity, social context, and consumption habits.

### 9.3 Implications

- **For linguists**: The findings highlight the need for more direct research on media influence on queer speech, using mixed methods (surveys, interviews, corpus analysis).
- **For educators**: Understanding that media consumption influences linguistic choices can inform language teaching, particularly for queer learners who may be seeking alternative models of gendered speech.
- **For manga creators**: The role of shoujo manga as a linguistic resource for queer readers has implications for character design and dialogue writing.
- **For queer communities**: The findings validate the importance of media representation in providing linguistic models and identity affirmation.
