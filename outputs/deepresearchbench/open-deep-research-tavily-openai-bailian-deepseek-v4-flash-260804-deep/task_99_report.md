# A Comprehensive Overview of Light-Based Therapies in Aesthetic Medicine: Laser, Intense Pulsed Light, and LED Phototherapy for Photoaging, Skin Whitening, and Hyperpigmentation (2020–2026)

## Introduction

Light-based therapies have become cornerstones of aesthetic medicine, offering non-invasive or minimally invasive solutions for photoaging, skin whitening/brightening, and hyperpigmentation (including age spots, melasma, and post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation). This report synthesizes recent scientific advancements (2020–2026) across three major platforms—laser, intense pulsed light (IPL), and light-emitting diode (LED) therapy—drawing exclusively on original peer-reviewed research. The fundamental mechanisms, clinical efficacy, safety profiles, and evolving protocols are examined for each modality.

---

## 1. Fundamental Mechanisms of Light-Based Therapies

### 1.1 Selective Photothermolysis and Chromophore Targeting

The foundational principle governing all light-based therapies is selective photothermolysis, first described by Anderson and Parrish in 1983. This theory enables targeted destruction of specific chromophores—melanin, hemoglobin, and water—by careful selection of wavelength, pulse duration, and fluence. Melanin serves as the primary target for hyperpigmentation and skin whitening treatments, with absorption peaks at 532 nm, 694 nm (ruby), 755 nm (alexandrite), and 1064 nm (Nd:YAG). Water absorption governs ablative and fractional resurfacing at 2940 nm (Er:YAG) and 10,600 nm (CO₂), while hemoglobin is targeted for vascular lesions at 532 nm and 585–595 nm (pulsed dye laser) [1].

### 1.2 Photoacoustic and Photomechanical Effects (Picosecond Domain)

Picosecond lasers (pulse width <550 ps, typically 300–900 ps) operate via photoacoustic/photomechanical disruption rather than purely thermal effects. The ultra-short pulse defeats the stress relaxation time of pigment targets, enabling fragmentation into smaller particles with less thermal damage to surrounding tissue [2]. At sub-nanosecond pulse widths, efficacy is extended through this mechanism, allowing for more effective pigment destruction with reduced collateral damage [3].

### 1.3 Laser-Induced Optical Breakdown and Cavitation

Fractional picosecond lasers produce laser-induced optical breakdown (LIOB) in the epidermis and laser-induced cavitation (LIC) in the dermis, stimulating skin regeneration and dermal remodeling. The LIOB process proceeds stepwise: pulse absorption by melanin, plasma formation and micro-explosion within melanosomes, then fragmentation and phagocyte clearance [4]. Higher energy levels result in more superficial intraepidermal LIOBs, while lower energy settings produce deeper intradermal LICs [5].

### 1.4 Subcellular Selective Photothermolysis (Laser Toning)

Low-fluence Q-switched Nd:YAG laser (LFQSNY, known as laser toning) operates via subcellular selective photothermolysis—destroying mature melanosomes without killing melanocytes. This sublethal injury to melanocytes reduces the risk of post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation (PIH) and is particularly valuable for melasma treatment in darker skin types [6].

### 1.5 Photobiomodulation (LED Therapy)

Photobiomodulation (PBM) involves the absorption of photons by endogenous chromophores, most notably cytochrome c oxidase (COX) in mitochondrial complex IV. COX absorbs red (~665 nm) and near-infrared (~810 nm) light, increasing ATP synthesis, modulating reactive oxygen species (ROS), and enhancing nitric oxide (NO) production. This triggers downstream signaling pathways that regulate cellular metabolism, proliferation, and inflammatory responses. A biphasic dose response (Arndt-Schulz law) governs PBM: low doses produce therapeutic effects, while higher doses may be less effective or even inhibitory [7][8][9].

## 2. Laser Therapy for Photoaging, Skin Whitening, and Hyperpigmentation

### 2.1 Picosecond Lasers

#### 2.1.1 Mechanisms and Wavelengths

Picosecond lasers emit ultra-short pulses and operate via photo-thermo-mechanical disruption (PTMD) and LIOB, fragmenting pigment and stimulating dermal remodeling with minimal thermal damage. Available wavelengths include 532 nm (frequency-doubled Nd:YAG), 730 nm, 755 nm (alexandrite), and 1064 nm (Nd:YAG). Fractional delivery systems (diffractive lens arrays, microlens arrays, holographic matrices) concentrate energy into microspots while sparing surrounding tissue [2][5].

#### 2.1.2 Picosecond Lasers for Melasma

**Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis (Feng et al., 2023):** A systematic review and meta-analysis published in *Lasers in Medical Science* evaluating the efficacy and safety of picosecond laser therapy for melasma concluded that the 1064 nm picosecond laser is safe and effective for melasma treatment. The efficacy of other picosecond laser wavelengths remains unconfirmed and requires further validation through large-scale randomized controlled trials [10].

**Network Meta-Analysis (Beijing Hospital, 2023):** A network meta-analysis published in *Dermatologic Surgery* systematically evaluated 20 studies with 1,182 patients. The analysis found that combination therapy with aminomethyl cyclic acid and 1064-nm picosecond laser likely offers the highest effective rate. Low-power fractional CO₂ laser resulted in the lowest melasma area and severity index (MASI) score after treatment, whereas IPL had the highest incidence of adverse reactions [11].

**Picosecond Laser + 2% Hydroquinone (Choi et al., 2017):** This randomized controlled trial evaluated a novel picosecond laser combining 1064 nm and 595 nm wavelengths plus topical 2% hydroquinone (HQ) for melasma in Korean patients. In a split-face design, 39 subjects received 7 weeks of daily 2% HQ on both sides, with weekly picosecond laser on one side for 5 weeks. The combination therapy was significantly superior: 76.92% of laser-treated sides achieved ≥51% improvement in relative lightness values vs only 2.56% on HQ alone. No serious adverse events occurred [12].

**Picosecond Nd:YAG (1064 nm) vs. Picosecond Alexandrite (755 nm) vs. 2% Hydroquinone (Frontiers, 2023):** This randomized, controlled, assessor-blinded clinical trial (ChiCTR2100050089) compared non-fractional picosecond Nd:YAG laser (PSNYL, 1064 nm), non-fractional picosecond alexandrite laser (PSAL, 755 nm), and 2% HQ cream for melasma in 60 female patients with Fitzpatrick skin types III–IV. All groups showed significant improvement from baseline. PSNYL achieved the greatest MASI reduction, significantly better than PSAL (p=0.016) and HQ (p=0.018). Patient satisfaction scores were highest in the PSNYL group. Adverse events were mild and transient; recurrence occurred in 6.8% of patients [13].

**Picosecond Laser + Sodium Hyaluronate for Melasma (Zou et al., 2025):** This retrospective study evaluated fractional picosecond laser combined with non-cross-linked sodium hyaluronate composite solution for mixed-type melasma in 30 female patients (Fitzpatrick skin types III–IV). MASI scores decreased significantly from 18.30 ± 7.85 to 8.20 ± 3.50 (55.2% reduction, P < 0.05). Physician's Global Assessment showed marked or moderate improvement in 70% of patients, and patient satisfaction was 86.7%. Side effects were mild and transient, with no PIH reported [14].

**755-nm Alexandrite Picosecond Laser for Melasma (PubMed, 2024):** A review of the 755-nm alexandrite picosecond laser, FDA-approved for benign pigmented lesions, found that monotherapy achieved 50-75% improvement in 40% of patients and significant MASI reduction (p<0.001). Combining laser with topical tranexamic acid (TTA) showed superior hemi-MASI improvement and patient satisfaction at 1 and 3 months post-treatment (p<0.05) compared to laser alone. Hydroquinone plus laser did not outperform hydroquinone alone [15].

**250-Picosecond Laser for Melasma and Pigmented Lesions in Korea (2023):** This retrospective study evaluated a 250-picosecond, 1,064-nm Nd:YAG laser (Picocare Majesty) for melasma and pigmented lesions in 87 patients at Soonchunhyang University Bucheon Hospital. Patients received 3–5 sessions of low-fluence laser toning (0.1–0.6 J/cm²). Results showed statistically significant reductions in polarized light pigmentation (from 22.38 to 20.02, p<0.001) and UV pigmentation (from 22.10 to 20.06, p<0.001). Skin tone improved significantly for the full face (55.57 to 55.91, p=0.035) [16].

**Picosecond 755-nm Alexandrite Laser for Melasma and PIH in Asian Patients (2017):** This study evaluated picosecond 755-nm alexandrite laser for treating melasma and PIH in three Korean patients (skin type IV) who had stopped responding to conventional Q-switched laser toning. All treatments were well tolerated with no adverse events. An ex vivo skin model confirmed that the picosecond laser caused less epidermal keratinocyte necrosis than a 532-nm QS laser while effectively reducing melanin content [17].

**PicoSure Pro FDA Clearance for Melasma:** The PicoSure Pro laser is the first and only picosecond laser granted FDA-clearance for treatment of melasma pigment (as well as Nevus of Ota and Hori's Macules). Clinical evidence from 9 publications (170 subjects) shows significant melasma clearance: a 28% MASI improvement at 12 weeks, 39% at 20 weeks, and 38% at 1 year in one study; another study found statistically significant improvement over Q-switched alexandrite laser [18].

#### 2.1.3 Picosecond Lasers for Photoaging and Skin Rejuvenation

**Fractional 1064 nm Nd:YAG Picosecond Laser for Asian Skin Rejuvenation (PMC, 2025):** This study evaluated fractional 1064 nm Nd:YAG picosecond laser for skin rejuvenation in 44 Vietnamese participants. Objective assessments using VISIA® showed statistically significant improvements in wrinkles, spots, texture, pores, and UV spots from the first month, sustained through three months (p<0.0001). Patient-reported satisfaction was high, with 70.45% reporting moderate improvement. Epidemiological analysis revealed that prolonged daily sun exposure (>60 minutes) significantly reduced UV spot improvement (p=0.01) [19].

**Fractional 1064 nm Nd:YAG Picosecond Laser vs. Fractional Q-Switched 1064-nm Nd:YAG Laser for Photoaging (Tang et al., 2025):** This split-face study compared fractional picosecond and fractional Q-switched 1064 nm Nd:YAG lasers for facial photoaging in 30 patients (Fitzpatrick skin types III–IV). Both lasers showed significant improvements in wrinkles, pores, and skin texture (p < 0.001), with no statistically significant differences between them. Histology confirmed increased collagen and improved elastic fiber alignment in both groups. FxPico caused significantly more pain (VAS 2.84 vs. 1.48, p < 0.0001) [20].

**Picosecond 1064-nm Nd:YAG Laser with Multi-Beneficial Serum for Photoaging (PMC, 2025):** This randomized, double-blind, split-face, placebo-controlled clinical trial evaluated a multi-beneficial serum (containing plant extracts, peptides, and ergothioneine) combined with a single 1064-nm picosecond Nd:YAG laser treatment for facial photoaging in 24 women. The test serum significantly improved skin barrier recovery (lower TEWL, higher SCH) and enhanced photoaging outcomes (greater reduction in fine wrinkles, mottled pigmentation, dullness, and crow's feet area) compared to placebo [21].

**Clinical Improvement of Photoaging-Associated Facial Hyperpigmentation in Korean Skin with Picosecond 1064-nm Nd:YAG Laser (2020):** This study evaluated picosecond 1064-nm Nd:YAG laser for photoaging-associated facial pigmentation in Korean subjects, concluding that the treatment is safe and effective for improving various photoaging-associated pigmentation conditions [22].

### 2.2 Q-Switched Lasers

#### 2.2.1 Q-Switched Nd:YAG for Melasma and Pigmented Lesions

Q-switched Nd:YAG lasers (1064 nm and 532 nm) remain a mainstay for treating pigmented lesions and melasma. The 1064 nm wavelength is preferred for darker skin types due to deeper penetration and reduced melanin absorption, lowering the risk of PIH. Low-fluence 1064 nm Nd:YAG (laser toning) is widely used for melasma, though multiple sessions are required and recurrence is common without maintenance [6].

A meta-analysis evaluating Q-switched laser combined with IPL versus either monotherapy for treating melasma (31 controlled trials, 2,801 patients) showed that combination therapy significantly reduced MASI scores (SMD = -1.20, 95% CI: -1.36 to -1.05), achieved a higher clinical efficacy rate (92.0% vs. 77.4%; RR = 1.10), and lowered recurrence rate (8.5% vs. 17.8%; RR = 0.54) compared to monotherapy. No significant differences were found in adverse event rates (RR = 0.92) [23].

#### 2.2.2 Q-Switched Alexandrite and Ruby Lasers

Q-switched alexandrite (755 nm) and ruby (694 nm) lasers are highly effective for epidermal pigmented lesions such as solar lentigines, ephelides, and café-au-lait macules. The 755 nm wavelength offers a balance between melanin absorption and depth penetration, making it suitable for Fitzpatrick skin types I–III. PIH rates are higher in darker skin types, necessitating careful patient selection and parameter adjustment [6].

### 2.3 Fractional Lasers

#### 2.3.1 Ablative Fractional Resurfacing (CO₂ and Er:YAG)

Ablative fractional lasers (10,600 nm CO₂ and 2940 nm Er:YAG) create microscopic columns of thermal injury, stimulating collagen remodeling and resurfacing. These are highly effective for photoaging, rhytides, and textural irregularities but carry significant downtime and risk of PIH, particularly in darker skin types. Recent studies have focused on optimizing parameters to minimize adverse effects.

For melasma, a network meta-analysis found that low-power fractional CO₂ laser resulted in the lowest MASI score after treatment, suggesting a role in recalcitrant cases [11]. However, ablative lasers are generally reserved for Fitzpatrick skin types I–III due to the high risk of PIH in darker skin.

#### 2.3.2 Non-Ablative Fractional Lasers (1550 nm Erbium-Glass)

Non-ablative fractional lasers (NAFL) at 1550 nm create microscopic thermal zones without disrupting the epidermis, stimulating dermal collagen with minimal downtime. NAFL is effective for photoaging, acne scars, and melasma. A randomized controlled trial comparing IPL combined with NAFL (IPL-NAFL) versus picosecond alexandrite laser with diffractive lens array (PSAL-DLA) for noninvasive facial rejuvenation in 17 Chinese women found that IPL-NAFL significantly improved pores compared to baseline (p<0.05), while PSAL-DLA significantly improved brown spots (p<0.01). PSAL-DLA caused less pain (p<0.01), shorter recovery from erythema/edema (p<0.001), lower incidence of PIH (23.5% vs. 58.8%, p<0.05), and higher patient satisfaction (p<0.01) [24].

## 3. Intense Pulsed Light (IPL) Therapy

### 3.1 Mechanism of Action

IPL devices use a flashlamp to emit polychromatic, noncoherent, noncollimated light across a broad wavelength spectrum of approximately 400 to 1400 nm. This broad spectrum allows for versatile treatment of multiple chromophores simultaneously. The working basis rests on selective photothermolysis, with melanin, hemoglobin, and water as targets. Cut-off filters (e.g., 515–755 nm) allow selective treatment of different targets [25].

IPL promotes collagen remodeling via selective photothermolysis, while photodynamic therapy (PDT) adds photochemical effects that upregulate heat shock proteins and matrix metalloproteinases, enhancing collagen synthesis. IPL irradiation increases the mRNA level of collagen I and exerts bidirectional influence on MMP-1 and TGF-β1 secretion, representing a potential mechanism for skin rejuvenation [26][27].

### 3.2 IPL for Photoaging and Skin Rejuvenation

**Comprehensive Real-World Study (Fan et al., 2026):** A comprehensive real-world study (2020–2025) evaluated long-term, regular IPL therapy for facial rejuvenation in 236 patients using the Lumenis M22 system with fluence 10–18 J/cm², pulse duration 5–20 ms, and 515–560 nm filters. Significant improvements were observed in erythema, pigmentation, and wrinkle indices after treatment (all p < 0.05). Multivariate logistic regression identified regular treatment intervals (OR = 13.62, p < 0.001) and total number of sessions (OR = 3.80, p = 0.010) as independent predictors of good response, while Fitzpatrick Type IV skin was a negative predictor (OR = 0.12, p = 0.001). No severe adverse events occurred. The clear correlation between treatment regularity and efficacy supports the concept of maintenance photorejuvenation [28].

**IPL Combined with Non-Crosslinked Hyaluronic Acid (2026):** A study published in 2026 evaluated IPL combined with non-crosslinked hyaluronic acid (NCSH) containing tranexamic acid (TXA) and vitamin C (VC) mesotherapy for facial photoaging. The authors concluded that IPL combined with NCSH/TXA/VC mesotherapy provided safe, effective, and well-tolerated improvement in facial photoaging [29].

**755-nm Filter IPL for Facial Rejuvenation (2025):** A 2025 study evaluated the efficacy and safety of a 755-nm filter IPL system for facial rejuvenation in 61 Asian patients (Fitzpatrick skin types II–IV). Results showed immediate facial tightening and jawline contouring, with 63.93% reporting minimal pain (VAS ≤1). Minor adverse events included transient erythema (n=6) and acneiform eruptions (n=3) [30].

**Periorbital Skin Rejuvenation (2020):** An open-label study evaluated IPL for periorbital skin rejuvenation in 38 patients using a 572 nm wavelength. Six months after the final session, improvement was assessed: 9.1% excellent, 21.2% considerable, 27.3% moderate, 27.3% mild, and 15.1% little/no improvement. No significant difference was found across Fitzpatrick skin types (II–IV). Patient satisfaction: 75.7% would recommend IPL [31].

### 3.3 IPL for Age Spots / Solar Lentigines

**Systematic Review (Mardani et al., 2025):** A systematic review of 41 clinical trials (3,234 patients, ages 24–92) for solar lentigines found that among laser therapies, IPL showed success rates of 74.6%–90%, compared to pulsed dye laser (27%–57%), Q-switched laser (36.36%–76.6%), picosecond laser (67.9%–93.02%), and fractional CO₂ laser (8%–23%). The authors concluded that laser therapy appears more effective than other modalities, with an acceptable safety profile, and that combining lasers with specific topical agents may further improve outcomes and reduce PIH [32].

**Short-Wavelength IPL for Solar Lentigines (2017):** A study of 40 Japanese patients (Fitzpatrick skin types III–V) using a short-wavelength IPL device (500–635 nm) with contact cooling showed statistically significant improvement in all patients (P<0.0001), with mean gray level values rising from 143.7 to 162.9. Ninety percent of patients reported satisfaction [33].

**IPL with KTP-like Filter for Solar Lentigines (2019):** A clinical trial using IPL with a KTP-like filter (525–585 nm) for solar lentigines on the face and hands enrolled 16 healthy Caucasian subjects (mean age 55, skin types II–III). Significant pigmentation improvement was observed at all follow-up visits (P < 0.0001). At 1 month, good to excellent outcomes were seen in 74.6% of facial areas and 90% of hand areas. At 6 months, over 60% still showed good to excellent results [34].

### 3.4 IPL for Melasma

**Comprehensive Review (Indian Journal of Dermatology, 2025):** A review of IPL efficacy in melasma notes that IPL's broad spectrum (500–1200 nm) allows absorption by both hemoglobin and melanin, making it effective for both pigment and vascular components. Key findings: Ertam Sagduyu et al. assessed two or three IPL sessions in 50 patients with epidermal or mixed-type melasma, revealing an average improvement of 43% in MASI scores. Shakeeb et al. conducted a randomized study involving 96 patients with epidermal melasma and observed reductions in MASI of 69% in the Kligman formula group, 62.5% in the IPL group, and a substantial 93.8% in the IPL and Kligman combination group [35].

**AOPT-LTL Technique for Melasma (2026):** A clinical study on the Advanced Optimal Pulse Technology with low energy, three pulses, long pulse width (AOPT-LTL) technique for melasma in 25 Chinese women (Fitzpatrick skin types III–IV, mean age 41.5 years) involved 6–14 sessions at 2-week intervals using 590/640 nm filters. After the first course (6 treatments), 76% of patients had MDR >20% (effective rate). By the end of treatment, all patients had MDR >20% (100% effective rate), with significant reductions in MASI and CEA scores. No serious adverse reactions occurred; 92% of patients were satisfied or very satisfied [36].

**IPL + Topical Tranexamic Acid for Melasma (2026):** A prospective, randomized, placebo-controlled trial evaluated IPL combined with topical 3% TXA cataplasm versus IPL plus placebo for melasma in 33 Chinese patients. At month 5, the combination group showed a significantly greater reduction in mMASI score (−50.52%) compared to IPL alone (−38.49%), with a between-group difference of −12.03% (p<0.05). The melanin index decreased by 36.31% in the combination group versus only 1.28% in the placebo group (p=0.02), indicating that TXA prevented the early post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation seen with IPL alone [37].

**IPL vs. IPL + Microneedling-Assisted Tranexamic Acid for Melasma (2026):** A retrospective study (2023–2024, Xi'an Jiaotong University) evaluated IPL alone vs. IPL combined with microneedling-assisted TA for melasma in 29 patients. Both treatments significantly improved VISIA spots and red areas and lowered MASI scores (P<.05). Satisfaction was higher in the combination group (86.7% vs. 71.4% at 1 month; 86.7% vs. 64.3% at 3 months) and recurrence was lower (6.7% vs. 21.5%) [38].

### 3.5 IPL for Post-Inflammatory Hyperpigmentation (PIH)

**Retrospective Study (2022):** A retrospective study evaluated IPL therapy for acne-related PIE and PIH in 60 Chinese patients (Fitzpatrick skin types III–IV) using a BBL device with sequential cut-off filters (640 nm, 590 nm, 560 nm). Results: 81.7% of patients showed complete or partial clearance; CADI and EAS scores improved significantly (p<0.01). Adverse effects were limited to transient erythema, dryness, and itching. Acne relapse occurred in 11.7% of patients at 6-month follow-up [39].

**Clinical Experience Protocol (2024):** A clinical experience article published in the *Journal of Cosmetic Medicine* detailed a standardized IPL treatment protocol for PIH. Recommended treatment schedule: 7–10 sessions every 3–4 weeks. Typical parameters: cutoff filters 560–695 nm, energy 1–40 J/cm², pulse widths 3–100 ms, double/triple pulses. Three case presentations illustrated successful outcomes including a burn PIH case resolved after 12 IPL sessions, an IPL burn PIH case resolved after 10 sessions, and a nanosecond laser PIH case resolved after 22 sessions [40].

### 3.6 Comparison of IPL with Other Light-Based Therapies

**Picosecond Laser vs. IPL for Facial Rejuvenation (2025):** A prospective randomized controlled trial comparing a 1064 nm fractional picosecond laser with IPL for facial rejuvenation in 38 Asian women at Huashan Hospital, Fudan University, found that the 1064 nm picosecond laser showed statistically significant superiority over IPL in reducing periorbital fine lines (p = 0.0315) and T-zone pore counts (p = 0.0473). Both treatments significantly improved Global Score for Photoaging, pigmented spots, and skin lightness, with no significant intergroup differences [41].

**IPL-NAFL vs. PSAL-DLA for Facial Rejuvenation (2025):** A randomized controlled trial comparing IPL combined with nonablative fractional laser (IPL-NAFL) versus picosecond alexandrite laser with diffractive lens array (PSAL-DLA) for noninvasive facial rejuvenation in 17 Chinese women found that IPL-NAFL significantly improved pores compared to baseline (p<0.05), while PSAL-DLA significantly improved brown spots (p<0.01). PSAL-DLA caused less pain (p<0.01), shorter recovery time (p<0.001), lower incidence of PIH (23.5% vs. 58.8%, p<0.05), and higher patient satisfaction (p<0.01) [24].

### 3.7 Safety Profile of IPL

IPL is typically well tolerated with minimal and self-limiting side-effect profiles. Common side effects include pain, erythema, edema, and pigment changes. Darker skin types require careful parameter selection to avoid complications like paradoxical hypertrichosis. The absence of postoperative purpura is a major advantage, minimizing downtime substantially [25][28].

A case report published in *Cureus* (August 2025) described a male in his late 20s with Fitzpatrick skin type IV who sustained second-degree burns after IPL therapy for post-acne erythema, performed by a non-medical operator at a spa. The authors emphasize that IPL is riskier in darker skin types due to melanin competition, and that non-individualized settings, lack of physician oversight, and dismissal of pain contributed to the injury. They advocate for stricter regulation, better training, and patient-specific parameter adjustments, especially for Fitzpatrick skin types IV–VI [42].

## 4. LED Phototherapy

### 4.1 Mechanisms of Action

LED phototherapy operates through photobiomodulation (PBM), a non-thermal, non-ablative process. The primary mechanism involves absorption of photons by cytochrome c oxidase in mitochondria, increasing ATP synthesis, modulating ROS, and enhancing NO production. This triggers downstream signaling pathways regulating cellular metabolism, proliferation, and inflammatory responses. A biphasic dose response (Arndt-Schulz law) governs PBM: low doses produce therapeutic effects, while higher doses may be less effective [7][8][9].

For collagen synthesis, red (633 nm) and near-infrared (830 nm) LEDs stimulate fibroblast proliferation and collagen synthesis, increase COL1A1 mRNA expression and pro-collagen I α1 protein, downregulate MMP1 and MMP3, and upregulate TIMP-1 and TIMP-2 [43][44].

Blue light (400–500 nm) targets different chromophores: it is absorbed mainly by the heme group, activates heme/cytochrome groups, modulates ROS, and produces anti-inflammatory effects. Blue light (415 nm) targets porphyrins in *Cutibacterium acnes* for antibacterial action, producing singlet oxygen that destroys bacteria. Blue light also exerts anti-inflammatory effects through modulation of cytokines (e.g., IL-1β, TNF-α) and inhibition of NF-κB [45][46].

Yellow light (570–590 nm) fights hyperpigmentation by reducing tyrosinase production. It also modulates the expression of collagen and MMPs in skin. Specifically, 590 nm LED inhibits HMEC-1 migration, tube formation, and secretion of VEGF and SCF through inhibition of the AKT/PI3K/mTOR pathway, which may serve as a novel therapeutic option for melasma [47].

### 4.2 LED for Skin Rejuvenation and Anti-Aging

**Red LED Mask Study (2023):** A 2023 study evaluated a red LED mask (Skin Light Dior x Lucibel) delivering 630±10 nm at 15.6 J/cm² in 12-minute sessions, twice weekly for 3 months on 20 healthy volunteers (ages 45–70). Results showed: crow's feet wrinkle depth decreased by 38.3% at 84 days; facial oval sagging reduced by 24.8%; skin firmness (R0) improved by 23.6%; elasticity (R5) increased by 18.7%; dermal density rose 47.7%; roughness decreased 23.8%; complexion homogeneity improved 34.9%; pore diameter shrank 32.8%; sebum rate dropped 70.3%; and porphyrin-containing pores decreased 23.2% [48].

**Photobiomodulation for Periocular Wrinkles (Mota et al., 2023):** A randomized controlled trial with 137 women aged 40–65 showed a 31.6% reduction in periocular wrinkle volume with red (660 nm) PBM at 3.8 J/cm² over 10 sessions in 4 weeks. Amber (590 nm) PBM showed a 29.9% reduction in periocular wrinkle volume, comparable to red light [49].

**PBM Frequency Optimization (2025):** A randomized, sham-controlled, double-blind clinical trial investigated PBM application frequency for facial rejuvenation in 95 women aged 45–60 using a red LED mask (660±10 nm, 6.4 mW/cm², 8.05 J/cm², 21 min). ImageJ analysis showed significant reductions in glabellar and right periorbital wrinkle length. FACE-Q satisfaction rates were 79.6% (3x/week) and 73.4% (2x/week), both significantly higher than control. The number of sessions did not significantly affect outcomes, indicating that two weekly sessions are sufficient for patient satisfaction [50].

**Yellow Light Composite Treatment (2025):** A randomized controlled trial compared 570 nm vs 590 nm yellow light combined with red light (620 nm) and infrared LED (850 nm) for treating facial skin photoaging in 30 patients. The 570 nm group showed significant reductions in GSP scores, wrinkle scores, and brown spot scores from baseline (P<0.05). The 590 nm group showed significant reductions in GSP and wrinkle scores (P<0.05). The difference in wrinkle scores between groups post-treatment was statistically significant (P<0.05), favoring the 570 nm group [51].

**OLED Technology (2024):** Organic light-emitting diodes (OLEDs) represent a next-generation PBM modality. In vitro experiments on human dermal fibroblasts and keratinocytes demonstrated that OLED irradiation (625–630 nm, 6 J/cm²) significantly increased COL1A1 mRNA expression (12.5-fold vs. control) and pro-collagen I α1 production, outperforming traditional LEDs. OLED also reduced MMP1 and MMP3 expression more effectively than LED. Both OLED and LED enhanced wound healing (8.6% vs. 2.5% recovery) and upregulated growth factors (VEGFα, FGF2, FGF7). In an in vivo UV-irradiated mouse model, OLED treatment increased dermal collagen fiber density from 28.5% to 59.6% at 8 weeks and reduced skin roughness [52][53].

### 4.3 LED for Hyperpigmentation and Melasma

**Pulsed 940 nm LED for Melasma (Barolet, 2018):** A pilot study evaluated pulsed PBM at 940 nm for dermal melasma. Seven female patients with bilateral dermal melasma received a two-step approach: microdermabrasion followed by pulsed 940 nm LED (90 mW/cm², 13.5 J/cm², sequential pulsing with 50% duty cycle) weekly for eight weeks. The PBM-treated side showed statistically significant improvement: MASI score reduction from 11.4 to 4.7 (p<0.001), and a 25% reduction in melanin index (p<0.05). Three patients followed up to 12 months maintained improvement. The study suggests PBM may also provide photoprevention (UV resistance) via p53 signaling [54].

**Amber LED (590 nm) vs. Topical Tranexamic Acid for Melasma (2025):** A randomized controlled double-blind pilot trial enrolled 21 women. No significant differences in MASI or PGA were found within or between groups, but the study was underpowered (51% power) due to early termination. Both groups showed sustained improvements in quality of life [55].

**Combination Amber + Infrared for Melanin Reduction (2020):** An in vitro study (A-375 melanoma cells) showed that amber LED (590 nm) alone reduced melanin by 37.4%, infrared laser (850 nm) by 12.6%, and their combination (30 J/cm² total, 75/25% ratio) reduced melanin by 63.6%. In vivo, five female volunteers receiving once-weekly treatments for four weeks showed decreased age spots, improved skin elasticity, and a face-lifting effect [56].

**830 nm and 850 nm LED Inhibition of Melanin Synthesis (Kim et al., 2012):** LED irradiation at 830 nm (dose-dependent, from 1 to 20 J/cm²) and 850 nm (1 J/cm²) significantly reduced melanin production and tyrosinase activity in vitro [57].

**Photobiomodulation for Melasma: Integrative Review (Galache et al., 2024):** An integrative review concluded that clinical studies suggest photobiomodulation effectively reduces melasma-associated hyperpigmentation. PBM using fairly similar parameters may be able to produce opposite effects in the skin, either lightening it or darkening it depending on what is required for optimum cosmetic outcomes [58].

### 4.4 LED for Acne Vulgaris

**Combination Blue and Red LED (Goldberg & Russell, 2006):** Combination blue (415 nm, 48 J/cm²) and red (633 nm, 96 J/cm²) LED phototherapy for mild to severe acne vulgaris showed mean reduction in lesion count of 31% at week 2, 46% at week 4 (p<0.001), 69% at week 8, and 81% at week 12 (p<0.001). Severe acne showed slightly better response (82.5% reduction) [59].

**Wearable LED Mask (2023):** A 7-week open-label study evaluated a wearable LED mask (Omnilux Clear) emitting 415-nm blue and 633-nm red light for home treatment of mild-to-moderate acne. After 7 weeks (10 minutes, 4x/week), significant reductions in inflammatory (mean -13.07, p<0.0001) and noninflammatory (mean -14.73, p<0.0001) lesions were observed; 86% of patients achieved ≥1-grade IGA reduction [60].

**Meta-Analysis (Ngoc et al., 2023):** A meta-analysis showed red and blue LEDs significantly reduced inflammatory acne lesions (overall SMD = −2.42, I² = 17%). Blue LED (SMD = −2.64) was slightly more effective than red LED (SMD = −2.30) [61].

### 4.5 Safety Profile of LED Phototherapy

LED phototherapy is nonthermal, nonablative, and when used alone does not cause damage to the epidermis or dermal tissue. There are no severe adverse events associated with the use of these devices and little to no downtime for the patient. Mild and transient adverse effects include erythema, dryness, warmth, burning, and temporary irritation [7][62].

**Special Considerations for Hyperpigmentation:** People with darker skin tones (Fitzpatrick IV–VI) may be more sensitive to visible light, including red light, and this sensitivity can cause hyperpigmentation where the resulting dark spots are more intense and longer-lasting than those from sunlight. Blue light should be used cautiously in individuals with melasma or darker skin types, as it can induce hyperpigmentation through Opsin-3-mediated melanogenesis [45][63].

**Contraindications:** Photosensitivity disorders (e.g., lupus, porphyria), use of photosensitizing medications, active melasma (caution required), pregnancy (avoid direct abdomen), malignant tumors in the treatment area, active tuberculosis, and severe heart/kidney disease.

## 5. Comparison and Combination of Therapies

### 5.1 Comparative Efficacy

**Melasma Treatment:** For melasma, the evidence hierarchy varies by modality. Low-fluence 1064 nm picosecond laser (PSNYL) appears to offer the greatest MASI reduction, significantly better than 755 nm picosecond laser and 2% HQ [13]. IPL provides modest improvement (43–62.5% MASI reduction alone, up to 93.8% with combination therapy) [35]. LED PBM shows promise but evidence is limited, with 940 nm pulsed PBM showing 59% MASI reduction in a small pilot study [54]. Combination therapy (laser/light + topical agents) is superior to monotherapy, with a network meta-analysis showing that combination with TXA and 1064-nm picosecond laser likely offers the highest effective rate [11].

**Photoaging/Skin Rejuvenation:** For photoaging, both picosecond lasers and IPL show significant improvements in wrinkles, pigmentation, and texture. Fractional 1064 nm picosecond laser and fractional Q-switched 1064 nm Nd:YAG laser show comparable efficacy for photoaging, though picosecond causes more pain [20]. IPL is highly effective for diffuse dyschromia and vascular components of photoaging, with success rates of 74.6%–90% for solar lentigines [32]. LED PBM consistently improves skin texture, elasticity, and fine wrinkles, with red light showing 31.6% reduction in periocular wrinkle volume [49] and 38.3% reduction in crow's feet depth [48].

**Solar Lentigines/Age Spots:** Picosecond lasers show the highest success rates (67.9%–93.02% >75% clearance), followed by IPL (74.6%–90%), Q-switched lasers (36.36%–76.6%), and fractional CO₂ lasers (8%–23%) [32].

### 5.2 Combination Therapy Strategies

**Laser + Topical Agents:** Combining picosecond laser with topical TXA shows superior melasma improvement compared to laser alone [15]. Combining IPL with topical TXA prevents early PIH and enhances pigment reduction [37]. Combining IPL with Kligman formula achieves 93.8% MASI reduction in melasma [35].

**IPL + Laser:** Combining Q-switched laser with IPL for melasma shows significantly better MASI reduction (SMD = -1.20), higher clinical efficacy (92.0% vs. 77.4%), and lower recurrence (8.5% vs. 17.8%) compared to monotherapy [23].

**IPL + Nonablative Fractional Laser:** IPL-NAFL significantly improves pores compared to PSAL-DLA, but PSAL-DLA causes less pain, shorter recovery, lower PIH incidence, and higher patient satisfaction [24].

**LED + Microcurrent:** A porcine study showed that combining 630-nm and 850-nm LEDs with microcurrent significantly increased collagen deposition (2.70-fold at week 2) and elastin deposition (1.92-fold at week 6) compared to untreated controls. LED treatment primarily induced collagen, while microcurrent was more effective for elastin [64].

## 6. Safety Considerations Across Modalities

**Laser Therapy:** Picosecond lasers offer a favorable safety profile for melasma and pigmented lesions, with lower PIH risk compared to Q-switched lasers due to photoacoustic rather than primarily thermal mechanisms. However, PIH remains a concern in darker skin types, particularly with 755 nm and 532 nm wavelengths. Low-fluence 1064 nm Nd:YAG (laser toning) is the safest laser option for melasma in Fitzpatrick skin types IV–VI [6][13].

**IPL:** IPL is typically well tolerated with minimal side effects. The main risk is burns in darker skin types (Fitzpatrick IV–VI) due to melanin competition, particularly when aggressive parameters are used. The AOPT-LTL technique with low energy, three pulses, and long pulse width offers a safer approach for melasma in Asian skin [36].

**LED:** LED phototherapy has the best safety profile of all modalities, with no severe adverse events reported. The primary concern is the potential for visible light-induced hyperpigmentation in darker skin types, particularly with blue light. Therapeutic doses are much lower than ambient sunlight exposure, but caution is advised in patients with melasma [45][63].

**Combination Therapy:** A systematic review and meta-analysis (Fithria et al., 2026) found that combination therapy (laser/light + topical agents) had a significantly higher risk of adverse events (OR 8.96, 95% CI 3.71–21.64), mainly erythema and PIH, especially in darker skin types [65].

## 7. Future Directions and Emerging Technologies

**OLED Technology:** Organic light-emitting diodes represent a next-generation PBM modality with superior collagen stimulation (12.5-fold COL1A1 mRNA expression) compared to traditional LEDs. Wearable OLED patches for wound healing and skin rejuvenation are under development [52][53].

**Patch-Based PBM:** A 2025 review summarized advances in patch-based photobiomodulation therapy. A wearable PBM patch using a flexible red-wavelength OLED surface light source showed excellent in vitro wound healing effects by effectively stimulating fibroblast proliferation (over 58% of control) and enhancing fibroblast migration (over 46% of control) [66].

**Hydrocolloid-Based OLED Patch (2025):** A platform combining OLEDs and hydrocolloid dressing accelerates wound healing, absorbs ooze, provides a moist environment, and improves skin adhesion. In vitro cell proliferation enhancement of up to 160% at 5 mW/cm² was confirmed [67].

**Artificial Intelligence and Personalized Protocols:** The integration of AI for personalized treatment parameters and real-time skin analysis is an emerging trend. The Fan et al. (2026) study identified treatment regularity and total sessions as independent predictors of good response, highlighting the importance of personalized maintenance protocols [28].

---

## Conclusion

The evidence base for light-based therapies in aesthetic medicine has expanded significantly between 2020 and 2026. Picosecond lasers, particularly 1064 nm Nd:YAG, have emerged as the most effective option for melasma and pigmented lesions in darker skin types, offering superior safety profiles compared to traditional Q-switched lasers. IPL remains a versatile and effective modality for photoaging, solar lentigines, and melasma, with combination therapy (IPL + topical agents) showing enhanced outcomes. LED phototherapy offers the safest profile with consistent improvements in skin rejuvenation, though its role in hyperpigmentation treatment requires further research.

Key principles for clinical practice include:
1. **Individualization:** Treatment parameters must be tailored to skin type, condition, and patient goals.
2. **Combination Therapy:** Combining light-based therapies with topical agents (e.g., TXA, HQ) consistently outperforms monotherapy for melasma and photoaging.
3. **Safety First:** Darker skin types (Fitzpatrick IV–VI) require conservative parameters, longer intervals, and rigorous photoprotection to minimize PIH risk.
4. **Maintenance:** Regular, long-term treatment (e.g., IPL maintenance sessions 1–2 times per year) is essential for sustained results.
5. **Emerging Technologies:** OLED and patch-based PBM represent promising frontiers for enhanced efficacy and patient convenience.
