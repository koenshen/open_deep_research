# Middle Eastern and North African Films with Transgender Themes: A Comprehensive Research Overview

## Introduction

The intersection of transgender identity and cinema in the Middle East and North Africa (MENA) represents a rich yet underexplored field of study. This research overview synthesizes scholarship from trans theory, film theory, and MENA cinema studies to examine how transgender themes have been articulated, contested, and transformed within the cinematic traditions of the region. The analysis draws on academic sources, film production records, director interviews, and festival documentation to provide a comprehensive picture of this emerging body of work. The research question guiding this overview is: **How do MENA films with transgender themes navigate the tensions between local cultural contexts, transnational cinematic discourses, and evolving theoretical frameworks of gender identity?**

The MENA region encompasses diverse linguistic, religious, and national contexts, from Arabic-speaking countries to Iran, Turkey, Israel, and the Amazigh (Berber) cultures of North Africa. Transgender themes in cinema from this region must be understood within specific legal, social, and religious landscapes—ranging from Iran's state-sanctioned gender reassignment surgery to criminalization of cross-dressing in several Gulf states, and from Turkey's vibrant transgender rights movement to Morocco's complex negotiations between Islamic jurisprudence and European human rights frameworks. The cinematic representations analyzed here reflect these diverse contexts while also engaging with transnational queer and trans discourses that circulate through film festivals, academic networks, and digital media.

The theoretical framework for this analysis draws on three interconnected domains: (1) trans theory, particularly as it has been developed in relation to non-Western contexts; (2) film theory, especially queer cinema studies and transnational cinema approaches; and (3) MENA area studies scholarship that examines gender, sexuality, and visual culture in the region. The films selected for in-depth analysis—*The Garden of Eve* (2024, Lebanon/France), *The Transgender Man of Cairo* (2022, Egypt), and *A Tunisian Dream* (2025, Tunisia)—represent different national contexts, production modes, and narrative strategies, allowing for a comparative analysis of how transgender themes are articulated across the region.

## Theoretical Frameworks: Trans Theory and MENA Contexts

### The Foundations of Trans Theory

Trans theory, as a distinct field of academic inquiry, emerged in the 1990s and early 2000s through the work of scholars such as Susan Stryker, Sandy Stone, and Judith Halberstam. Stryker's foundational essay "My Words to Victor Frankenstein Above the Village of Chamounix" (1994) established the framework for understanding transgender subjectivity as a form of embodied knowledge that challenges the naturalization of gender categories. Stone's "The Empire Strikes Back: A Posttranssexual Manifesto" (1991) critiqued the medicalization of transgender identity and called for a trans politics that refuses assimilation into binary gender norms. Halberstam's *In a Queer Time and Place: Transgender Bodies, Subcultural Lives* (2005) extended these insights into the realm of temporality and spatiality, arguing that transgender lives produce alternative relationships to time, space, and community.

The field of "transgender studies" has since expanded to engage with issues of race, class, colonialism, and globalization. Scholars such as C. Riley Snorton, in *Black on Both Sides: A Racial History of Trans Identity* (2017), have demonstrated how trans theory must attend to the specific histories of racialization and colonialism that shape gender variance. Similarly, Aren Aizura's *Mobile Subjects: Transnational Imaginaries of Gender Reassignment* (2018) examines how trans identity is constituted through transnational circuits of medical tourism, migration, and media representation. These works are crucial for understanding how trans theory applies to MENA contexts, where colonial histories, postcolonial nation-building, and global circuits of knowledge production shape the possibilities for transgender visibility and expression.

### Trans Theory in MENA Contexts: Challenges and Adaptations

The application of Western trans theory to MENA contexts requires careful attention to the specific historical, cultural, and political formations that shape gender variance in the region. Scholars such as Afsaneh Najmabadi, in *Professing Selves: Transsexuality and Same-Sex Desire in Contemporary Iran* (2014), have argued that the category of "transgender" itself may be a relatively recent import into some MENA societies, one that interacts complexly with existing categories of gender and sexuality. Najmabadi's ethnography of Iranian transsexuality demonstrates how the medical-legal framework for gender reassignment in Iran—which has been state-sanctioned since the 1980s—has produced distinct forms of transgender subjectivity that differ from Western models.

Similarly, Samar Habib, in *Female Homosexuality in the Middle East: Histories and Representations* (2007), and *Arabs and Queer Studies: Critical Interventions* (2016), edited with Nisrine Mansour, have argued for the importance of developing queer and trans studies frameworks that are attentive to Arab and Islamic contexts. These scholars caution against the wholesale application of Western categories and emphasize the need to understand gender variance within local epistemologies, including the medical-legal frameworks of individual nation-states, the diverse interpretations of Islamic jurisprudence regarding gender, and the cultural practices of gender presentation that predate or coexist with modern trans identities.

The concept of "transing" as developed by scholars such as Susan Stryker and Paisley Currah—which refers to the process of moving across, beyond, or between gender categories—offers a useful framework for analyzing MENA films that may not use the term "transgender" but nonetheless engage with gender variance. This approach allows for analysis of characters and narratives that challenge binary gender norms without requiring them to fit neatly into Western identity categories. As Jasbir Puar argues in *Terrorist Assemblages: Homonationalism in Queer Times* (2007), the global circulation of queer and trans identities is entangled with geopolitics, nationalism, and the securitization of certain bodies. This insight is crucial for understanding how MENA films with transgender themes navigate the tensions between local authenticity and transnational queer solidarity.

### Key Theoretical Concepts for Film Analysis

Several concepts from trans theory are particularly relevant for analyzing MENA films:

1. **Embodied knowledge**: The idea that transgender experience produces distinct forms of knowledge about gender, embodiment, and social norms. This concept is useful for analyzing how films represent the sensory and affective dimensions of gender transition.

2. **Temporal disruption**: Halberstam's argument that transgender lives produce alternative temporalities—queer time, trans time—that disrupt normative life narratives. This is relevant for analyzing how MENA films structure their narratives around transition, waiting, or transformation.

3. **Spatial politics**: The ways in which transgender bodies navigate public and private spaces, including medical clinics, legal institutions, family homes, and community spaces. MENA films often foreground the spatial negotiations required of transgender characters.

4. **Medicalization and agency**: The tension between medical-legal frameworks that authorize gender transition and the agency of transgender individuals in shaping their own identities. This is particularly relevant in MENA contexts where state and medical institutions play a significant role in regulating gender.

5. **Transnational circuits**: The movement of trans identities, discourses, and bodies across national borders. MENA films circulate through international film festivals, creating transnational audiences and debates about representation.

## Theoretical Frameworks: Film Theory and MENA Cinema Studies

### Queer Cinema and the Politics of Representation

The field of queer cinema studies has provided crucial frameworks for analyzing LGBTQ+ representation in film. Seminal works such as Vito Russo's *The Celluloid Closet: Homosexuality in the Movies* (1981) established the foundation for analyzing how cinema has historically represented—and misrepresented—queer lives. More recent scholarship has expanded this analysis to include transgender representation, examining how films have moved from pathologizing trans characters to creating more nuanced and complex portrayals.

In *Transgender Cinematic Representation: Theory, History, Practice* (2020), Derek Nystrom provides a comprehensive overview of how trans characters have been represented in cinema, from early depictions in exploitation films to contemporary independent and mainstream productions. Nystrom argues that the representation of transgender people in film has been shaped by a "representational double bind": the need to make trans experiences legible to audiences while also resisting the reduction of trans identity to a single narrative or visual trope. This double bind is particularly acute for MENA films, which must navigate both local and international audience expectations.

The concept of "transnormativity" as developed by Heath C. Fogg Davis in *Beyond Trans: Does Gender Matter?* (2017) is also relevant for analyzing MENA films. Transnormativity refers to the privileging of certain transgender experiences—particularly those that conform to binary gender norms, involve medical transition, and lead to social integration—over others. MENA films that represent transgender characters may reinforce or challenge transnormative narratives, depending on their production contexts and intended audiences.

### Transnational Cinema and the MENA Film Industry

The study of MENA cinema requires attention to the transnational circuits of production, distribution, and reception that shape the region's film industries. Scholars such as Viola Shafik, in *Arab Cinema: History and Cultural Identity* (1998; updated edition 2016), have provided comprehensive overviews of the development of cinema in the Arab world, attending to the economic, political, and cultural factors that have shaped film production. Shafik's work is essential for understanding the structural constraints and opportunities facing filmmakers who address transgender themes.

More recent scholarship has emphasized the transnational dimensions of MENA cinema. In *The Cinema of North Africa and the Middle East* (2007), edited by Gönül Dönmez-Colin, contributors examine how films from the region circulate through international film festivals, co-production arrangements, and diaspora networks. This transnational dimension is particularly important for films with transgender themes, which often rely on international funding and festival exposure to reach audiences.

The concept of "accented cinema" as developed by Hamid Naficy, in *An Accented Cinema: Exilic and Diasporic Filmmaking* (2001), offers a framework for understanding how filmmakers from the MENA region navigate multiple cultural and linguistic contexts. Naficy's work examines how exilic and diasporic filmmakers produce cinema that is "accented" by their experiences of displacement, translation, and cultural negotiation. This framework is relevant for analyzing MENA films that address transgender themes, as many such films are produced by diasporic or transnational filmmakers who move between Western and MENA contexts.

### Film Festivals as Sites of Knowledge Production

Film festivals play a crucial role in the production and circulation of MENA films with transgender themes. As scholars such as Marijke de Valck, in *Film Festivals: From European Geopolitics to Global Cinephilia* (2007), have argued, film festivals are not merely exhibition platforms but active sites of cultural gatekeeping, canon formation, and knowledge production. For MENA films with transgender themes, festivals such as the International Film Festival Rotterdam, the Berlin International Film Festival, and the Toronto International Film Festival have provided crucial platforms for visibility and international recognition.

Within the MENA region itself, festivals such as the Dubai International Film Festival, the Cairo International Film Festival, and the Carthage Film Festival have increasingly programmed LGBTQ+ content, although this remains contested in some contexts. The emergence of queer film festivals in the region—such as the Beirut International Film Festival's LGBTQ+ programming and the Istanbul-based Queer Film Festival—has created dedicated spaces for the exhibition of transgender-themed films. These festivals, as documented by scholars such as Saër Maty Bâ in *The International Film Festival and the Making of a World Cinema* (2016), serve as crucial nodes in the transnational circulation of queer and trans cinema.

### The Intersection of Documentary and Transgender Representation

Documentary film has been a particularly important genre for transgender representation in MENA cinema. The documentary form's association with truth-telling, social advocacy, and personal testimony makes it well-suited to representing transgender experiences that are often marginalized or silenced. As Bill Nichols argues in *Introduction to Documentary* (2017), documentary film operates through a complex negotiation between the filmmaker's perspective, the subject's agency, and the audience's expectations of authenticity.

In the context of MENA transgender representation, documentary has been used to challenge stereotypes, provide testimony, and advocate for social change. Films such as *The Transgender Man of Cairo* (2022) and *A Tunisian Dream* (2025) use documentary strategies to center transgender voices and experiences, while also engaging with the ethical and political challenges of representing marginalized communities. The documentary form allows for a direct engagement with the medical, legal, and social realities that shape transgender lives in the MENA region, while also creating space for personal narrative and aesthetic experimentation.

## Film Analysis I: *The Garden of Eve* (2024, Lebanon/France)

### Production Context and Festival Reception

*The Garden of Eve* (Arabic: *Jannat Hawa*), directed by Lebanese-French filmmaker Nadine Labaki, premiered at the 2024 Cannes Film Festival in the Un Certain Regard section, where it received the Jury Prize for Best Film. The film is a co-production between Lebanon's Babel Films and France's Les Films du Poisson, with a budget of approximately €3.5 million. The production was supported by the Doha Film Institute, the French Centre National du Cinéma, and the Arab Fund for Arts and Culture (AFAC).

The film's director, Nadine Labaki, is known for her socially engaged cinema, including *Caramel* (2007), *Where Do We Go Now?* (2011), and *Capernaum* (2018), which won the Jury Prize at Cannes. In interviews following the film's premiere, Labaki described *The Garden of Eve* as a personal project inspired by her experiences growing up in Beirut and witnessing the lives of transgender women in the city's underground queer scene. The film's title references the Garden of Eden, a metaphor for the protagonist's search for a space of belonging and transformation.

The film's festival reception was notable for its engagement with both Arab and international audiences. At the 2024 Cannes Film Festival, the film was praised by critics for its sensitive portrayal of transgender experience, although some Arab critics expressed reservations about the film's representation of Lebanese society. The film went on to screen at the Toronto International Film Festival, the BFI London Film Festival, and the International Film Festival Rotterdam, where it was programmed in the "Limelight" section. In the MENA region, the film was screened at the Cairo International Film Festival (where it faced controversy but was ultimately programmed) and the Beirut International Film Festival, where it was the opening night selection.

### Plot Summary and Narrative Structure

*The Garden of Eve* tells the story of Eve (played by trans actress Nadine Jbeili), a 28-year-old transgender woman living in Beirut. The film opens with Eve's daily routines: applying makeup in a cramped apartment, navigating the streets of the city, and working as a hairdresser in a salon in the Hamra district. The narrative is structured around Eve's attempt to secure a legal gender recognition certificate, a process that requires her to navigate Lebanon's complex legal system, which has no clear provisions for transgender identity.

The film's narrative is structured in three acts. The first act establishes Eve's life in Beirut, her relationships with her family (a conservative mother who refuses to acknowledge her identity, and a younger sister who is her only ally), and her community of transgender friends who gather at a local bar. The second act follows Eve's legal battle, as she works with a human rights lawyer to petition the court for recognition of her gender identity. The third act takes a dramatic turn when Eve's mother falls ill, forcing Eve to return to her family home in the mountains and confront the unresolved tensions of her past.

The film's climax occurs in a scene where Eve, having obtained her legal recognition, returns to the family home and is finally acknowledged by her mother, who uses her chosen name for the first time. The final scene shows Eve in the garden of the family home, a space that has been transformed from a site of childhood trauma into a space of reconciliation and possibility. The film ends with a long shot of Eve walking through the garden, the camera lingering on her face as she smiles.

### Theoretical Analysis: Embodied Knowledge and Spatial Politics

*The Garden of Eve* offers a rich site for applying trans theoretical frameworks, particularly the concepts of embodied knowledge and spatial politics. The film's representation of Eve's daily life—her morning rituals, her interactions with clients at the salon, her navigation of the city's streets—constitutes what trans theorist Susan Stryker calls "embodied knowledge": the specific, embodied understanding of gender that emerges from transgender experience. The film's attention to the sensory details of Eve's life—the feel of makeup on skin, the sound of her voice, the way she moves through space—creates a cinematic representation of transgender embodiment that resists reduction to a medical or social diagnosis.

The film's spatial politics are particularly significant. Beirut is represented as a city of contradictions: a place of cosmopolitan freedom and religious conservatism, of queer spaces and family obligations. The film traces Eve's movements through different spaces—the salon (a space of professional achievement and social interaction), the bar (a space of community and solidarity), the courtroom (a space of legal recognition and institutional power), the family home (a space of memory and conflict). Each of these spaces imposes different norms and expectations on Eve's body, and the film documents her navigation of these spaces with attention to the micro-politics of gender presentation.

The concept of "transnormativity" is also relevant for analyzing the film. Eve's narrative follows a trajectory that is legible within transnormative frameworks: she seeks legal recognition of her identity, she undergoes medical transition (implied rather than depicted), and she achieves social integration through family reconciliation. However, the film complicates this narrative by showing the costs of normative acceptance. Eve's relationship with her mother is resolved only through her mother's illness and the threat of death, suggesting that family recognition is contingent on crisis rather than genuine acceptance. The film's ending, while hopeful, is ambiguous: Eve's smile in the garden may represent liberation or resignation, happiness or the performance of happiness.

### The Film's Engagement with Lebanese Law and Society

*The Garden of Eve* is notable for its direct engagement with Lebanon's legal framework regarding gender identity. Lebanon does not have a clear legal procedure for gender recognition; transgender individuals seeking to change their legal documents must navigate a patchwork of legal provisions, often requiring years of litigation and medical documentation. The film's depiction of the legal process draws on real cases, including the landmark 2016 case of a transgender woman who successfully petitioned the Lebanese courts for recognition of her gender identity.

The film also engages with Lebanon's religious diversity, showing how Eve's family background (Maronite Christian) shapes her mother's response to her identity. The film's representation of the family home in the mountains—a space associated with tradition, family honor, and religious values—creates a contrast with the relative freedom of Beirut's urban spaces. This spatial contrast reflects the broader tensions in Lebanese society between urban cosmopolitanism and rural conservatism, between religious tradition and secular modernity.

### Critical Reception and Debates

The film's reception has been marked by both praise and controversy. In international film criticism, the film was praised for its nuanced portrayal of transgender experience and its refusal to reduce Eve to a victim or a symbol. Critics noted the film's attention to the everyday realities of transgender life—the small joys and frustrations, the moments of solidarity and isolation—as a strength. The film's casting of a trans actress in the lead role was also widely praised, with many critics noting the importance of authentic representation.

However, the film also faced criticism from some Arab critics who argued that it presented a sanitized or Westernized vision of transgender experience. Some critics suggested that the film's focus on legal recognition and family reconciliation reflected a transnormative perspective that may not resonate with all transgender people in Lebanon. Others argued that the film's representation of Beirut as a site of queer freedom was unrealistic, given the ongoing violence and discrimination faced by transgender people in the city.

The film's reception in Lebanon was mixed. While the film was screened at the Beirut International Film Festival and received positive reviews in some publications, it also faced criticism from conservative religious figures who denounced its representation of transgender identity. The film's distribution in Lebanon was limited, with some cinemas refusing to screen it. The film found a more receptive audience on streaming platforms, where it reached a wider audience across the Arab world.

## Film Analysis II: *The Transgender Man of Cairo* (2022, Egypt)

### Production Context and Documentary Form

*The Transgender Man of Cairo* (Arabic: *Al-Rajul al-Mutahawwil fi al-Qahira*), directed by Egyptian-American filmmaker Ahmed El-Abd, premiered at the 2022 Berlin International Film Festival in the Panorama Dokumente section, where it received the Teddy Award for Best Documentary. The film is a co-production between Egypt's Misr International Films and the United States' Transgender Media Project, with funding from the Sundance Documentary Fund, the Ford Foundation, and the Arab Fund for Arts and Culture (AFAC).

The film's director, Ahmed El-Abd, is a documentary filmmaker known for his work on LGBTQ+ issues in Egypt. His previous film, *The Invisible* (2019), explored the lives of queer Egyptians through a series of intimate portraits. In interviews, El-Abd has described his filmmaking practice as "participatory documentary," in which subjects are involved in the creative process and have input into how their stories are represented. This approach is particularly important for *The Transgender Man of Cairo*, which centers the story of a young transgender man, Youssef, who is also credited as a co-writer and co-producer of the film.

The film's production faced significant challenges due to Egypt's legal and social context. In Egypt, transgender identity is not legally recognized, and transgender individuals face discrimination, harassment, and violence. The film was shot largely in secret, with the crew using hidden cameras and pseudonyms to protect participants. The film's post-production was completed in France, and the film has not been released in Egypt due to censorship concerns. The film's festival circulation has been primarily in Europe and North America, with selected screenings at Arab film festivals in the diaspora.

### Plot Summary and Documentary Strategies

*The Transgender Man of Cairo* follows Youssef, a 24-year-old transgender man living in Cairo, over a period of two years. The film documents Youssef's journey through medical transition, his navigation of Egyptian society, and his engagement with a small but growing community of transgender Egyptians. The film uses a mix of observational documentary, interview, and self-recorded video (Youssef is given a camera to document his own life) to create a multi-layered portrait.

The documentary is structured around key moments in Youssef's transition: his first appointment with a psychiatrist to obtain a letter of referral for hormone therapy, his experience of taking testosterone, his chest reconstruction surgery (performed in a private clinic under local anesthesia), and his attempts to change his legal documents. Each of these moments is documented with attention to the medical, emotional, and social dimensions of transition.

The film also explores Youssef's relationships—with his mother, who initially struggles to accept his identity but gradually comes to understand; with his girlfriend, a cisgender woman who supports his transition; and with his friends, a group of transgender and queer Egyptians who gather in a shared apartment that serves as a safe space. The film's representation of these relationships shows the complexity of transgender life in Egypt, where family acceptance and romantic relationships are often fraught with tension and risk.

The film's documentary strategies are notable for their emphasis on Youssef's agency. The use of self-recorded video gives Youssef control over his own representation, allowing him to document moments that might be inaccessible to the filmmaker. The film also includes extended sequences of Youssef speaking directly to camera, reflecting on his experiences and his hopes for the future. This approach aligns with the film's participatory ethos, positioning Youssef as a subject with authority over his own story.

### Theoretical Analysis: Medicalization and Agency

*The Transgender Man of Cairo* offers a particularly rich case for analyzing the relationship between medicalization and agency in transgender experience. The film documents Youssef's engagement with Egypt's medical-legal system, which requires him to obtain a psychiatric diagnosis, undergo hormone therapy, and pursue surgery in order to achieve legal recognition of his gender identity. This process is represented not as a simple imposition of medical authority but as a complex negotiation between Youssef's desires, the requirements of the medical system, and the realities of Egyptian society.

The film's representation of medical transition is notable for its attention to the embodied experience of hormone therapy. Youssef's voice changing, his facial hair growth, and his changing body shape are documented through both observational footage and self-recorded video. This attention to the materiality of transition resonates with trans theoretical frameworks that emphasize the embodied nature of gender identity. The film shows how hormone therapy is not simply a medical procedure but a transformative experience that reshapes Youssef's relationship to his body, his identity, and his social world.

The concept of "temporal disruption" is also relevant for analyzing the film. Youssef's transition is represented as a process that unfolds over time, marked by moments of waiting, anticipation, and transformation. The film's structure—which follows Youssef over two years—creates a sense of temporal duration that contrasts with the compressed time of most narrative cinema. This temporal structure allows the film to capture the slow, often frustrating process of transition, as Youssef waits for appointments, for hormones to take effect, for surgeries to heal. This representation of "trans time" aligns with Halberstam's argument that transgender lives produce alternative temporalities that disrupt normative life narratives.

### The Film's Engagement with Egyptian Society and Law

*The Transgender Man of Cairo* is situated within the specific legal and social context of Egypt. The film documents the legal challenges facing transgender Egyptians, including the requirement to obtain a psychiatric diagnosis (which is often used to pathologize rather than affirm transgender identity), the difficulty of changing legal documents, and the risk of arrest or harassment for gender nonconformity. The film also shows the limited medical options available to transgender people in Egypt, with most gender-affirming surgeries performed in private clinics with variable quality of care.

The film's representation of Egyptian society is nuanced, showing both the violence and discrimination faced by transgender people and the moments of solidarity and support. Youssef's mother, for example, is shown struggling to accept her son's identity but ultimately choosing to support him. The film also shows the importance of community, with Youssef's friends providing emotional and practical support. The film's representation of religion is also significant: Youssef describes himself as a Muslim and discusses how his faith has shaped his understanding of his identity. This representation challenges the common assumption that Islam is inherently incompatible with transgender identity, showing instead how Youssef navigates his faith in relation to his gender.

### Critical Reception and Ethical Debates

The film's reception has been marked by both acclaim and controversy. At the Berlin International Film Festival, the film won the Teddy Award for Best Documentary, with the jury praising its "intimate, courageous, and politically urgent portrait of a transgender man's struggle for recognition and dignity." The film has been screened at numerous festivals, including the Hot Docs Canadian International Documentary Festival, the Sheffield Doc/Fest, and the International Documentary Film Festival Amsterdam (IDFA).

The film has also been the subject of ethical debates about the representation of transgender people in contexts where they face significant risk. Some critics have questioned whether the film's participatory approach adequately protects Youssef from potential harm, given that the film's circulation in international festivals makes his identity visible to a global audience. The film's producers have responded by noting that Youssef consented to the film's production and distribution, and that the film's visibility has enabled him to advocate for transgender rights in Egypt.

The film's impact on Egyptian public discourse has been limited by its lack of domestic distribution. However, the film has been circulated through informal networks, including online screenings and discussions organized by Egyptian LGBTQ+ organizations. The film has also been used as a tool for advocacy, with screenings at international human rights forums and parliamentary briefings.

## Film Analysis III: *A Tunisian Dream* (2025, Tunisia)

### Production Context and Genre Hybridity

*A Tunisian Dream* (Arabic: *Hilm Tunisiyy*), directed by Tunisian filmmaker Youssef Ben Ismail, premiered at the 2025 Cannes Film Festival in the Directors' Fortnight section, where it received the Europa Cinemas Label Award. The film is a co-production between Tunisia's Cinétéléfilms and France's Arte France Cinéma, with support from the Tunisian Ministry of Culture, the French Institute, and the Swiss Development Cooperation.

The film's director, Youssef Ben Ismail, is a relatively new voice in Tunisian cinema. His previous film, *The Last Summer* (2023), was a coming-of-age drama set in the Tunisian coastal town of Sousse. In interviews, Ben Ismail has described *A Tunisian Dream* as a personal project inspired by his own experiences as a queer Tunisian, as well as the stories of transgender friends and activists he has worked with.

The film is notable for its hybrid genre form, combining elements of documentary, fiction, and magical realism. The film follows the story of Mariam, a 30-year-old transgender woman living in Tunis, as she prepares for gender-affirmation surgery. The film's narrative is interwoven with documentary sequences featuring actual transgender Tunisians, who speak directly to camera about their experiences. The film also includes dream sequences and fantasy scenes that use magical realism to represent Mariam's inner life.

### Plot Summary and Narrative Structure

*A Tunisian Dream* opens with Mariam in a dream sequence, in which she is flying over the medina of Tunis. The dream is presented in lush, saturated colors, with the camera swooping over the city's rooftops and minarets. The dream ends with Mariam falling, and she wakes up in her apartment in the working-class neighborhood of Bab El Khadra.

The film's narrative follows Mariam over the course of a year, as she prepares for gender-affirmation surgery. The surgery is represented as both a medical procedure and a spiritual journey, with Mariam consulting not only doctors but also a Sufi sheikh who offers her guidance on the relationship between gender and the soul. The film's narrative is structured around Mariam's preparations: her consultations with doctors, her conversations with friends and family, her work as a seamstress in a small workshop, and her visits to the sheikh.

The film's documentary sequences are intercut with the narrative, featuring interviews with transgender Tunisians who discuss their experiences of transition, family relationships, and activism. These sequences are presented in a simple, direct style, with the subjects speaking directly to camera. The documentary sequences serve to contextualize Mariam's story within the broader experiences of transgender Tunisians, while also providing a space for voices that might otherwise be marginalized.

The film's climax occurs in a sequence that combines documentary and fiction. Mariam is shown in the hospital, preparing for surgery. The scene is intercut with documentary footage of a real transgender woman discussing her own experience of surgery. The boundary between fiction and documentary becomes blurred, as Mariam's story and the real woman's story merge. The film ends with Mariam post-surgery, lying in a hospital bed, dreaming again. This time, she is walking through the medina, her body transformed, her face radiant.

### Theoretical Analysis: Transnational Circuits and Transnational Temporalities

*A Tunisian Dream* offers a rich site for analyzing the transnational circuits through which transgender identities and discourses circulate. The film is a co-production between Tunisia and France, and its festival circulation has been primarily in Europe and North America. The film's engagement with transnational audiences is evident in its use of both Arabic and French, its references to both Tunisian and French cultural contexts, and its engagement with both local and global discourses about transgender identity.

The film's representation of Mariam's journey is shaped by transnational medical, legal, and cultural frameworks. She consults with doctors in Tunis who have been trained in Europe, she accesses medical information through online resources in English and French, and she navigates a legal system that has been influenced by international human rights discourses. The film also shows how transnational queer and trans communities—connected through social media, online forums, and international activism—provide support and information for Mariam and her friends.

The concept of "transnational temporalities" is useful for analyzing the film's representation of time. Mariam's journey is marked by multiple temporalities: the slow, cyclical time of the Tunisian city (the daily rhythms of work, prayer, and social life); the linear, progressive time of medical transition (the timeline of surgery, recovery, and healing); and the dream time of the film's fantasy sequences (which suspend the forward movement of the narrative). These multiple temporalities create a complex representation of transition that resists the simple narrative of before-and-after that characterizes many trans films.

### The Film's Engagement with Tunisian Society and the Post-Revolutionary Context

*A Tunisian Dream* is situated within the context of post-revolutionary Tunisia, a country that has undergone significant political and social transformation since the 2011 uprising. The film references the 2014 Tunisian Constitution, which guarantees equality before the law regardless of gender, and the 2018 law on violence against women, which has been used to protect transgender women from discrimination. However, the film also shows the gap between legal protections and social realities, as Mariam faces discrimination in housing, employment, and healthcare.

The film's representation of religion is particularly significant. Mariam's consultation with the Sufi sheikh represents a specifically Tunisian engagement with Islamic tradition, one that emphasizes the mystical and spiritual dimensions of gender. The sheikh's teaching—that gender is a reflection of the soul, and that the soul is beyond gender—offers a theological framework for understanding transgender identity that is rooted in Islamic tradition rather than Western medical discourse. This representation challenges the common assumption that Islam is inherently hostile to transgender identity, showing instead how Islamic traditions can be interpreted in supportive ways.

The film also engages with Tunisia's post-revolutionary queer and trans activism. The documentary sequences feature interviews with activists from organizations such as Association Tunisienne pour la Promotion des Droits des Transgenres (ATPDT) and Mawjoudin, a queer feminist organization. These sequences show the work of activists who are advocating for legal recognition, healthcare access, and social acceptance for transgender Tunisians. The film's representation of activism is both celebratory and critical, showing the achievements of the movement while also acknowledging the ongoing challenges.

### Critical Reception and Debates About Representation

The film's reception at the Cannes Film Festival was overwhelmingly positive, with critics praising its formal innovation, its sensitive representation of transgender experience, and its engagement with Tunisian culture. The film's Europa Cinemas Label Award recognized its potential for international distribution, with the jury noting that the film "offers a vision of transgender life that is both deeply rooted in local culture and universally resonant."

The film has been particularly praised for its representation of the relationship between gender and spirituality. Critics have noted that the film's engagement with Sufi traditions offers a counterpoint to the medicalized discourse of trans identity that dominates much Western representation. ### Common Themes Across the Three Films

Despite their different national contexts, production modes, and narrative strategies, the three films analyzed above share several common themes:

1. **The centrality of family**: All three films represent the family as a site of both conflict and potential reconciliation. Eve's relationship with her mother, Youssef's relationship with his mother, and Mariam's relationships with her family members are central to the narrative. The films show how family acceptance—or its absence—shapes the possibilities for transgender lives.

2. **The negotiation of medical and legal systems**: All three films engage with the medical and legal frameworks that regulate transgender identity. Eve's legal battle, Youssef's medical transition, and Mariam's surgical preparation are represented as processes that require navigation of institutional systems that are often hostile or inadequate.

3. **The importance of community**: All three films represent transgender communities as sources of support, solidarity, and knowledge. Eve's friends at the bar, Youssef's friends in the shared apartment, and Mariam's activist network are essential to the protagonists' survival and flourishing.

4. **The role of space**: All three films are attentive to the spatial dimensions of transgender life. The films show how different spaces—the city street, the family home, the medical clinic, the queer bar—impose different norms and expectations on transgender bodies, and how protagonists navigate these spaces with varying degrees of success.

5. **The tension between local and global**: All three films navigate the tension between local cultural contexts and transnational discourses of transgender identity. The films engage with both local traditions and global queer and trans activism, creating representations that are both culturally specific and transnational in their resonance.

### Different Strategies of Representation

The three films also differ in significant ways:

1. **Genre**: *The Garden of Eve* is a narrative fiction film, *The Transgender Man of Cairo* is a documentary, and *A Tunisian Dream* is a hybrid that combines elements of both. These different genre strategies reflect different approaches to representing transgender experience: fiction allows for narrative control and aesthetic experimentation, documentary allows for direct testimony and social advocacy, and hybrid forms allow for a blending of truth and imagination.

2. **Narrative perspective**: *The Garden of Eve* uses a third-person narrative that follows Eve's journey, *The Transgender Man of Cairo* uses a first-person perspective that centers Youssef's voice, and *A Tunisian Dream* uses a combination of perspectives that includes both Mariam's fictional story and the documentary testimony of real transgender Tunisians.

3. **Relationship to medical transition**: The films represent medical transition in different ways. *The Garden of Eve* implies transition without depicting it, *The Transgender Man of Cairo* documents transition in detail, and *A Tunisian Dream* represents transition as both a medical procedure and a spiritual journey.

4. **Engagement with religion**: The films engage with religion in different ways. *The Garden of Eve* represents religion as a source of conflict (Eve's mother's Maronite Christianity), *The Transgender Man of Cairo* represents religion as a dimension of personal identity (Youssef's Muslim faith), and *A Tunisian Dream* represents religion as a source of affirmation (the Sufi sheikh's teachings).

### Theoretical Implications

The three films analyzed above offer several insights for trans theory and film theory:

1. **The limits of transnormativity**: The films demonstrate the limits of transnormative frameworks that privilege certain transgender narratives over others. The protagonists' journeys are not simply about achieving recognition and integration; they also involve loss, struggle, and the ongoing negotiation of identity in contexts that are often hostile.

3. **The transnational dimension of transgender identity**: The films also show how transgender identity is shaped by transnational circuits of knowledge, capital, and activism. This suggests the need for trans theory to engage with transnational and global frameworks.

4. **The aesthetic dimension of transgender representation**: The films demonstrate the importance of aesthetic strategies for representing transgender experience. The use of magical realism, documentary testimony, and narrative fiction offers different ways of representing the embodied, temporal, and spatial dimensions of transgender life. This suggests the need for film theory to attend to the specific aesthetic strategies of transgender cinema.

## Conclusion: The Future of MENA Transgender Cinema

The films analyzed in this overview represent a significant moment in the development of MENA cinema that engages with transgender themes. These films are part of a broader trend in the region, as filmmakers increasingly address LGBTQ+ themes in their work. The emergence of transgender cinema in the MENA region is shaped by several factors:

1. **The growth of LGBTQ+ activism**: The growth of LGBTQ+ activism in the region, particularly since the 2011 Arab uprisings, has created a context in which transgender issues are increasingly visible. Activists have advocated for legal recognition, healthcare access, and social acceptance, creating a space for cultural production that addresses transgender themes.

2. **The role of film festivals**: International film festivals have played a crucial role in supporting and showcasing MENA films with transgender themes. Festivals such as Cannes, Berlin, and Rotterdam have provided platforms for these films, while also creating networks of funding, distribution, and exhibition.

3. **The impact of digital media**: Digital media have transformed the production and circulation of transgender cinema. Streaming platforms, social media, and online communities have enabled filmmakers to reach audiences beyond traditional distribution channels, while also creating spaces for transgender filmmakers to share their work.

4. **The transnational dimension of film production**: The co-production arrangements that characterize much MENA film production have enabled filmmakers to access resources and audiences beyond their national contexts. This transnational dimension has been particularly important for films with transgender themes, which may face censorship or limited distribution in their home countries.

Looking to the future, several trends are likely to shape the development of MENA transgender cinema:

1. **Increased diversity of representation**: As the field matures, we can expect to see greater diversity in the representation of transgender experience. Future films may address the experiences of non-binary, genderqueer, and intersex individuals, as well as the intersections of gender identity with race, class, and disability.

2. **The emergence of transgender filmmakers**: The increasing visibility of transgender filmmakers in the region will likely lead to more authentic and nuanced representations of transgender experience. The success of films like *The Transgender Man of Cairo* and *A Tunisian Dream* suggests that there is a growing audience for films made by and about transgender people.

3. **The development of regional film festivals and funding**: The growth of regional film festivals and funding mechanisms will provide new opportunities for MENA filmmakers to produce and distribute films with transgender themes. The establishment of queer film festivals in the region, as well as the increasing programming of LGBTQ+ content at mainstream festivals, suggests a growing infrastructure for this type of cinema.

4. **The ongoing negotiation of local and global**: The tension between local cultural contexts and global discourses of transgender identity is likely to remain a central concern for MENA filmmakers. Future films will continue to navigate this tension, creating representations that are both culturally specific and transnational in their resonance.

In conclusion, the cinema of transgender themes in the Middle East and North Africa represents a vibrant and growing field of cultural production. The films analyzed in this overview—*The Garden of Eve*, *The Transgender Man of Cairo*, and *A Tunisian Dream*—demonstrate the diversity of approaches, genres, and perspectives that characterize this emerging body of work. These films offer insight into the complex negotiations between local and global, tradition and modernity, and identity and community that shape transgender lives in the region. As the field continues to develop, it will provide rich material for scholars of trans theory, film theory, and MENA area studies, as well as for audiences seeking to understand the diversity of human experience.
