# Therapeutic Interventions Modulating Plasma Metal Ion Concentrations for Cardiovascular Disease Prevention and Treatment: A Comprehensive Evidence Review

## Introduction

The relationship between metal ion homeostasis and cardiovascular health has emerged as a critical area of investigation. Both deficiency and excess of essential trace elements can disrupt cardiac function, vascular integrity, and metabolic pathways. This report synthesizes clinical evidence from randomized controlled trials, meta-analyses, and observational studies examining interventions that modulate plasma metal ion concentrations—including supplementation, chelation therapy, and dietary modifications—for the prevention and treatment of cardiovascular diseases.

## Magnesium

### Epidemiological Evidence and Mechanistic Basis

Dietary magnesium intake is inversely associated with cardiovascular disease risk across multiple outcomes. A comprehensive review indicates that higher magnesium intake is associated with a 22% reduction in stroke risk, a borderline 10% reduction in coronary heart disease risk, and a 31% reduction in heart failure risk when comparing highest versus lowest intake categories [1]. Circulating magnesium levels (per 0.2 mmol/L increment) are associated with 30% lower cardiovascular disease risk, with consistent trends for lower ischemic heart disease and fatal ischemic heart disease [2].

The mechanistic basis for magnesium's cardiovascular benefits is well-established. Magnesium acts as a physiological calcium channel blocker, stimulating prostacyclin and nitric oxide release to promote vasodilation. It inhibits NMDA receptors in the brainstem and N-type calcium channels at nerve endings, reducing catecholamine release. Magnesium deficiency activates the NLRP3 inflammasome, increases isolevuglandin production in dendritic cells, and promotes salt-sensitive hypertension. Additionally, magnesium depletion increases aldosterone secretion independently of renin, likely by enhancing sensitivity to angiotensin II [3].

Approximately 50% of adults in the United States have magnesium intakes below the Estimated Average Requirement (EAR), particularly due to low consumption of whole grains, green vegetables, and pulses. The US RDA is 310–320 mg/day for women and 410–420 mg/day for men, though newer metabolic unit data suggest that requirements increase with body weight [1].

### Blood Pressure Reduction

Multiple meta-analyses of randomized controlled trials have examined magnesium supplementation for blood pressure reduction. The most recent and comprehensive analysis, published in *Hypertension* in 2025, included 38 randomized controlled trials with 2,709 participants. The median magnesium dose was 365 mg/day over a median intervention period of 12 weeks. Overall, magnesium supplementation reduced systolic blood pressure by 2.81 mmHg and diastolic blood pressure by 2.05 mmHg compared to placebo [4].

Notably, greater reductions were observed in specific subgroups. Hypertensive individuals on medication experienced a systolic blood pressure reduction of 7.68 mmHg, while those with hypomagnesemia saw a reduction of 5.97 mmHg. Normotensive groups showed no significant effect. No dose-response relationship was found, and the authors cautioned that high heterogeneity across trials warrants careful interpretation [4].

A 2017 meta-analysis of 11 randomized controlled trials in individuals with insulin resistance, prediabetes, or noncommunicable chronic diseases found that magnesium supplementation (365–450 mg/day) significantly reduced systolic blood pressure by 4.18 mmHg and diastolic blood pressure by 2.27 mmHg [5]. A 2016 meta-analysis of 34 randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trials (2,028 participants) confirmed a significant reduction in systolic blood pressure of 2.00 mmHg and diastolic blood pressure of 1.78 mmHg, with dose-response analysis indicating that 300 mg/day or 1 month of supplementation is sufficient to elevate serum magnesium and lower blood pressure [6].

A randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled clinical trial in diabetic hypertensive adults with hypomagnesemia found that oral magnesium chloride (450 mg elemental magnesium daily for 4 months) produced striking reductions in systolic blood pressure (−20.4 mmHg vs. −4.7 mmHg, P=0.03) and diastolic blood pressure (−8.7 mmHg vs. −1.2 mmHg, P=0.02) compared to placebo, in addition to significant increases in HDL-cholesterol [7].

However, a 2006 Cochrane review of 12 randomized controlled trials (n=545) concluded that the evidence for a causal association between magnesium supplementation and blood pressure reduction was weak and likely due to bias, noting that most trials were of poor quality. The Cochrane review found that magnesium did not significantly reduce systolic blood pressure (mean difference -1.3 mmHg, 95% CI -4.0 to 1.5) but did produce a small statistically significant reduction in diastolic blood pressure (mean difference -2.2 mmHg, 95% CI -3.4 to -0.9) [8]. The discrepancy between older and newer meta-analyses likely reflects improvements in trial quality and the inclusion of larger, better-designed studies in recent analyses.

### Atrial Fibrillation Prevention After Cardiac Surgery

Postoperative atrial fibrillation (POAF) occurs in 20-40% of cardiac surgery patients and is linked to stroke, prolonged hospitalization, and higher mortality. Hypomagnesemia is common after cardiac surgery, making magnesium supplementation a logical prophylactic strategy.

A 2025 systematic review and meta-analysis of 24 randomized controlled trials with 3,373 participants found that magnesium may reduce the risk of POAF compared to control (RR 0.55, 95% CI 0.41–0.74; low certainty). In a subgroup analysis of trials with low risk of bias, magnesium showed a significant reduction (RR 0.70, 95% CI 0.58–0.84; high certainty). Magnesium had no significant effect on all-cause mortality or length of hospital stay [9].

A 2005 meta-analysis of 20 randomized controlled trials (2,490 patients) found that magnesium significantly reduced the incidence of postoperative atrial fibrillation from 28% in controls to 18% in the treatment group (OR 0.54, 95% CI 0.38–0.75). However, it did not significantly affect hospital length of stay or in-hospital mortality [10].

The POMPAE trial protocol, currently evaluating continuous intravenous magnesium sulfate in the perioperative period targeting serum concentrations of 1.5-2.0 mmol/L, represents the next generation of evidence. Early results suggest that this approach may not reduce POAF, highlighting the complexity of magnesium's role in this setting [11].

### Magnesium for Other Arrhythmias

Hypomagnesemia (serum Mg²⁺ <0.65 mmol/L) affects ion channels and action potential dynamics, leading to electrical instability. It promotes hypokalemia by impairing Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase and ROMK channels. Low magnesium increases the risk of atrial fibrillation by approximately 50% in long-term follow-up. For ventricular arrhythmias, magnesium is recommended for torsade de pointes (even with normal serum magnesium) and refractory ventricular fibrillation. However, large trials such as ISIS-4 and MAGIC failed to show a mortality benefit of magnesium in acute myocardial infarction, suggesting that magnesium's role may be limited to specific arrhythmia contexts rather than universal prophylaxis [12].

### Magnesium in Heart Failure and Prediabetes

A 2022 double-blind randomized controlled trial in prediabetic adults found that 12 weeks of magnesium supplementation (250 mg/day magnesium oxide) significantly increased HDL-cholesterol levels compared to placebo but did not improve other cardiometabolic markers including blood pressure, glucose, insulin, HbA1c, or inflammatory markers [13].

## Selenium

### Mechanistic Role and Deficiency States

Selenium is an essential micronutrient that functions through 25 selenoproteins, including glutathione peroxidases (GPx), thioredoxin reductases, and iodothyronine deiodinases. These proteins regulate antioxidant defense, inflammation, redox homeostasis, and thyroid metabolism. Selenium deficiency leads to decreased GPx activity, increasing susceptibility to oxidative stress and lipid peroxidation [14].

### Keshan Disease: The Definitive Selenium Deficiency Cardiomyopathy

Keshan disease is an endemic cardiomyopathy first identified in Keshan County, Heilongjiang Province, China. It is caused by a combination of dietary selenium deficiency and infection with a mutated strain of Coxsackievirus B3. The disease primarily affects children aged 2-10 years and women of childbearing age, characterized by myocardial necrosis, fibrosis, cardiac enlargement, and congestive heart failure. The average annual incidence historically stood at 10 per 100,000 population, with mortality rates exceeding 98% in some regions [15].

The causal role of selenium deficiency is supported by multiple lines of evidence: low soil selenium in endemic areas, lower selenium levels in affected individuals, and the effectiveness of selenium supplementation in reducing incidence. In a large trial providing selenium-fortified table salt to over one million people, the incidence of Keshan disease dropped dramatically from 25.23 to 2.7 per 100,000 individuals. The disease has four clinical types—acute, subacute, chronic, and latent—and treatment varies accordingly, with acute cases using high-dose ascorbic acid and coenzyme Q10, while chronic cases use standard heart failure medications [15].

Selenium deficiency is increasingly seen in clinical settings due to gastrointestinal operations, bariatric surgery, and long-term parenteral nutrition. A case report describes a 53-year-old woman with Crohn's disease, severe malnutrition, and bowel resection who presented with dilated cardiomyopathy (ejection fraction 25%). Treatment with parenteral selenium supplementation (100 µg/day for three weeks) resulted in recovery of ejection fraction to 40% at one-month follow-up, demonstrating that selenium-deficient cardiomyopathy can be reversible with supplementation [16].

### Selenium and Heart Failure

Suboptimal selenium levels (<100 µg/L) are prevalent in more than 70% of patients with heart failure and are associated with lower exercise capacity, lower quality of life, and worse prognosis. Analysis of 2,382 patients with worsening heart failure from the BIOSTAT-CHF cohort found that approximately 25% had serum selenium <70 µg/L (below clinical reference), and more than 50% had values <100 µg/L, both associated with poorer outcomes. Women and older patients were more affected [17].

Mechanistically, selenium deficiency disrupts mitochondrial electron transport, reducing ATP production and increasing reactive oxygen species. Selenoproteins modulate oxidative stress, mitochondrial function, thyroid metabolism, endoplasmic reticulum stress, inflammation, and immunity. The deficiency does not increase heart attack risk per se but makes the effects more severe by weakening antioxidant defense and reducing energy production in myocardial cells, leading to a 50% higher mortality rate [18].

### The KiSel-10 Trial: Combined Selenium and Coenzyme Q10

The KiSel-10 trial is the most prominent randomized controlled trial examining selenium supplementation for cardiovascular outcomes. This double-blind, placebo-controlled trial from Sweden enrolled 443 elderly participants (aged 70-88) with low baseline selenium levels (mean serum selenium 67.1 μg/L). Participants received 200 μg selenium (as selenized yeast) plus 200 mg coenzyme Q10 daily or placebo for 4 years, with median follow-up of 5.2 years [19].

Results showed a significant reduction in cardiovascular mortality in the active treatment group versus placebo (5.9% vs. 12.6%, P=0.015). NT-proBNP levels were significantly lower in the active group (mean 214 ng/L vs. 302 ng/L at 48 months, P=0.014), and echocardiography revealed significantly better cardiac function scores (P=0.03). A 10-year follow-up demonstrated persistent reduction in cardiovascular mortality risk (HR 0.51, 95% CI 0.36–0.74, P=0.0003), and a 12-year follow-up confirmed a 40% reduction in risk of dying from heart disease (28.1% vs. 38.7%, HR 0.59, 95% CI 0.42–0.81, P=0.001) [20].

A critical secondary analysis examined whether the effect depended on baseline selenium levels. Among participants with baseline selenium <65 μg/L, cardiovascular mortality was 24.1% in controls vs. 12.1% in the supplemented group (absolute risk reduction 12.0%, P=0.015). In the middle group (65–85 μg/L), mortality was 14.0% vs. 6.0% (absolute risk reduction 8.0%, P=0.040). In those with baseline selenium >85 μg/L, no significant difference was observed (17.5% vs. 15.0%, P=0.79). The authors concluded that supplementation reduces cardiovascular mortality only in those with low selenium status (≤85 μg/L), and no benefit was seen in those with adequate selenium levels [21].

### Meta-Analyses of Selenium Supplementation and CVD

A meta-analysis of 16 prospective observational studies and 16 randomized controlled trials showed a nonlinear relationship of cardiovascular disease risk with blood selenium concentrations across a range of 30–165 μg/L, with significant benefit observed within a narrow selenium range of 55–145 μg/L. Oral selenium supplements (median dose: 200 μg/day) significantly raised blood selenium concentrations by 56.4 μg/L but had no effect on cardiovascular disease (RR=0.91, 95% CI 0.74–1.10) [22].

A systematic review and meta-analysis of 43 randomized controlled trials found that selenium alone showed no association with cardiovascular disease or all-cause mortality. However, when selenium was part of an antioxidant mixture, a decreased risk was seen for cardiovascular disease mortality (RR 0.77, 95% CI 0.62–0.97, P=0.02) and all-cause mortality (RR 0.90, 95% CI 0.82–0.98, P=0.02). Conversely, when selenium was absent from antioxidant mixtures, an increased risk of all-cause mortality was observed (RR 1.09, 95% CI 1.04–1.13, P=0.0002) [23].

A Cochrane review published in 2013 concluded that selenium supplements do not prevent cardiovascular disease in well-nourished individuals and may slightly increase the risk of type 2 diabetes. No significant effects were observed for all-cause mortality (RR 0.97), cardiovascular disease mortality (RR 0.97), or nonfatal cardiovascular disease events (RR 0.96). The evidence was limited to populations with high selenium status (e.g., US soil), and the authors concluded that selenium supplements for cardiovascular disease prevention are not justified and should not be encouraged [24].

A 2023 umbrella review of 76 meta-analyses covering 96 health outcomes found that selenium intake is associated with reduced risks of all-cause mortality, digestive system cancers, depression, Keshan disease, and Kashin-Beck disease, while also improving sperm quality and outcomes in autoimmune thyroid disease. However, selenium intake was linked to a higher risk of type 2 diabetes and non-melanoma skin cancer. The vast majority of included studies were rated as low or very low quality using GRADE assessment, and the authors concluded that routine selenium supplementation is not recommended for the general population [25].

### Observational Studies on Selenium and CVD

A cross-sectional study of 39,372 US adults (NHANES 2003–2018) found that the middle tertile of dietary selenium intake was associated with a 16% lower cardiovascular disease risk (OR 0.84, 95% CI 0.74–0.96), while the highest tertile showed no significant benefit. Restricted cubic spline analysis revealed a nonlinear relationship: cardiovascular disease risk decreased with increasing selenium intake up to an inflection point of 135.28 µg/day, beyond which the protective effect weakened or reversed. Similar nonlinear patterns were observed for stroke (inflection point 100.3 µg/day) and atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease [26].

### Ongoing and Recent Trials

The SIRI-HF (Selenium Intervention Registry Randomized Trial in Heart Failure) is the largest non-pharmaceutical heart failure trial in Scandinavia, enrolling over 4,300 patients across 22 hospitals in Sweden and Norway. The study randomly assigns patients to receive selenium or placebo for three years, with an additional year of follow-up. Early, unpublished data show that 93% of older heart failure patients in a Swedish clinical registry had low selenium levels [27].

The SELEQT-HF (SELEnium and CoQ10 NuTrition for Heart Failure) study is a pragmatic, registry-based randomized controlled trial in the Netherlands evaluating whether adding selenium and coenzyme Q10 to standard heart failure therapy reduces cardiovascular events and deaths. The study will enroll 1,100 adults with chronic heart failure and elevated NT-proBNP levels [28].

The SUSTAIN CSX trial, a randomized controlled trial published in JAMA Surgery, investigated whether high-dose intravenous selenium supplementation (2000 µg/L perioperatively, then 1000 µg/L daily in ICU) reduces postoperative organ dysfunction and mortality in high-risk cardiac surgery patients. Among 1,416 patients, results showed no significant difference between groups: median days alive and free from organ dysfunction were similar, and 30-day mortality was 4.2% in the selenium group vs. 5.0% in placebo (OR 0.82, 95% CI 0.50-1.36, P=0.44) [29].

## Zinc

### Zinc Deficiency and Cardiovascular Risk

Zinc is an essential trace element critical for the function of hundreds of enzymes, including those involved in antioxidant defense, immune function, and cellular signaling. Zinc deficiency affects approximately 17% of the global population, rising to 35% in low-income populations in South Asia and Africa. Low serum zinc levels are associated with increased risk of cardiovascular disease onset [30].

Both zinc deficiency and zinc overload can accelerate atherosclerosis, with deficiency being the more clinically relevant concern. Zinc deficiency leads to increased oxidative stress, impaired blood vessel and immune function, altered lipid metabolism, and affected platelet function, all of which accelerate atherosclerosis development. Zinc supplementation may be a promising treatment for atherosclerosis, though larger clinical trials are needed [31].

### Zinc and Heart Failure

Zinc deficiency is common in heart failure patients due to multiple factors: impaired micronutrient consumption, hyper-inflammatory state, upregulation of the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone axis, diminished absorption, and hyperzincuria from heart failure medications such as thiazide diuretics. Long-term use (>3 months) of thiazide diuretics increases urinary zinc loss and may lower blood zinc levels [32].

Zinc homeostasis in the heart is crucial for cardiac function. ZIP13 dysfunction is associated with cardiac dysfunction, and zinc signaling pathways are related to the molecular mechanisms of cardiovascular functions. The tolerable upper intake level of zinc is 40 mg per day in adults [33].

### Zinc Supplementation and Blood Pressure

The evidence for zinc supplementation on blood pressure is less robust than for magnesium. Low-dose and long-duration zinc interventions appear to have beneficial effects of comparable or even greater magnitude than high-dose and short-duration interventions. Zinc supplementation has an atheroprotective effect and contributes to higher concentrations of HDL-cholesterol and apoproteins, and lower total cholesterol levels [34].

## Iron

### Iron Deficiency in Heart Failure: Prevalence and Impact

Iron deficiency is present in approximately 50% of patients with heart failure, and up to 80% in the post-acute heart failure setting. It is defined as serum ferritin <100 ng/mL or ferritin 100–299 ng/mL with transferrin saturation (TSAT) <20%. Iron deficiency in heart failure is associated with reduced exercise capacity, poor quality of life, and increased mortality, independent of the presence of anemia [35].

### Landmark Clinical Trials of Intravenous Iron in Heart Failure

**FAIR-HF Trial (2009):** This randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial published in the New England Journal of Medicine investigated intravenous ferric carboxymaltose (FCM) in 459 patients with chronic heart failure (NYHA class II or III), reduced left ventricular ejection fraction (≤40% for NYHA II, ≤45% for NYHA III), and iron deficiency. Patients were randomized 2:1 to receive 200 mg of intravenous FCM or saline placebo. At 24 weeks, 50% of patients receiving FCM reported being much or moderately improved compared to 28% of placebo patients (odds ratio for improvement, 2.51, 95% CI 1.75–3.61). Among those assigned to FCM, 47% had NYHA functional class I or II at week 24, compared to 30% of placebo patients (odds ratio for improvement by one class, 2.40, 95% CI 1.55–3.71). Benefits were seen in both anemic and non-anemic subgroups. The 6-minute walk test distance improved by a mean of 35 meters at 24 weeks [36].

**CONFIRM-HF Trial (2015):** This multicenter, randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled study evaluated intravenous FCM versus placebo in 304 symptomatic heart failure patients with LVEF ≤45% and iron deficiency over 52 weeks. The primary endpoint was change in 6-minute walk test distance from baseline to Week 24. FCM significantly improved 6MWT at Week 24 (33±11 m, P=0.002), with sustained benefit at Week 52 (36±11 m, P<0.001). Secondary endpoints including NYHA class, Patient Global Assessment, quality of life, and fatigue score improved from Week 24 onward. Importantly, FCM reduced the risk of hospitalization for worsening heart failure (HR 0.39, 95% CI 0.19-0.82, P=0.009) [37].

**AFFIRM-AHF Trial (2020):** This multicenter, double-blind, randomized, placebo-controlled study evaluated intravenous FCM in 1,132 patients hospitalized for acute heart failure with iron deficiency and LVEF <50%. The primary composite outcome was total heart failure hospitalizations and cardiovascular death up to 52 weeks. The primary outcome occurred in 52.5% of the FCM group compared with 67.6% of the placebo group (rate ratio 0.79, 95% CI 0.62–1.01, P=0.059), which did not reach statistical significance. However, total heart failure hospitalizations were significantly reduced: 217 vs. 294 events (RR 0.74, 95% CI 0.58-0.94, P=0.013). Cardiovascular death did not differ between groups (13.8% vs. 14.2%, P=0.809). A COVID-19 sensitivity analysis showed a significant reduction for the primary endpoint (RR 0.75, 95% CI 0.59-0.96, P=0.024). Improved health-related quality of life was observed with FCM [38].

**IRONMAN Trial (2022):** This investigator-initiated, event-driven, open-label, randomized trial compared intravenous ferric derisomaltose (FDI) versus usual care in 1,137 patients with heart failure, reduced ejection fraction (LVEF ≤45%), and iron deficiency (TSAT <20% or ferritin <100 µg/L). Patients were recruited from 70 UK sites and followed for a median of 2.7 years. The primary composite endpoint was recurrent heart failure hospitalizations and cardiovascular death, which occurred at a rate of 22.4 per 100 patient-years in the FDI group vs. 27.5 per 100 patient-years in the usual care group (rate ratio 0.82, 95% CI 0.66–1.02, P=0.070), which did not reach statistical significance. A prespecified COVID-19 sensitivity analysis showed a significant reduction (RR 0.76, 95% CI 0.58–1.00, P=0.047). Fewer cardiac serious adverse events occurred with FDI (36% vs. 43%, P=0.016). A detailed adjudication analysis found that FDI reduced all-cause unplanned hospitalizations by 17% (rate ratio 0.83, P=0.02), with similar reductions in cardiovascular and non-cardiovascular hospitalizations [39].

**HEART-FID Trial (2023):** This double-blind, randomized, placebo-controlled trial evaluated intravenous FCM in 3,065 ambulatory patients with heart failure, reduced ejection fraction (≤40%), and iron deficiency. The primary outcome was a hierarchical composite of death within 12 months, hospitalizations for heart failure within 12 months, or change in 6-minute walk distance from baseline to 6 months. Death by month 12 occurred in 8.6% vs 10.3% (FCM vs placebo). Total heart failure hospitalizations were 297 vs 332. The overall unmatched win ratio was 1.10 (99% CI, 0.99 to 1.23; P=0.02), not reaching the prespecified significance level of 0.01. The authors concluded that among ambulatory patients with heart failure with reduced ejection fraction and iron deficiency, there was no apparent difference between FCM and placebo with respect to the hierarchical composite endpoint [40].

**FAIR-HF2 Trial (2025):** This multicenter randomized clinical trial evaluated intravenous FCM in 1,105 patients with heart failure (LVEF ≤45%) and iron deficiency across 70 sites in 6 European countries, with median follow-up of 16.6 months. The three primary endpoints were: (1) time to cardiovascular death or first heart failure hospitalization (HR 0.79, 95% CI 0.63-0.99, P=0.04); (2) total heart failure hospitalizations (rate ratio 0.80, P=0.12); and (3) time to cardiovascular death or first heart failure hospitalization in patients with TSAT <20% (HR 0.79, P=0.07). Using a Hochberg procedure to adjust for multiple primary outcomes, none of the endpoints met the prespecified significance thresholds. However, patients reported significant improvements in quality of life. The benefit was most pronounced in the first year, when higher doses were given [41].

### Meta-Analyses of Intravenous Iron in Heart Failure

A 2021 meta-analysis of seven randomized trials (2,166 patients) found that intravenous iron significantly reduced the composite outcome of first heart failure hospitalization or cardiovascular death (OR 0.73, 95% CI 0.59–0.90, P=0.003), driven predominantly by a reduction in heart failure hospitalization (OR 0.67, 95% CI 0.54–0.85, P=0.0007). The effect on cardiovascular mortality alone was not statistically significant (OR 0.89, 95% CI 0.66–1.21, P=0.47) [42].

A 2023 meta-analysis of 14 randomized controlled trials (6,624 patients) found that intravenous iron significantly reduced cardiovascular death (OR 0.867, 95% CI 0.755-0.995, P=0.0427), combined cardiovascular death and heart failure admission (OR 0.838, 95% CI 0.751-0.936, P=0.0015), first heart failure admission (OR 0.855, 95% CI 0.744-0.983, P=0.0281), and total heart failure admissions (rate ratio 0.739, 95% CI 0.661-0.827, P<0.0001). Heterogeneity was partly explained by baseline TSAT: trials with mean TSAT ≤20% showed greater benefit. The authors concluded that intravenous iron reduces both cardiovascular death and heart failure-related events in a broad heart failure population, and that lower baseline TSAT may be important for the effect on heart failure admissions [43].

A Bayesian meta-analysis of six trials (7,175 patients) conducted as part of the FAIR-HF2 analysis showed a 19% reduction in recurrent heart failure hospitalizations or cardiovascular death (sensitivity HR 0.812, P=0.035) and a 28% reduction at 12 months [44].

### Guideline Recommendations

The 2023 ESC focused update of the heart failure guidelines provides the following recommendations for intravenous iron:
- **Class I, Level A**: Intravenous iron supplementation is recommended in symptomatic patients with HFrEF and HFmrEF, and iron deficiency, to alleviate HF symptoms and improve quality of life.
- **Class IIa, Level A**: Intravenous iron supplementation with ferric carboxymaltose or ferric derisomaltose should be considered in symptomatic patients with HFrEF and HFmrEF, and iron deficiency, to reduce the risk of HF hospitalization, irrespective of hospitalisation history [45].

The 2022 AHA/ACC/HFSA guideline provides a **Class IIa, Level B-R** recommendation that in patients with HFrEF and iron deficiency with or without anemia, intravenous iron replacement is reasonable to improve functional status and quality of life. Oral iron is not recommended as it is ineffective in heart failure patients due to poor absorption and side effects [46].

### Iron Chelation Therapy for Myocardial Iron Overload

Iron overload is the primary cause of mortality and morbidity in thalassemia major. Iron overload-induced cardiomyopathy is the leading cause of death in patients with transfusion-dependent thalassemia. Three FDA-approved iron chelators are available:

**Deferoxamine (DFO):** A parenteral iron chelator administered subcutaneously via pump for 8–12 hours daily (35-50 mg/kg on 3-7 days/week). It has been the historical standard for long-term iron chelation therapy.

**Deferiprone (DFP):** An oral iron chelator taken three times daily (75 mg/kg t.i.d.). It is described as the best option for improving cardiac function. However, its use is restricted due to risks of neutropenia and agranulocytosis, affecting approximately 10% of patients.

**Deferasirox (DFX):** An oral agent taken once daily (20-30 mg/kg). Approved by the FDA in 2005 and EMA in 2006, it is the most widely used chelator, with about 80% of patients taking it globally [47].

A longitudinal study of 98 patients with beta thalassemia major treated with deferasirox over a mean of 6.9 years found that cardiac magnetic resonance T2* significantly increased overall, with greater improvement in patients with baseline cardiac iron overload (T2* <20 ms). Among 13 patients with left ventricular ejection fraction below 56% at baseline, 85% normalized their cardiac function (LVEF increased from 50.6% to 60.2%). No cardiac deaths or incident heart failure occurred during 12 years of follow-up [48].

Intensive combined chelation with deferoxamine plus deferiprone has shown superiority to deferoxamine monotherapy for established cardiac disease. In a phase 4 interventional trial of 28 patients with beta-thalassemia major and iron-induced cardiomyopathy, those on combination therapy had zero cardiac events over 42±6 months, whereas the monotherapy group experienced four cardiac events including three deaths (P=0.0049) [49].

Combined oral chelation with deferasirox plus deferiprone has emerged as a safe and feasible option for patients with limited response to monotherapy. A systematic review found that in patients with severe iron overload, the combined regimen showed significant reductions in serum ferritin and liver iron concentration, and significant increases in cardiac T2* values, with high compliance rates and a well-tolerable safety profile [50].

### Hemochromatosis and Cardiac Iron Overload

Hereditary hemochromatosis is the most common autosomal recessive genetic disorder, primarily affecting those of northern European descent (prevalence 1 in 300-500). It is caused by HFE gene mutations (C282Y and H63D) leading to hepcidin deficiency and excessive iron absorption. Iron accumulates in the liver, pancreas, heart, joints, skin, and endocrine glands, causing cirrhosis, hepatocellular carcinoma, diabetes, cardiomyopathy, hypogonadism, and arthropathy. Approximately 75% of patients are asymptomatic at diagnosis [51].

Treatment is primarily phlebotomy (50-100 sessions then maintenance 3-4 times/year) to achieve ferritin <50 µg/L. Iron chelation (deferoxamine, deferiprone, deferasirox) is used for cases with anemia, heart disease, or poor venous access. Early diagnosis and treatment normalize life expectancy, while cirrhosis and diabetes significantly reduce survival. The most important prognostic factor is the presence or absence of hepatic fibrosis or cirrhosis at diagnosis [51].

## Copper

### Copper Deficiency and Cardiovascular Disease

Copper is an essential trace metal that regulates mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation, iron mobilization, connective tissue crosslinking, antioxidant defense, and vascular integrity. The recommended daily allowance for copper is 0.9 mg/day, while the recommended optimal intake is 2.6 mg/day. NHANES III data revealed that more than 80% of 103,655 people studied in the US received less than the RDA from their diet [52].

Copper deficiency in animals produces almost every risk factor for ischemic heart disease, including hypercholesterolemia, glucose intolerance, hypertension, and increased oxidative stress. It increases susceptibility of LDL to oxidation, raises HMG-CoA reductase activity, and promotes glycation and inflammation. Human studies show that leukocyte copper levels (a better marker than plasma copper) correlate inversely with coronary artery disease severity [53].

A cross-sectional study of 14,876 US adults (NHANES 2011-2018) found a significant negative correlation between dietary copper intake and myocardial infarction risk. Compared to the lowest copper intake quartile (median 0.65 mg/day), the highest quartile (median 1.79 mg/day) had an odds ratio of 0.69 (95% CI 0.53–0.88) after full adjustment. The relationship was non-linear (L-shaped), with protective effects plateauing at higher intakes. Subgroup analyses showed stronger protective associations in participants aged ≥50 years, females, overweight individuals, smokers, hypertensives, and diabetics [54].

### Copper Chelation Therapy: The TACT and TACT2 Trials

**TACT Trial (2013):** The Trial to Assess Chelation Therapy was a randomized, blinded, parallel factorial study evaluating disodium EDTA chelation therapy versus placebo in 1,708 post-myocardial infarction patients. Patients received 40 infusions of either a 10-component EDTA-based chelation mixture or saline placebo. The primary composite endpoint (death, MI, stroke, coronary revascularization, hospitalization for angina) at 5 years was significantly lower with chelation vs. placebo (26.5% vs. 30%, HR 0.82, 95% CI 0.69–0.99, P=0.035), driven mainly by a reduction in coronary revascularizations. Diabetic patients showed greater benefit (HR 0.61, 95% CI 0.45-0.83, P=0.002). However, hypocalcemia was more common with chelation (6.2% vs. 3.5%, P=0.008) [55].

**TACT2 Trial (2024):** This trial aimed to replicate the TACT finding in a more targeted population of 959 participants aged ≥50 years with diabetes and a history of MI. The double-masked, placebo-controlled trial compared 40 weekly EDTA chelation infusions versus placebo. Median follow-up was 48 months. The primary composite endpoint occurred in 35.6% of the chelation group and 35.7% of the placebo group (adjusted HR 0.93, 95% CI 0.76–1.16, P=0.53). Although chelation significantly reduced median blood lead levels from 9.03 μg/L to 3.46 μg/L (P<0.001), the treatment did not reduce cardiovascular events. The authors concluded that EDTA chelation is ineffective for secondary prevention in stable patients with coronary artery disease, diabetes, and prior MI. The discrepancy between TACT and TACT2 is likely due to lower contemporary blood lead levels and widespread use of modern cardioprotective medications [56].

### Copper Dyshomeostasis, Cuproptosis, and Cardiovascular Disease

Copper dyshomeostasis—both excess and deficiency—can lead to pathological changes in the cardiovascular system. Elevated serum copper is linked to atherosclerosis and ischemic stroke risk, while copper deficiency also impairs cardiac function. The newly identified cell death mechanism cuproptosis involves copper ions binding to lipoylated mitochondrial proteins (e.g., DLAT), causing aggregation and Fe-S cluster loss, leading to energy collapse. This process contributes to myocardial injury in various cardiovascular conditions [57].

Therapeutic strategies under investigation include copper chelators (triethylenetetramine, tetrathiomolybdate, EDTA) for conditions of copper excess, and copper ion carriers (elesclomol, disulfiram) for deficiency. However, these approaches remain largely in preclinical stages and require further investigation [58].

## Calcium

### Calcium Supplementation and Cardiovascular Disease Risk: The Controversy

The evidence regarding calcium supplementation and cardiovascular disease risk is among the most contentious in nutritional research, with meta-analyses yielding conflicting results.

**Meta-analyses showing increased risk:**

A 2021 meta-analysis of 13 double-blind, placebo-controlled RCTs (n=28,935 intervention, 14,243 control; 92.8% postmenopausal women, mean age 66.3 years) found that calcium supplements significantly increased the risk of cardiovascular disease by 15% (RR 1.15, 95% CI 1.06-1.25, I²=0.0%) and coronary heart disease by 16% (RR 1.16, 95% CI 1.05-1.28, I²=0.0%) in healthy postmenopausal women. The absolute risk difference for cardiovascular disease was 8.6 per 1000 persons and for coronary heart disease was 8.8 per 1000 persons. Subgroup analysis indicated that both dietary calcium intake of 700–1000 mg/day and supplementary calcium of 1000 mg/day elevated cardiovascular disease and coronary heart disease risk. The authors concluded that "supplementary calcium intakes should be discouraged" [59].

The Bolland et al. 2010 meta-analysis showed that calcium supplements (without coadministered vitamin D) were associated with an increased risk of myocardial infarction. In the meta-analysis of trial-level data, 296 people had a myocardial infarction (166 allocated to calcium, 130 to placebo). Bolland 2011 found that in women not taking personal calcium supplements at randomization in the WHI, calcium plus vitamin D increased the risk of cardiovascular events by 13–22% [60].

**Meta-analyses showing no significant increase in risk:**

A 2023 systematic review and meta-analysis of 12 RCTs found no significant association between calcium supplementation (alone or with vitamin D) and myocardial infarction, total stroke, heart failure admission, or all-cause/cardiovascular mortality. Subgroup analyses did not alter the findings [61].

A 2023 meta-analysis of 11 randomized placebo-controlled trials (7 trials of calcium alone, n=8,634; 6 trials of calcium plus vitamin D, n=46,804) found no significant associations for calcium alone or calcium plus vitamin D with myocardial infarction, coronary heart disease death, any coronary heart disease, stroke, or all-cause mortality. The authors concluded that "calcium supplements were not associated with any significant hazard for CHD, stroke, or all-cause mortality and excluded excess risks above 0.3%–0.5% per year for CHD or stroke" [62].

A 2016 systematic review and meta-analysis by Chung et al. (commissioned by the National Osteoporosis Foundation) analyzed 4 randomized trials and 27 observational studies. The trials showed no statistically significant differences in cardiovascular disease events or mortality between calcium supplementation groups and placebo. Cohort studies revealed no consistent dose–response relationships between calcium intake and cardiovascular mortality or stroke. The authors concluded that "calcium intake within tolerable upper intake levels (2000 to 2500 mg/d) is not associated with CVD risk in generally healthy adults" [63].

### The Women's Health Initiative Calcium/Vitamin D Trial

The Women's Health Initiative (WHI) CaD trial randomly assigned 36,282 postmenopausal women to 1,000 mg calcium plus 400 IU vitamin D3 daily or placebo for seven years. The original publication (Hsia et al., 2007, Circulation) found that calcium plus vitamin D neither increased nor decreased the risk of cardiovascular events. During 7 years of follow-up, myocardial infarction or coronary heart disease death was not significantly different between groups [64].

### Potential Mechanisms and Clinical Recommendations

The proposed mechanism for potential harm from calcium supplements involves acute increases in serum calcium levels that may promote vascular calcification and plaque buildup. This effect is not observed with dietary calcium, which is absorbed more slowly and accompanied by other nutrients that may modulate its effects.

The National Osteoporosis Foundation and American Society for Preventive Cardiology 2016 clinical practice guideline concluded that dietary and supplemental calcium are safe for the heart as long as total intake does not exceed 2,000–2,500 mg/day. The expert panel advised: "Discontinuation of supplemental calcium for safety reasons is not necessary and may be harmful to bone health" [65].

The JACC Focus Seminar (2020) summarized that some observational and RCT data suggest that calcium supplementation (especially at >1,400 mg/day) may increase risk of cardiovascular disease events, while dietary calcium intake does not. The authors concluded that "calcium supplementation should be used cautiously, striving for recommended intake of calcium predominantly from food sources" [66].

## Conclusion

The evidence for therapeutic interventions modulating plasma metal ion concentrations in cardiovascular disease varies considerably by metal and clinical context. Intravenous iron supplementation for iron-deficient heart failure patients represents the most robustly supported intervention, with consistent benefits across multiple large randomized trials and meta-analyses for improving symptoms, quality of life, and reducing heart failure hospitalizations. Current guidelines provide Class I recommendations for this approach.

Magnesium supplementation shows moderate evidence for blood pressure reduction, particularly in hypertensive and hypomagnesemic individuals, and for preventing postoperative atrial fibrillation after cardiac surgery. However, the evidence quality is limited by heterogeneity across trials.

Selenium supplementation demonstrates clear benefit in deficiency states such as Keshan disease, and the KiSel-10 trial suggests benefit in elderly populations with low baseline selenium when combined with coenzyme Q10. However, meta-analyses of randomized trials in well-nourished populations do not support routine supplementation and suggest potential harm at high doses.

Copper and zinc show promising mechanistic links to cardiovascular disease, but clinical trial evidence is insufficient to recommend routine supplementation. The TACT2 trial effectively closed the door on EDTA chelation therapy for cardiovascular disease prevention in modern populations.

Calcium supplementation remains controversial, with conflicting meta-analyses and no clear resolution. The weight of evidence suggests that calcium from dietary sources is safe, while high-dose supplements may carry modest cardiovascular risk that must be balanced against bone health benefits.

The emerging concepts of cuproptosis and ferroptosis represent new frontiers in understanding metal ion dysregulation in cardiovascular disease, potentially opening avenues for novel therapeutic interventions. However, these remain largely in preclinical stages of investigation.
