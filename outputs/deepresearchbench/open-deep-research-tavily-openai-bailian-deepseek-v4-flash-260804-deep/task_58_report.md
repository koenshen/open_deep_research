# Horizontal Gene Transfer in Plants and Animals: Frequency, Mechanisms, and Adaptive Significance

## Introduction

Horizontal gene transfer (HGT)—the interspecific transmission of genetic material across reproductive barriers—has long been recognized as a dominant evolutionary force in prokaryotes, but its significance in non-microbial eukaryotes has only recently come into focus. Historically considered rare in plants and animals, accumulating evidence over the past two decades reveals that HGT is far more common than previously appreciated, though still much less frequent than in bacteria. The key question is not whether HGT occurs in eukaryotes, but how often, by what mechanisms, and—critically—what adaptive benefits these transferred genes confer.

This report synthesizes peer-reviewed primary literature to examine the occurrence and evolutionary significance of HGT in plants and animals, covering both well-documented cases and recent discoveries up to 2026. The findings reveal a striking pattern: while HGT events are individually rare, those that persist and become fixed in populations tend to provide significant adaptive advantages, often enabling evolutionary innovations that would be difficult to achieve through mutation alone.

## The Frequency of HGT in Non-Microbial Eukaryotes: A Complex Picture

### Why HGT Is Rarer in Eukaryotes Than Prokaryotes

The theoretical framework for understanding eukaryotic HGT has been substantially advanced by Keeling's 2024 review in *Nature Reviews Genetics*, which addresses the apparent paradox of why HGT is not more common in eukaryotes despite features that should increase exposure to foreign DNA [1][2][3]. The author argues that while phagocytosis, endosymbiosis, and cellular complexity increase opportunities for DNA acquisition, this is counterbalanced by several factors: the sequestration of germline cells in most animals, the complexity of eukaryotic genomes with their introns and chromatin structure, and fundamentally, the nature of selection in eukaryotes. As Keeling states, "aligning theory with data suggests that HGT is less pervasive in eukaryotes due to evolutionary constraints, not merely a lack of opportunity" [1][3].

The "weak-link model" proposed by Huang provides a complementary framework, arguing that unicellular eukaryotes and early developmental stages (zygotes, embryos, spores) of multicellular eukaryotes are vulnerable entry points for foreign DNA, concluding that HGT is likely underestimated due to phylogenetic signal loss and methodological biases [6].

### Quantitative Estimates of HGT Frequency

Despite the constraints, the accumulated evidence demonstrates that HGT is a significant evolutionary force in both plants and animals:

**In plants**, a 2021 review in *Life* states that "HGTs are very common in nature and concern all living organisms including plants," with over 600 plant-to-plant and 800 bacterial-to-plant cases documented [1]. A study of 17 grass species found that 13 of them carry laterally transferred genes, with an estimated rate of 3.72 such genes per 10,000 [15][18]. In *Alloteropsis semialata*, researchers found that it acquired foreign genes approximately every 35,000 years throughout its evolutionary history [17]. The most extreme case is the mitochondrial genome of *Amborella trichopoda*, which has sustained "proportionately more HGT than any other eukaryotic (or even prokaryotic) genome examined to date," acquiring entire mitochondrial genomes from algae and moss [1][13].

**In animals**, the frequency varies dramatically by lineage. The bdelloid rotifers stand as the most extreme animal example, with approximately 10% of their genes horizontally acquired from non-metazoan sources [3][4]. A systematic study of 218 insect genomes published in *Cell* (2022) identified 1,410 genes acquired via 741 distinct transfers, with 88% of species having at least one HGT-acquired gene [26][29][30]. In vertebrates, a 2025 study of 242 ray-finned fish species identified 18–19 teleost-to-teleost HGT events involving 17 different genes [38][40][54]. For transposable elements specifically, a 2025 study across 247 animal genomes identified 6,043 independent horizontal transfer events [36].

The euHGT dataset compiled by Spaulding and Fierst (2025) catalogs 9,511 protein-coding HGT candidates from published literature, with the top seven phyla being Rotifera, Arthropoda, Nematoda, Chordata, Porifera, Echinodermata, and Mollusca [4][41].

### The Importance of Methodological Rigor

A cautionary perspective from *Frontiers in Plant Science* (2026) argues that many reported interkingdom HGT cases in plants may be overestimated due to methodological limitations, including the "last-one-out" scenario (differential gene loss mimicking HGT) and incomplete representation of eukaryotic homologs in public databases. The authors reanalyzed 322 iHGT candidates and found only 25.5% remained consistent with iHGT when updated homology searches were performed [3]. This underscores the importance of rigorous criteria, including phylogenetic incongruence, high sequence similarity between distantly related species, irregular taxonomic distribution, and functional validation.

## Plant-to-Plant HGT: From Grasses to Parasitic Plants

### The Grass Family as a HGT Hotspot

Grasses (Poaceae) have emerged as one of the best-documented groups for plant-to-plant HGT. A landmark PNAS study (2019) by Dunning et al. used a chromosome-level genome assembly of *Alloteropsis semialata* and genomic data from 146 other grass species, discovering 59 laterally acquired genes clustered in 23 fragments up to 170 kb long [16]. These fragments originated from at least nine different donor species that diverged from *Alloteropsis* 20–40 million years ago. The majority of these genes are expressed and "have added functions to the recipient genome" [16].

Dr. Luke Dunning stated: "Grasses are simply stealing genes and taking an evolutionary shortcut. They are acting as a sponge, absorbing useful genetic information from their neighbours to out compete their relatives and survive in hostile habitats without putting in the millions of years it usually takes to evolve these adaptations" [22].

A 2021 study in *New Phytologist* by Hibdige et al. scanned 17 diverse grass species and found LGTs in 13 species, with significant variation in the amount received. Key findings include that rhizomatous species acquired statistically more genes, "probably because this growth habit boosts opportunities for transfer into the germline," and that the amount of LGT increases with phylogenetic relatedness [18]. The exact mechanism of grass-to-grass HGT remains unknown, though wind pollination and rhizomes have been suggested as possible facilitators [15][18].

### C4 Photosynthesis Gene Transfer

A 2012 study in *Current Biology* on the grass subtribe Neurachninae showed that two C4 taxa exchanged the gene encoding the C4 form of phosphoenolpyruvate carboxylase (*ppc-B2*) through lateral gene transfer [78][91]. This demonstrates that "plant-plant lateral nuclear gene transfers can be a potent source of genetic novelty and adaptation in flowering plants" [91].

### Parasitic Plants: The Most Dramatic Case of Functional HGT

Parasitic plants have emerged as hotspots for HGT due to the intimate vascular connections they form with their hosts through haustoria.

**Dodder (*Cuscuta* spp.)**: A study published July 22, 2019 in *Nature Plants* revealed that dodder has stolen over 100 functional genes from its hosts. Researchers identified 108 functional genes, 18 of which are present in all dodder species, suggesting early acquisition in the ancestral dodder [29][33]. The stolen genes contribute to haustoria structure, defense responses, amino acid metabolism, and—remarkably—one produces microRNA that silences the host's own defense genes, facilitating parasitism [34]. Claude dePamphilis stated: "We present the most dramatic case known of functional horizontal gene transfer ever found in complex organisms" [29].

**Rafflesia cantleyi**: A 2012 study in *BMC Genomics* identified 49 actively transcribed HGTs (2.1% of observed transcripts) in this holoparasite, with many retaining introns and expressed at levels comparable to vertically inherited genes [30]. *Rafflesia arnoldii* has lost its entire chloroplast genome and nearly half of its conserved genes, with its genome described as "loaded with borrowed genes from hosts and composed of 90% repeated elements" [11][30].

**Striga hermonthica (Witchweed)**: A 2010 *Science* article provided the first empirical evidence of HGT from a crop to a parasitic plant, showing that *Striga* contains a nuclear gene that clusters with sorghum genes [32][36].

**Orobanchaceae family**: A systematic 2016 PNAS study found that the number of HGT events increases with the degree of parasitic dependence. The nonparasitic *Lindenbergia* had only one potential HGT, while the most parasitic species (*Phelipanche*) had the most. Transferred genes showed strong purifying selection and were preferentially expressed in the haustorium [35].

### Multi-Step HGT: The Pytheas Sequence

A 2025 study in *Scientific Reports* described a chain of HGT involving a DNA sequence named *Pytheas* that originated from the plastid genome of *Cuscuta*, moved to its mitochondrion, then horizontally transferred to a host plant in the Fabaceae, and finally to the holoparasitic plant *Orobanche rigens*. This is the first documented multi-step transfer linking three plant species via a host-mediated pathway [55].

### Mitochondrial HGT in Flowering Plants

A 2003 *Nature* study demonstrated that "standard mitochondrial genes, encoding ribosomal and respiratory proteins, are subject to evolutionarily frequent horizontal transfer between distantly related flowering plants" [5]. The *Amborella trichopoda* mitochondrial genome is the most extreme example: at 3.9 megabases, it contains "six genome equivalents of their average size in flowering plants" and has acquired entire mitochondrial genomes from algae and moss [13][5].

## Animal-to-Animal and Within-Animal HGT

### Bdelloid Rotifers: The Champions of Animal HGT

Bdelloid rotifers represent the most extreme case of HGT in the animal kingdom. The landmark 2008 *Science* study by Gladyshev, Meselson, and Arkhipova reported that these asexual animals contain "many genes that appear to have originated in bacteria, fungi, and plants, concentrated in telomeric regions" [1][2]. Subsequent studies have confirmed that approximately 10% of bdelloid rotifer genes are horizontally acquired from non-metazoan sources [3][4].

**Freezing tolerance through bacterial ice-binding proteins**: A 2025 study by James A. Raymond in *PNAS* showed that Antarctic bdelloid rotifers possess multiple copies of DUF3494-type ice-binding protein (IBP) genes horizontally acquired from bacteria. The rotifers survived 24 years of storage at -25°C, reviving within 45 minutes—a record for controlled laboratory studies [7].

**Antifungal defense through bacterial antibiotic genes**: A 2024 study in *Nature Communications* by Nowell et al. found that when bdelloid rotifers are challenged with the fungal pathogen *Rotiferophthora globospora*, horizontally acquired genes were over twice as likely to be upregulated as other genes. The most markedly overrepresented were clusters resembling bacterial polyketide and nonribosomal peptide synthetases (PKS/NRPS) that produce antibiotics. Upregulation of these clusters in a pathogen-resistant rotifer species was nearly ten times stronger than in a susceptible species [4]. Dr. Chris Wilson notes that "these complex genes—some of which aren't found in any other animals—were acquired from bacteria but have undergone evolution in rotifers" [3].

**Cellulolytic enzymes**: A 2015 study by Szydlowski et al. reported "multiple horizontally acquired genes from fungal and prokaryotic donors encode cellulolytic enzymes in the bdelloid rotifer *Adineta ricciae*" [9].

The mechanism linking desiccation tolerance to HGT is particularly compelling: when desiccated, rotifer chromosomes fracture, and upon rehydration, DNA repair mechanisms stitch the genome back together while inadvertently incorporating foreign DNA fragments [6]. The estimated rate of gain is 12.8 genes per million years, versus 2.0 losses, and the proportion of foreign genes is significantly higher in species from desiccating habitats [5].

### Antifreeze Protein Gene Transfer: Herring to Smelt

The most well-documented case of animal-to-animal HGT of a functional gene is the transfer of an antifreeze protein (AFP) gene from Atlantic herring (*Clupea harengus*) to rainbow smelt (*Osmerus mordax*). The evidence is considered "conclusive" by the researchers [17]. The AFP gene in smelt is over 95% identical to the herring gene at the intron level, despite the two species having diverged over 250 million years ago [14][15][16]. The smelt's gene carries three transposable elements unique to herring, acting as a "tag" indicating its origin [17]. The transfer is estimated to have occurred about 20 million years ago, with the proposed mechanism being sperm-mediated gene transfer during spawning [17]. The transferred AFP gene provides rainbow smelt with the ability to survive in icy Arctic waters [16][17].

### Horizontal Transfer of Transposable Elements (HTT) in Animals

HTT is now recognized as a major force shaping animal genome evolution. A 2018 review by Gilbert and Feschotte argues that "horizontal inheritance has had a more profound impact in eukaryotic evolution than is commonly appreciated" [22].

**Massive scales**: A 2020 study in *Nature Communications* screened 307 vertebrate genomes and inferred a minimum of 975 independent HTT events between lineages that diverged more than 120 million years ago, with 93.7% involving ray-finned fishes [23]. A 2017 study in *PNAS* analyzing 195 insect genomes detected 2,248 HTT events [24]. A 2025 study across 247 animal genomes identified 6,043 independent HTT events, with over 85% involving DNA transposons [26].

**The BovB retrotransposon**: BovB has been extensively horizontally transferred between diverse animal species, contributing to more than 1% of anole, opossum, platypus, and wallaby genomes [27][28].

**The SPIN (SPACE INVADERS) transposons**: A 2008 study in *PNAS* identified a family of DNA transposons that are nearly identical (≈96% pairwise nucleotide identity) across species including murine rodents, bushbaby, little brown bat, tenrec, opossum, anole lizard, and African clawed frog—despite their hosts diverging up to 360 million years ago [29].

**Bats as a hotspot**: A 2023 study in *Molecular Biology and Evolution* reported that "bats have repeatedly been shown to experience horizontal transfer of TEs at what appears to be a high rate compared with other mammals" [34].

**Mavericks as vectors**: A 2026 seminar at Institut Curie reported that Mavericks—ancient virus-like transposons related to giant viruses—are "one of the long-sought vectors of horizontal gene transfer" in animals, particularly in nematodes [38].

### Immune Gene Transfers in Fish

A 2025 study by Policarpo et al. in *Molecular Biology and Evolution* identified 18–19 teleost-to-teleost HGT events involving 17 different genes across 11 teleost fish orders. Most transferred genes are involved in immunity (e.g., lectins, lysozymes, phospholipases, SAMD9, B4GALNT2) or are pore-forming proteins (natterins, stonustoxins). The study notes: "This clear enrichment for immune genes suggests that they are preferentially retained after HGT, and thus more often beneficial to the host than other genes, enhancing and/or diversifying the species defense repertoire" [38]. The proposed mechanism involves external fertilization, where sperm may absorb foreign DNA [38][39][57].

## Cross-Kingdom HGT: Major Transfers Between Kingdoms

### Bacteria-to-Plant HGT

**Agrobacterium T-DNA transfer**: The most well-studied example of natural HGT from bacteria to plants is the transfer of T-DNA from *Agrobacterium* species to plant genomes. *Agrobacterium tumefaciens* and *A. rhizogenes* are regarded as "nature's most effective plant genetic engineer" [26][28]. Specific documented cases include:

- **Nicotiana glauca**: Contains cellular T-DNA (cT-DNA) in the form of an imperfect inverted repeat containing homologs of oncogenes and an opine synthase gene [19][20]
- **Linaria vulgaris**: Contains cT-DNA as a tandem imperfect direct repeat [19][22]
- **Sweet potato (*Ipomoea batatas*)**: The only known domesticated crop that carries naturally occurring, heritable *Agrobacterium* T-DNAs, which are expressed and inherited. The maintenance of cT-DNA in these genomes suggests "a possible selective advantage, such as increased root mass for drought tolerance" [65][62]

A 2025 study by Shaposhnikov and Matveeva identified 93 new species of naturally transgenic plants, with transfers occurring between 0.62 and 24 million years ago [60].

**Arabidopsis-microbiome HGT**: A 2024 study in *ISME Communications* from The Hebrew University of Jerusalem identified 75 unique full-length genes horizontally transferred between *Arabidopsis thaliana* and its microbiome. Of these, 59 were transferred from bacteria to plants, and 16 from plants to bacteria. Genes exchanged in both directions are enriched in carbohydrate metabolism functions, while bacteria-to-plant transfers include auxin biosynthesis genes. As functional proof, expressing a bacterial *DET2* homologue from *Leifsonia* in *Arabidopsis det2* mutants rescued the dwarf phenotype, demonstrating that bacterial genes can functionally replace plant homologues [2][8].

**Origin of land plants**: A study in *New Phytologist* (Feb. 2024) found that charophyte algae acquired genes from fungi and bacteria via HGT at least 600 million years ago, with genes involved in carbohydrate digestion later repurposed for remodeling plant cell walls and defending against pathogens [54].

### Bacteria-to-Animal HGT

**Wolbachia-to-insect gene transfers**: *Wolbachia* is the most documented source of bacteria-to-animal HGT. Key cases include:

- **Adzuki bean beetle**: The first documented case of HGT from a prokaryotic endosymbiont to a multicellular eukaryotic host, with a *Wolbachia* genome fragment integrated into the X chromosome [41]
- **Drosophila ananassae**: An entire ~1.4 Mbp *Wolbachia* genome integrated into chromosome 2L [37][58]
- **Mosquito Aedes aegypti**: Two adjacent genes with ~50% amino acid identity to *Wolbachia* genes, transcribed at levels comparable to functional genes [38]

A systematic 2007 bioinformatic approach detected LGT from *Wolbachia* in ~33% of sequenced arthropod genomes [43].

**Insects broadly**: A systematic study of 218 insect genomes found that insects acquired 1,410 genes from microbes via 741 distinct transfers. These genes are involved in mating behavior, nutrition, growth, and environmental adaptation. The most prevalent foreign gene, LOC105383139, originated from the bacterium *Listeria* and has persisted for over 300 million years. In diamondback moths, males lacking this gene courted females significantly less and produced fewer viable eggs [45][83].

**Leaf beetles**: A 2024 PNAS study identified two bacterial-origin glycosyl hydrolase 48 family genes (GH48-1 and GH48-2) in the leaf beetle *Plagiodera versicolora*. RNAi knockdown of GH48 genes significantly reduced larval survival, growth, and pupation rates when feeding on mature leaves, demonstrating that HGT-derived cellulases are essential for exploiting a less competitive food resource [80].

### Fungus-to-Plant HGT

**Fhb7 gene**: The *Fhb7* gene, cloned from *Thinopyrum elongatum*, confers broad resistance to Fusarium head blight and crown rot in wheat. *Fhb7* encodes a glutathione S-transferase that detoxifies trichothecene mycotoxins. Evidence supports that *Th. elongatum* gained *Fhb7* through HGT from an endophytic *Epichloë* fungus. Introgressing *Fhb7* into wheat provides resistance without yield penalty [59].

**Neochrome in ferns**: A 2014 PNAS study demonstrated that ferns acquired the chimeric photoreceptor gene neochrome from hornworts via HGT approximately 179 million years ago. Fern neochromes are nested within hornwort neochromes in gene trees, strongly supporting HGT. The acquisition of neochrome is proposed to have provided a phototropic advantage for ferns under low-light angiosperm canopies, facilitating their modern diversification [41][48]. Kathleen Pryer noted that the unprotected gametophyte stage provides perfect conditions for gene sharing: "A gametophyte is also a really compact structure, and the sex organs are right there, with lots of contact with other plants that are all competing for light and space" [48][49].

**Shikimate pathway**: The phenylalanine ammonia lyase (PAL) gene from fungi was transferred to an ancestral land plant via arbuscular mycorrhizal symbiosis, enabling flavonoid and lignin synthesis and land plant colonization [3].

### Fungus-to-Animal HGT

**Carotenoid biosynthesis in aphids**: This is one of the most well-documented cases of HGT from fungi to animals. The pea aphid (*Acyrthosiphon pisum*) produces carotenoid pigments using enzymes encoded in its own genome, with phylogenetic analyses demonstrating that the carotenoid biosynthesis genes originated from a lateral gene transfer from fungi [8][15][16][17]. A 2025 study in *Insects* demonstrated that under cold stress, high light intensity activates carotenoid-mediated light-driven ATP synthesis, increasing ATP content by 240% and significantly enhancing population fitness under cold conditions [3].

**Spider mites**: The two-spotted spider mite possesses horizontally transferred fungal carotenoid biosynthetic genes. Mutations in the phytoene desaturase gene cause complete albinism and, critically, failure to enter diapause, demonstrating that endogenous carotenoid synthesis is essential for this adaptation [5].

**Gall midges**: These are the third arthropod group known to possess fungal-derived carotenoid biosynthesis genes, with phylogenetic analyses suggesting independent HGT events from related fungal donors [12].

### Plant-to-Insect HGT

**Whitefly (*Bemisia tabaci*) and BtPMaT1**: This is the first documented case of functional horizontal gene transfer from a plant to an insect (Xia et al., 2021, *Cell*). The whitefly acquired the plant gene *BtPMaT1* between 35 and 80 million years ago, likely via a virus. *BtPMaT1* encodes a phenolic glucoside malonyltransferase that detoxifies phenolic glycosides, which are major plant defensive compounds. Remarkably, when researchers used RNA interference to silence *BtPMaT1* in whiteflies feeding on tomato plants, nearly all modified whiteflies died within a week, compared to only ~20% mortality in controls [21][26][31]. A subsequent systematic search uncovered 49 plant-like genes deriving from at least 24 independent HGT events in the whitefly genome [24][27].

### Animal-to-Plant and Other Directions

**Rattlesnake fern**: A 2005 study discovered that the genome of the rattlesnake fern (*Botrypus virginianum*) contains genes from the Loranthaceae family of flowering plants—the first documented HGT between an angiosperm and a fern. Two possible mechanisms are proposed: ancient root parasitism or indirect transfer via shared mycorrhizal fungi [55][56].

**Human-to-bacteria HGT**: A 685-bp fragment of the human LINE L1 retrotransposon was found integrated into the genome of *Neisseria gonorrhoeae* with 98-100% identity, demonstrating that HGT can occur from a mammalian host to a bacterial pathogen [55][30].

## Adaptive Benefits and Evolutionary Innovations from HGT

The accumulated evidence reveals that HGT in non-microbial eukaryotes, while rare, consistently provides significant adaptive advantages. The following are the most well-documented cases where HGT has led to novel traits or evolutionary innovations:

### Photosynthetic and Light-Response Innovations

- **Neochrome in ferns**: The acquisition of this chimeric photoreceptor from hornworts allowed ferns to thrive in low-light understory environments, driving the modern diversification of approximately 9,000 fern species [41][48]
- **C4 photosynthesis**: Lateral transfer of the C4-specific *ppc-B2* gene between *Neurachne* grasses enabled photosynthetic adaptation to hot, dry environments [78][91]

### Environmental Stress Tolerance

- **Antifreeze proteins in fish**: The transfer of the AFP gene from herring to smelt provided the ability to survive in icy Arctic waters [16][17]
- **Ice-binding proteins in rotifers**: Bacterial IBP genes enable Antarctic bdelloid rotifers to survive 24 years of storage at -25°C [7]
- **Herbicide and toxin resistance**: Wild grasses act as "sponges" for genetic information, with Dr. Dunning noting that "counterfeiting genes is giving the grasses huge advantages and helping them to adapt to their surrounding environment and survive" [22]

### Pathogen Defense and Immunity

- **Fusarium head blight resistance**: The *Fhb7* gene from *Epichloë* fungus confers resistance to one of the most devastating wheat diseases [59]
- **Antibiotic biosynthesis in rotifers**: Horizontally acquired bacterial PKS/NRPS gene clusters allow rotifers to produce antimicrobial compounds against fungal pathogens [4]
- **Immune gene diversification in fish**: 18–19 teleost-to-teleost HGT events involving immune genes (lectins, lysozymes, phospholipases) have expanded the defense repertoire of ray-finned fishes [38]

### Dietary and Metabolic Innovations

- **Plant toxin detoxification in whiteflies**: The *BtPMaT1* gene from plants enables whiteflies to neutralize phenolic glycosides, allowing them to feed on a wide range of host plants [21][26]
- **Cellulases in leaf beetles**: Bacterial GH48 genes enable larvae to digest cellulose-rich mature leaves, reducing intraspecific competition [80]
- **Carotenoid biosynthesis in aphids**: Fungal genes enable autonomous carotenoid production, providing benefits including cold tolerance and pigmentation [8][15][16]

### Parasitic Adaptations

- **Dodder's stolen genes**: Over 100 functional genes contribute to haustoria structure, defense responses, amino acid metabolism, and microRNA-based host defense suppression [29][33]
- **Orobanchaceae**: HGT-acquired genes are preferentially expressed in the haustorium, the parasitic feeding organ, and show strong purifying selection [35]

### Venom Evolution

- **Centipede venom**: A 2021 study in *Nature Communications* reported that centipede venoms have been repeatedly stocked by HGT from bacteria and fungi. At least seven gene families were horizontally transferred, including β-pore-forming toxins and peptidylarginine deiminases. This is "the first evidence for the implication of horizontal gene transfer in the evolutionary origin of venom in an animal lineage" [39].

### Behavior and Reproduction

- **Male courtship in Lepidoptera**: The horizontally acquired gene LOC105383139 from *Listeria* was shown using CRISPR-Cas9 to be essential for male courtship behavior in diamondback moths [26][30]

## Why Is HGT Rare Yet Impactful in Non-Microbial Eukaryotes?

The research consistently points to a fundamental paradox: HGT is individually rare in plants and animals compared to prokaryotes, but when it does occur and persist, it often provides disproportionately large adaptive benefits. Several factors explain this pattern:

**Germline sequestration**: Most animals sequester their germline early in development, creating a barrier that foreign DNA must cross to be heritable. Plants lack this barrier, which may explain why HGT appears more frequent in plants than in animals.

**Genomic complexity**: Eukaryotic genomes have introns, chromatin structure, and complex regulatory systems that foreign genes must adapt to. The finding that many HGT genes in insects acquire introns from native genomes, especially repeat-rich introns, and that these are associated with higher expression levels, suggests a domestication process [26][30].

**Selection pressure**: As Keeling (2024) argues, "The ability to eat other cells and take up endosymbionts both increase exposure to foreign genes… but because it fundamentally changed the kind of variation required by selection to drive adaptation, it also undercut the necessity for most such genes" [1][3]. In other words, eukaryotic complexity reduces the need for HGT to generate adaptive variation.

**The "first do no harm" principle**: The systematic study of HGT in 10 representative eukaryotes found that most effects were in non-coding regions (introns), consistent with selection against disruptive integrations [1][8].

**Mechanistic constraints**: Successful HGT requires nuclear exit, transport to recipient cells, genomic integration, and expression—a multi-step process that is rarely successful.

When these barriers are overcome, however, the transferred genes can provide evolutionary shortcuts that would be difficult to achieve through mutation alone. As Dr. Dunning noted about grasses: "They are acting as a sponge, absorbing useful genetic information from their neighbours to out compete their relatives and survive in hostile habitats without putting in the millions of years it usually takes to evolve these adaptations" [22].

## Recent Discoveries and Future Directions (2023–2026)

The past few years have yielded several transformative discoveries:

**Starship transposons in fungi**: A series of studies (2023–2025) revealed that giant 'Starship' transposons are active drivers of HGT in fungi, enabling rapid adaptation. A 2025 bioRxiv preprint provided the first experimental demonstration that Starship elements can transfer between fungal species, with transfer frequencies ranging from 16.7% to 96.9% [16][20][22].

**Systematic fish HGT**: The 2025 Policarpo et al. study in *Molecular Biology and Evolution* provided the most comprehensive survey of HGT in teleost fish, identifying immune gene enrichment [38].

**Multi-step HGT in plants**: The 2025 *Pytheas* sequence study demonstrated a chain of three successive HGT events linking a parasitic plant, its host, and another parasite [55].

**Holoparasitic plant gene replacement**: A July 2026 study offers one of the most striking examples of HGT, where a holoparasitic plant replaces its own genes with host DNA to survive [57].

**Naturally transgenic plants**: A 2025 study identified 93 new species of naturally transgenic plants, dramatically expanding our understanding of *Agrobacterium*-mediated HGT [60].

**euHGT dataset**: The 2025 compilation of 9,511 HGT candidates provides a valuable resource for future research [4][41].

**CRISPR-enabled HGT**: The proposal of "CRISPR-enabled horizontal gene transfer" (CRISPR-HGT) as a programmable framework for crop engineering represents a potential future direction, recreating the evolutionary process of ancient gene transfers [7][38].

---

## Conclusion

Horizontal gene transfer in non-microbial eukaryotes is a rare but evolutionarily significant phenomenon. The accumulated evidence from peer-reviewed primary literature demonstrates that while individual HGT events are infrequent—occurring perhaps once every 35,000 years in grasses or once every 100,000 years in bdelloid rotifers—those that persist provide substantial adaptive benefits. These transfers have enabled evolutionary innovations ranging from C4 photosynthesis and shade adaptation in plants to freezing tolerance, antifungal defense, venom evolution, and dietary specialization in animals.

The emerging picture is one of a "slow drip" of genetic innovation that, over evolutionary timescales, has significantly shaped the genomes and phenotypes of plants and animals. The mechanisms remain diverse and incompletely understood, encompassing parasitic haustoria, natural grafting, vectors such as viruses and fungi, sperm-mediated transfer, transposable elements, and the remarkable DNA repair processes of desiccation-tolerant organisms. Each new discovery continues to challenge our understanding of the boundaries between species and the fluidity of genetic information in the natural world.
