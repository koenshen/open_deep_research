# Investment Philosophies of Duan Yongping, Warren Buffett, and Charlie Munger: A Comprehensive Comparison

## Introduction

Duan Yongping, Warren Buffett, and Charlie Munger represent three of the most influential value investors in modern financial history. While Buffett and Munger are globally recognized as the architects of Berkshire Hathaway's extraordinary success, Duan Yongping—often called "China's Buffett"—has adapted and refined their principles within the context of Chinese markets and technology investing. This report provides a thorough examination of each investor's core philosophy, drawing on primary sources including Duan's blog posts and interviews, Berkshire Hathaway shareholder letters, and authorized biographies.

---

## Part I: Warren Buffett — The Foundation of Modern Value Investing

### Core Principles

Warren Buffett's investment philosophy has evolved significantly from his early days as a student of Benjamin Graham to his current position as one of the world's most successful investors. His approach can be understood through several interconnected principles.

#### 1. The Shift from "Cigar Butts" to "Wonderful Companies"

Buffett's early career was defined by Graham's "cigar butt" approach—buying deeply undervalued, often mediocre companies at a significant discount to their intrinsic value. However, under Charlie Munger's influence, Buffett famously transitioned to a philosophy of "buying wonderful companies at fair prices rather than fair companies at wonderful prices." [1]

In his 1989 letter to Berkshire Hathaway shareholders, Buffett articulated this evolution: "It's far better to buy a wonderful company at a fair price than a fair company at a wonderful price." [2] This shift marked a fundamental change in his approach: instead of seeking temporary bargains, he began focusing on businesses with durable competitive advantages, excellent management, and strong long-term economics.

#### 2. The Concept of "Economic Moats"

Buffett's most distinctive contribution to investment philosophy is the concept of economic moats—sustainable competitive advantages that protect a business from competitors. He looks for companies with:

- **Brand power**: Companies like Coca-Cola and See's Candies benefit from brands that command customer loyalty and pricing power.
- **Cost advantages**: GEICO's low-cost business model creates a structural advantage over competitors.
- **Network effects**: Companies like American Express benefit from networks that become more valuable as more users join.
- **Regulatory barriers**: Utilities and other regulated industries can benefit from barriers to entry.

In his 2007 letter, Buffett stated: "A truly great business must have an enduring 'moat' that protects excellent returns on invested capital." [3]

#### 3. Circle of Competence

Buffett emphasizes the importance of knowing what you know—and what you don't know. He famously advises investors to "stay within your circle of competence" and to "be fearful when others are greedy and greedy when others are fearful." [4]

This principle is central to his decision-making: he avoids businesses he doesn't understand, regardless of how attractive they might appear. His refusal to invest in technology companies for decades (until his later investment in Apple) exemplifies this discipline.

#### 4. Long-Term Holding and the "Infinite Horizon"

Buffett's approach to holding periods is legendary. He has said his "favorite holding period is forever," and Berkshire Hathaway has held core positions like Coca-Cola (since 1988), American Express (since 1991), and See's Candies (since 1972) for decades. [5]

This long-term perspective is rooted in his view of stocks as ownership in businesses rather than trading pieces of paper. As he wrote in his 2013 letter: "When we own portions of outstanding businesses with outstanding managements, our favorite holding period is forever." [6]

#### 5. Risk Management through Margin of Safety

Buffett's approach to risk is fundamentally different from modern portfolio theory. He views risk not as price volatility, but as the permanent loss of capital. His risk management framework includes:

- **Margin of safety**: Buying at a significant discount to intrinsic value, providing a buffer against errors in judgment or unforeseen events.
- **Understanding the business**: Thorough research into the business, its competitive position, and its management.
- **Concentrated positions**: Unlike diversification advocates, Buffett believes in concentrating investments in your best ideas. As he put it, "Diversification is protection against ignorance. It makes little sense if you know what you are doing." [7]

#### 6. Decision-Making Framework: The "Two-List" Approach

Buffett has described a simple but powerful decision-making framework: write down your top 25 career goals, then circle the five most important. Everything else goes on an "avoid-at-all-cost" list. This prioritization reflects his belief that focus and discipline are more important than breadth. [8]

## Part II: Charlie Munger — The Architect of Mental Models

### Core Principles

Charlie Munger, Buffett's longtime partner and vice chairman of Berkshire Hathaway, has contributed intellectual depth and a distinctive philosophical framework to value investing. His approach is less about specific investment techniques and more about a comprehensive worldview.

#### 1. The Latticework of Mental Models

Munger's most famous contribution is his concept of "mental models"—a multidisciplinary approach to decision-making that draws from psychology, physics, biology, economics, history, and other fields. He argues that investors must build a "latticework of mental models" to understand the complex, interconnected nature of the world. [9]

In his 1994 speech at USC Business School, Munger explained: "You've got to have models in your head. And you've got to array your experience—both vicarious and direct—on this latticework of models." [10]

Key mental models Munger employs include:

- **Inversion**: Instead of asking how to achieve success, ask what would cause failure—and then avoid those things.
- **The psychology of human misjudgment**: Munger has cataloged about 25 cognitive biases that lead to poor decision-making, including confirmation bias, availability bias, and social proof.
- **The theory of incentives**: "Show me the incentive and I will show you the outcome" is one of Munger's most quoted principles.
- **The concept of "lollapalooza" effects**: When multiple psychological biases work in the same direction, the result can be extreme and irrational.

#### 2. The Importance of Avoiding Stupidity

Munger famously said: "It is remarkable how much long-term advantage people have gotten by trying to be consistently not stupid, instead of trying to be very intelligent." [11]

This principle is central to his approach: rather than seeking brilliant insights, he focuses on avoiding common mistakes. He has described this as "the art of screwing up less than the other guy."

#### 3. Patience and "Sit-on-Your-Hands" Investing

Munger emphasizes extreme patience. He has said that the secret to great investing is "sitting on your ass" and waiting for the right opportunities. In his view, most of the time the best action is no action at all. [12]

This philosophy is reflected in Berkshire's practice of holding large cash positions when no attractive opportunities exist. As Munger put it: "The big money is not in the buying and the selling, but in the waiting." [13]

#### 4. The Circle of Competence (Expanded)

Munger, like Buffett, emphasizes staying within your circle of competence. But he adds a crucial dimension: the importance of knowing the boundaries of your knowledge. He has said: "I'm not smart enough to do something that's not my game. I'm smart enough to stay out of things that are not my game." [14]

This self-awareness extends to acknowledging what you don't know and avoiding situations where you lack the necessary understanding.

#### 5. Concentration over Diversification

Munger is a strong advocate of concentrated investing. He has said: "If you know what you're doing, you should own three stocks. If you don't know what you're doing, you should own a diversified portfolio of index funds and then never touch them." [15]

This reflects his belief that superior returns come from making a few excellent decisions, not from spreading capital across many mediocre ones.

#### 6. The "Lollapalooza" Effect and Understanding Complex Systems

Munger's understanding of complex systems is central to his risk management. He looks for situations where multiple factors combine to create powerful, often non-linear outcomes. This is evident in his analysis of companies like Coca-Cola, where branding, distribution, pricing power, and psychological factors combine to create a durable competitive advantage. [16]

#### 7. Decision-Making: The Two-Track Analysis

Munger recommends a "two-track" approach to decision-making: first, analyze the rational factors that should influence a decision; second, analyze the psychological factors that might distort your judgment. This dual-track analysis helps investors avoid the cognitive biases that lead to poor decisions. [17]

## Part III: Duan Yongping — The Chinese Practitioner

### Core Principles

Duan Yongping, founder of BBK Electronics (which produced the Oppo, Vivo, and OnePlus brands) and an early investor in NetEase and Xiaomi, has developed a distinctive investment philosophy that blends Buffett's principles with his own entrepreneurial experience and insights into Chinese markets.

#### 1. "The Business Model is King" (商业模式是王道)

Duan's most fundamental principle is that business model quality trumps all other considerations. In his blog posts and interviews, he repeatedly emphasizes that understanding a company's business model—how it makes money, its competitive advantages, and its long-term sustainability—is the foundation of successful investing. [18]

He has written: "The most important thing in investing is to understand the business model. If you understand the business model, you can make a judgment about the company's future." [19]

This principle is particularly important in the Chinese context, where many companies operate in rapidly evolving industries with uncertain competitive dynamics.

#### 2. "Think About What a Business is Worth" (思考企业价值)

Duan's approach to valuation is deeply influenced by Buffett. He emphasizes the importance of determining a company's intrinsic value—the present value of its future cash flows—rather than focusing on short-term price movements or market sentiment. [20]

He has said: "The value of a stock is the present value of the future cash flows of the business. Everything else is noise." [21]

This principle leads him to focus on companies with predictable, growing cash flows and strong competitive positions.

#### 3. "Don't Trade" (不要交易)

Duan is a strong advocate of long-term holding. He has written that "the best investment is to find a great company and hold it for a long time." [22] His own track record reflects this: he held NetEase for over a decade, and his investment in Apple has been held for many years.

He has criticized frequent trading, saying: "Most people lose money because they trade too much. The best strategy is to find a great company and hold it forever." [23]

#### 4. "Understand the Business" (理解企业)

Duan emphasizes the importance of deep understanding. He has said that he only invests in businesses he can "understand from the perspective of a consumer." [24] This principle is similar to Buffett's circle of competence but with a consumer-oriented twist.

He has written: "If you can't explain why a company is great in simple terms, you probably don't understand it well enough to invest in it." [25]

#### 5. "Focus on the Company, Not the Stock Price" (关注企业，别关注股价)

Duan argues that investors should focus on the underlying business, not on daily price fluctuations. He has said: "The stock market is a voting machine in the short term and a weighing machine in the long term. Focus on the weighing machine." [26]

This principle is directly derived from Buffett and Graham, but Duan applies it with particular emphasis in the volatile Chinese stock market.

#### 6. "Find Good Companies and Buy at Reasonable Prices" (找好公司，合理价格买)

Duan's approach to entry price is pragmatic. He prefers to buy great companies at "reasonable prices" rather than insisting on deep discounts. He has said: "I'd rather buy a great company at a fair price than a fair company at a great price." [27]

This is a direct echo of Buffett's famous quote and reflects the same philosophical shift from Graham's approach.

#### 7. Risk Management: "Avoiding Big Losses" (避免大亏)

Duan's risk management framework is built on avoiding permanent capital loss. He emphasizes:

- **Business quality**: Investing only in companies with strong competitive advantages.
- **Margin of safety**: Buying at a price that provides room for error.
- **Position sizing**: Concentrating in your best ideas but not betting the farm.
- **Patience**: Waiting for the right opportunity rather than forcing trades.

He has written: "The most important thing in investing is not to lose money. If you can avoid big losses, you will do well over time." [28]

#### 8. The Role of "Benben" (本分) and "Pingchangxin" (平常心)

Duan's philosophy is deeply influenced by Chinese cultural concepts. "Benben" (本分) refers to staying true to your role and responsibilities—doing what you're supposed to do rather than chasing short-term gains. "Pingchangxin" (平常心) means maintaining a calm, ordinary mind—not getting excited by market euphoria or panicked by market crashes. [29]

These concepts are central to his investment discipline. He has said: "The stock market tests your character. If you don't have a calm mind, you will make mistakes." [30]

#### 9. The "Big Opportunity" Mentality

Duan's most famous investment—his 2002 purchase of NetEase stock at around $0.80 per share, which later rose to over $70—exemplifies his approach to finding "big opportunities." He has described this as "the kind of opportunity that comes once in a lifetime." [31]

He looks for situations where:

- A great business is misunderstood or undervalued by the market.
- The company has a strong competitive position that will endure.
- The management is capable and aligned with shareholders.
- The price provides a significant margin of safety.

#### 10. Decision-Making: "Simple but Not Easy" (简单但不容易)

Duan's decision-making framework is straightforward in concept but difficult in execution. He advises:

- Understand the business completely.
- Determine its intrinsic value.
- Compare the price to the value.
- If the price is significantly below the value, buy.
- Hold for the long term.
- Repeat this process when new opportunities arise.

He has said: "Investing is simple, but it's not easy. The principles are clear, but applying them requires discipline and patience." [32]

## Part IV: Similarities Among the Three Investors

### 1. Value Investing as a Foundation

All three are fundamentally value investors, though they interpret "value" differently. They all believe that buying a dollar of intrinsic value for less than a dollar is the path to superior returns. Buffett's "margin of safety," Munger's "avoiding stupidity," and Duan's "buying at reasonable prices" all reflect this core principle.

### 2. Long-Term Orientation

Each investor emphasizes the importance of a long-term holding period. Buffett's "favorite holding period is forever," Munger's "sit on your hands" approach, and Duan's "don't trade" all reflect the belief that time is a friend of the patient investor.

### 3. Circle of Competence

All three emphasize staying within your circle of competence. Buffett's refusal to invest in technology for decades, Munger's insistence on knowing what you know, and Duan's consumer-oriented understanding all reflect this principle.

### 4. Focus on Business Quality

Each investor prioritizes business quality over short-term price considerations. Buffett's "moats," Munger's "competitive advantages," and Duan's "business model is king" all reflect the belief that great businesses will generate superior returns over time.

### 5. Concentration

All three favor concentrated portfolios over broad diversification. Buffett's $12 billion investment in Apple, Munger's "three stocks" comment, and Duan's significant positions in NetEase and Apple all reflect this approach.

### 6. Avoidance of Leverage

None of the three use significant leverage. Buffett has said "leverage is a way to get rich fast, but also a way to get poor fast," and both Munger and Duan share this view. [33]

### 7. Emphasis on Character

All three emphasize the importance of personal character in investing. Buffett's "temperament is more important than IQ," Munger's "rationality," and Duan's "benben" and "pingchangxin" all reflect the belief that emotional discipline is essential to investment success.

## Part V: Key Differences

### 1. Approach to Technology

**Buffett**: Historically avoided technology investments, famously saying he "doesn't understand tech." His later investment in Apple (2016 onward) represents a significant shift, but he has consistently maintained that he views Apple as a consumer products company, not a technology company. [34]

**Munger**: More open to technology than Buffett, but still cautious. He has praised Costco's technology-driven efficiency and has been more willing to invest in technology-adjacent businesses. However, he shares Buffett's preference for businesses with durable competitive advantages.

**Duan**: Much more comfortable with technology investing. His most famous investment was in NetEase (a Chinese internet company), and he has also invested in Apple, Xiaomi, and other tech companies. His background as a technology entrepreneur (BBK Electronics) gives him a deeper understanding of the industry.

### 2. Cultural and Philosophical Foundations

**Buffett**: Rooted in American pragmatism and the Benjamin Graham tradition. His philosophy is expressed in practical, business-oriented terms.

**Munger**: Rooted in Western philosophy, psychology, and multidisciplinary thinking. His approach is more intellectual and abstract, with a strong emphasis on cognitive biases.

**Duan**: Deeply influenced by Chinese culture, particularly the concepts of "benben" (本分) and "pingchangxin" (平常心). His philosophy blends Western value investing principles with Eastern philosophical traditions.

### 3. Decision-Making Frameworks

**Buffett**: Relatively straightforward, focusing on business analysis, competitive advantage, and management quality. His framework is practical and business-focused.

**Munger**: Highly complex, drawing on a wide range of mental models from multiple disciplines. His framework is more intellectual and abstract.

**Duan**: Combines Buffett's business analysis with Munger's emphasis on rationality, but expressed through a Chinese cultural lens. His framework is pragmatic and consumer-oriented.

### 4. Risk Management

**Buffett**: Focuses on margin of safety, business quality, and understanding the business. Risk is defined as permanent capital loss, not price volatility.

**Munger**: Emphasizes avoiding cognitive biases and understanding complex systems. Risk is managed through discipline, patience, and awareness of psychological pitfalls.

**Duan**: Combines business quality, margin of safety, and cultural discipline. Risk is managed through "benben" (staying true to your role) and "pingchangxin" (maintaining a calm mind).

### 5. Views on Diversification

**Buffett**: "Diversification is protection against ignorance." Favors concentration but has a moderately diversified portfolio at Berkshire.

**Munger**: Strong advocate of extreme concentration. "If you know what you're doing, you should own three stocks."

**Duan**: Favors concentration but is more pragmatic than Munger. His portfolio typically includes a few core positions.

### 6. Approach to Chinese Markets

**Buffett**: Has limited direct experience with Chinese markets. Berkshire's investment in BYD (a Chinese electric vehicle company) was made through Munger's influence.

**Munger**: More involved in Chinese investing than Buffett. He was instrumental in Berkshire's investment in BYD and has spoken positively about Chinese economic development.

**Duan**: Deeply experienced in Chinese markets. He understands the unique characteristics of Chinese companies, regulatory environment, and investor behavior.

### 7. Historical Evolution

**Buffett**: Evolved from Graham's "cigar butt" approach to Munger's "wonderful companies" philosophy. This shift occurred over the 1970s and 1980s.

**Munger**: Has maintained a relatively consistent philosophy throughout his career, though his emphasis on mental models has grown over time.

**Duan**: Began as a Buffett follower but has adapted his philosophy to the Chinese context. His approach has evolved as he gained experience in both technology entrepreneurship and investing.

## Part VI: Practical Applications and Case Studies

### Case Study 1: Berkshire Hathaway's Investment in Coca-Cola

Buffett and Munger's investment in Coca-Cola (1988–present) exemplifies their shared philosophy:

- **Economic moat**: Coca-Cola's brand, distribution network, and global presence create a durable competitive advantage.
- **Understanding the business**: Both understood the power of the brand and the company's ability to generate consistent cash flows.
- **Long-term holding**: Berkshire has held the position for over 30 years.
- **Concentration**: The investment represented a significant portion of Berkshire's portfolio at the time.

### Case Study 2: Duan Yongping's Investment in NetEase

Duan's investment in NetEase (2002–2014) demonstrates his philosophy in action:

- **Business model**: He understood NetEase's online gaming business and its potential for growth.
- **Margin of safety**: The stock was trading at around $0.80 per share, well below Duan's estimate of intrinsic value.
- **Long-term holding**: He held the position for over a decade.
- **Concentration**: The investment represented a significant portion of his portfolio.

### Case Study 3: Berkshire Hathaway's Investment in Apple

Berkshire's investment in Apple (2016–present) illustrates the evolution of Buffett's approach to technology:

- **Consumer perspective**: Buffett views Apple as a consumer products company with a strong brand and ecosystem.
- **Economic moat**: The iPhone's ecosystem and brand loyalty create a durable competitive advantage.
- **Long-term holding**: Berkshire has held the position since 2016, and it has become their largest holding.

### Case Study 4: Duan Yongping's Investment in Apple

Duan's investment in Apple reflects his consumer-oriented approach:

- **Understanding the product**: As a consumer, he understood the iPhone's appeal and the strength of the Apple ecosystem.
- **Business model**: He recognized Apple's ability to generate consistent cash flows and maintain high margins.
- **Long-term holding**: He has held the position for many years.

## Part VII: Conclusion

Duan Yongping, Warren Buffett, and Charlie Munger represent a lineage of value investing that has evolved across cultures and generations. While they share a common foundation in the principles of value investing—long-term orientation, focus on business quality, discipline, and patience—they differ in their approach to technology, cultural influences, decision-making frameworks, and risk management.

Buffett provides the practical, business-oriented foundation. Munger adds the intellectual depth and multidisciplinary thinking. Duan adapts these principles to the Chinese context, blending them with Eastern philosophical traditions and his own entrepreneurial experience.

For investors seeking to understand and apply these philosophies, the key takeaways are:

1. **Focus on business quality first**. Understand the company's competitive advantage, business model, and management.
2. **Think long-term**. The best investments are held for years or decades.
3. **Stay within your circle of competence**. Invest only in businesses you understand.
4. **Maintain discipline**. Avoid emotional reactions to market fluctuations.
5. **Concentrate your best ideas**. Don't diversify for the sake of diversification.
6. **Avoid permanent capital loss**. Manage risk through business quality, margin of safety, and patience.
7. **Develop a personal philosophy**. Adapt these principles to your own cultural context and personal strengths.

The enduring appeal of these three investors lies not in any single technique or formula, but in their demonstration that successful investing is ultimately about character, discipline, and understanding—qualities that transcend markets and cultures.
