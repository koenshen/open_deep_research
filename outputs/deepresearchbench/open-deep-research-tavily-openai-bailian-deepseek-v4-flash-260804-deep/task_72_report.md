# The Restructuring Impact of Artificial Intelligence on the Labor Market: A Comprehensive Literature Review

## Introduction

Artificial Intelligence (AI) has emerged as a defining technology of the Fourth Industrial Revolution, fundamentally reshaping labor markets across the globe. This literature review synthesizes findings from high-quality, peer-reviewed journal articles to examine how AI-driven automation and augmentation are restructuring employment, wages, skills, and industry composition. The review covers six major themes: job displacement, skill polarization, new job creation, wage effects, sectoral shifts, and gaps and contradictions in the literature. The analysis draws on foundational theoretical frameworks, empirical studies from multiple countries, and emerging research on generative AI's labor market impacts.

---

## 1. Foundational Frameworks for Understanding AI and Labor

### 1.1 The Task-Based Approach to Technology and Work

The task-based framework, pioneered by Autor, Levy, and Murnane (2003), fundamentally shifted how economists understand technology's impact on labor. Rather than treating workers as homogeneous inputs, this approach recognizes that occupations consist of bundles of tasks, and technology affects specific tasks rather than entire occupations. Computer capital substitutes for workers in performing routine, codifiable tasks—both cognitive and manual—while complementing workers in non-routine problem-solving and complex communications tasks [1]. Using Dictionary of Occupational Titles (DOT) task measures paired with Census and CPS employment data from 1960 to 1998, the authors found that since the 1970s, U.S. labor input of routine cognitive and manual tasks declined while non-routine analytic and interactive tasks rose. These shifts were concentrated in rapidly computerizing industries and were small or absent in the pre-computer 1960s. The model can explain 60 percent of the estimated relative demand shift favoring college labor during 1970 to 1998 [1].

Autor (2013) further developed the task approach, arguing that the canonical production function, which equates factor identity with factor role, is static and opaque. Instead, tasks are the fundamental units of production, and skills are applied to tasks. The assignment of factors to tasks is determined by comparative advantage, driven by technological feasibility and economic cost. Capital typically substitutes for labor in routine, codifiable tasks while complementing labor in non-routine tasks, which can lead to employment polarization [2].

### 1.2 Automation, Reinstatement, and the Displacement-Productivity Framework

Acemoglu and Restrepo (2018, 2019) developed a comprehensive task-based framework specifically for analyzing AI and automation. Their core insight distinguishes between two fundamental forces: the displacement effect, where automation replaces workers in tasks they previously performed, reducing labor demand and wages; and the productivity effect, where cost savings from automation increase demand for labor in non-automated tasks [3][4]. The net impact on labor demand depends on which effect dominates.

The most powerful countervailing force is the creation of new labor-intensive tasks, which generates a reinstatement effect that increases the labor share and labor demand. The authors state: "The history of technology is not only about the displacement of human labor by automation technologies... Instead, the displacement effect of automation has been counterbalanced by technologies that create new tasks in which labor has a comparative advantage" [4].

Applying this framework to US data from 1947–2017, the authors decompose changes in the economy-wide wage bill (per capita) into productivity, composition, substitution, and task content effects. For 1947–1987, the wage bill grew at 2.5% per year, driven by strong productivity growth (2.4% per year) and roughly equal displacement (0.48% per year) and reinstatement (0.47% per year) effects, resulting in a stable overall task content. During 1987–2017, wage bill growth slowed to 1.33% per year, with weaker productivity growth (1.54% per year) and a significant negative shift in task content (−0.35% per year decline). This shift reflects stronger displacement (0.7% per year) and weaker reinstatement (0.35% per year), especially in manufacturing [4].

### 1.3 AI as a General-Purpose Technology

Brynjolfsson, Rock, and Syverson (2017) characterize AI, especially machine learning, as a general-purpose technology (GPT) that is pervasive, improvable, and spawns complementary innovations. They address the modern productivity paradox: rapid advances in AI and soaring stock prices contrast with declining measured productivity growth and stagnant real incomes. Reviewing four explanations—false hopes, mismeasurement, redistribution, and implementation/restructuring lags—they argue that the lags explanation is most compelling. Historical patterns of GPTs (e.g., electrification, computers) took decades to fully impact aggregate statistics, and past productivity growth is a poor predictor of future growth (R² < 0.03) [5].

## 2. Job Displacement and Automation Risk

### 2.1 The Frey and Osborne Landmark Study and Its Critics

Frey and Osborne (2017) conducted the most influential early study of automation risk, estimating that approximately 47% of total US employment is at high risk of computerization. Using a Gaussian process classifier applied to 702 detailed occupations, they identified three key bottlenecks to automation: complex social interactions, creative work requiring meaningful novelty, and perception and manipulation of irregular objects in unstructured environments. Jobs most at risk are low-skill, low-income roles such as cashiers, telemarketers, and security guards, while jobs requiring dexterity and adaptability (cleaners, plumbers) are among the last to be automated [6].

However, Coelli and Borland (2019) provide a rigorous critique of the Frey and Osborne methodology. They identify several major flaws: the hand-labelling of 70 occupations as fully automatable was subjective and likely biased by machine-learning experts' optimism; the nine O*NET variables used to represent bottlenecks to automation are inconsistently applied; the threshold (0.7) for 'high risk' is arbitrary, with sensitivity analysis showing predicted job loss varies from 64% to 31% depending on cut-off; and empirically, FO's predictions do not explain actual US employment changes from 2013 to 2018 better than the standard routine-task-intensity (RTI) classification. When controlling for RTI or ALM occupation categories, FO's probabilities lose significance [7].

Arntz, Gregory, and Zierahn (2016), using a task-based approach with OECD data, found that only 9% of jobs in OECD countries are fully automatable, with an additional 32% of jobs likely to change significantly rather than disappear—a dramatic revision downward from the 47% figure [8].

### 2.2 Empirical Evidence on Robots and Jobs

Acemoglu and Restrepo (2020) provide systematic evidence on the effects of industrial robots on US labor markets using data from commuting zones (1990–2007). Their empirical strategy uses a measure of exposure to robots based on national robot penetration across 19 industries weighted by local baseline employment shares, instrumenting US robot adoption with robot penetration in nine other advanced economies. One additional robot per thousand workers reduces the employment-to-population ratio by 0.18–0.34 percentage points and wages by 0.25–0.5%. These effects are robust to controlling for imports from China/Mexico, routine job decline, offshoring, and other capital measures. The negative impacts are concentrated in manufacturing, routine manual/blue-collar occupations, and workers with less than college education. The automotive industry employs 39% of existing industrial robots, followed by electronics (19%), metal products (9%), and plastic and chemicals (9%) [9].

### 2.3 AI Exposure and Establishment-Level Evidence

Acemoglu, Autor, Hazell, and Restrepo (2022) analyze the impact of AI on U.S. labor markets using near-universe online vacancy data from Burning Glass Technologies (2010–2018). Constructing three AI exposure indices (Felten, Raj & Seamans; Webb; Brynjolfsson, Mitchell & Rock's SML) based on establishments' baseline occupational structures, they find that AI exposure is strongly associated with increased AI-related vacancy postings and changes in skill demands. AI-exposed establishments are reducing hiring in non-AI positions. However, at the occupation or industry level, there are no detectable effects on overall employment or wage growth, implying that AI is currently substituting for humans in a subset of tasks but is not yet having detectable aggregate labor market consequences [10].

### 2.4 The "Wrong Kind of AI" and Excessive Automation

Acemoglu and Restrepo (2019) argue that current AI development is biased toward automation—replacing labor in existing tasks—rather than creating new tasks where humans can be productively employed. This "wrong kind of AI" leads to stagnating labor demand, declining labor share, rising inequality, and lower productivity growth. They identify market failures that steer innovation toward automation: externalities, competing paradigms, reduced government steering, private-sector biases, tax policies favoring capital, and missing complementary inputs. The authors conclude: "If at this critical juncture insufficient attention is devoted to inventing and creating demand for, rather than just replacing, labor, that would be the 'wrong' kind of AI from the social and economic point of view" [11].

## 3. Skill Polarization and the Hollowing Out of Middle-Skill Jobs

### 3.1 The Rise of Job Polarization

Autor and Dorn (2013) offer a unified analysis of the growth of low-skill service occupations between 1980 and 2005 and the concurrent polarization of US employment and wages. They hypothesize that polarization stems from the interaction between consumer preferences, which favor variety over specialization, and the falling cost of automating routine, codifiable job tasks. Using a spatial equilibrium model, they find that local labor markets that specialized in routine tasks differentially adopted information technology, reallocated low-skill labor into service occupations (employment polarization), experienced earnings growth at the tails of the distribution (wage polarization), and received inflows of skilled labor. The share of US labor hours in service occupations grew by 30% between 1980 and 2005 after having been flat or declining in the three prior decades. Among non-college workers, service employment rose 53% [12].

Goos, Manning, and Salomons (2014) document pervasive job polarization across 16 Western European countries from 1993 to 2010, with rising employment shares for high-paid professionals and low-paid service workers, and declining shares for middling routine occupations. Their model attributes polarization to routine-biased technological change (RBTC) and offshoring, finding RBTC to be the dominant driver. The model explains approximately 79% of the employment share increase for high-paid occupations, 74% of the decline for middling occupations, and 66% of the increase for low-paid occupations [13].

### 3.2 The Great Reversal in Cognitive Task Demand

Beaudry, Green, and Sand (2013) document a reversal in the demand for cognitive tasks in the US labor market around the year 2000. Prior to 2000, demand for skilled workers in cognitive occupations was rising; after 2000, it declined despite continued growth in the supply of educated workers. High-skilled workers moved down the occupational ladder into jobs traditionally held by lower-skilled workers, pushing the latter further down or out of the labor force. The authors propose a model where cognitive tasks build organizational capital (a stock, not a flow), leading to a boom-bust cycle: during the 1990s (investment phase), demand for cognitive tasks rose, raising employment and wages; after 2000 (maturity phase), demand fell, causing de-skilling and falling employment [14].

### 3.3 AI and the Potential for Rebuilding Middle-Class Jobs

Autor (2024) presents a contrarian perspective, arguing that AI, contrary to fears of mass job displacement, presents a unique opportunity to rebuild middle-class jobs by augmenting human expertise rather than replacing it. The paper traces the historical evolution of expertise from artisanal work through the Industrial Revolution's mass expertise to the Information Age's elite expertise. Computerization concentrated decision-making power among highly educated workers while hollowing out middle-skill jobs. AI, by acquiring tacit knowledge and supporting judgment, can enable a broader set of workers with foundational training to perform high-stakes tasks currently monopolized by elite experts. Examples include nurse practitioners, GitHub Copilot (56% productivity gain), ChatGPT (40% time savings, quality gains for less skilled writers), and AI customer service tools (14% productivity boost, reduced quit rates). The paper emphasizes that AI's impact depends on institutional choices, not technological inevitability: "The unique opportunity that AI offers humanity is to push back against the process started by computerization — to extend the relevance, reach and value of human expertise for a larger set of workers" [15].

### 3.4 Critical Perspectives on the Polarization Narrative

The Economic Policy Institute (2013) systematically challenges the 'job polarization' theory, presenting eight principal findings: technology-based explanations fail to account for key wage patterns, including the stability of the 50/10 wage gap after the 1980s; 'occupational upgrading' has been occurring since at least 1950, well before the rise in inequality; evidence for job polarization is weak and the data fit poorly; in the 2000s, there was no overall job polarization—employment grew at the bottom but was flat in the middle and top; occupational employment trends do not drive wage patterns, as changes within occupations dominate changes across occupations; and the expansion of low-wage service occupations is too small and its timing contradicts the tasks framework. The paper concludes that there is no currently available technology-based story that adequately explains the wage trends of the last three decades [16].

## 4. New Job Creation and AI-Augmented Work

### 4.1 The Reinstatement Effect: New Tasks for Labor

Acemoglu, Kong, and Restrepo (2024) provide reduced-form estimation of the reinstatement effect using US data (1980–2016). A 10% loss of tasks due to automation leads to a 12% relative wage decline and 8.2% reduction in hours worked per adult, while a 10% gain in new tasks leads to an 8.5% wage increase and 26% increase in hours. Automation and new tasks together account for 67–84% of between-group wage changes and 53–68% of employment changes. The authors state: "Automation and new tasks, jointly, explain about 67%-84% of the variation in between-group wage growth in the US from 1980 to 2016, while standard factor-augmenting (skill-biased) technologies appear to explain no more than a few percentage points of these changes" [17].

### 4.2 Empirical Evidence on AI-Driven Job Creation

Chen, Srinivasan, and Zakerinia (2024) use O*Net task data and a near-universe dataset of U.S. job postings (LightCast, 2019–2024) to develop automation and augmentation indices via ChatGPT classification. Applying synthetic difference-in-differences with ChatGPT's November 2022 launch as the shock, they find a 17% decline in job postings per firm per quarter for automation-prone occupations and a 22% increase for augmentation-prone occupations. Skill analysis shows a 24% decrease in AI-exposed skills for automation-prone jobs and a 15% increase for augmentation-prone jobs. The study concludes: "generative AI-driven automation reduces labor demand and skill requirements in structured cognitive-task jobs, while increasing both demand and skill complexity in positions that involve human-AI collaboration" [18].

Hampole, Papanikolaou, Schmidt, and Seegmiller (2026) use NLP on 58 million LinkedIn profiles, 14 million job postings, and O*NET task data covering 2010–2023. When AI could perform most tasks in a role, employment share fell by about 14% within firms. But when AI affected only a few tasks, employment grew as workers focused on complementary skills. High-wage roles heavily exposed to AI saw a 3% employment increase over five years due to productivity gains. Firms using AI extensively experienced 6% higher employment growth and 9.5% more sales growth. The paper attributes about 14% of realized variation in occupational employment share growth to AI exposure [19].

Babina and Fedyk (2025) find that AI investments (measured via firm-level AI worker share) are associated with a ~20% increase in sales growth and ~2% annual employment growth over a decade, primarily through product innovation (13% more trademarks, 24% more product patents). However, AI favors skilled workers: a one-standard-deviation increase in AI investment leads to a 3.7% rise in college-educated workers, a 7.2% decline in non-college workers, and a flattening of hierarchies (1.6% more junior employees, 0.8% fewer middle managers) [20].

### 4.3 AI-Augmented Work: Complementarity Evidence

Brynjolfsson, Li, and Raymond (2023) provide causal evidence from a large-scale randomized experiment in a call center. The introduction of a generative AI conversational assistant raised productivity (measured as issues resolved per hour) by 14% on average, with the largest gains for novice and low-skilled workers (34% improvement). The tool also improved customer sentiment, reduced employee attrition, and increased employee retention [21].

Agrawal, Gans, and Goldfarb (2023) challenge the prevailing view that AI automation inevitably leads to job loss and inequality. They argue instead that such automation can create a 'Turing Transformation'—a scenario where automating tasks that require rare, high-value skills actually expands opportunities for a larger pool of workers, raises total labor income, and reduces inequality. They present a formal model in which two tasks are perfect complements: one task requires specialized skills (rare), the other generic skills (abundant). When AI automates the specialized task, it allows firms to hire more generic workers, boosting employment and wages for the majority while eliminating the skill premium for the few specialists. Real-world examples include GPS navigation (automating taxi drivers' street knowledge, enabling ride-hailing platforms to employ millions), AI in call centers (raising productivity of low-skilled workers), and machine translation (automating multilingual fluency, enabling global trade for non-fluent workers) [22].

### 4.4 New Job Types Emerging from AI

Systematic reviews document specific new roles emerging from AI adoption. Machine learning engineers, data scientists, and AI specialists are the fastest-growing job categories. New roles include AI ethicists and governance specialists, human-AI collaboration managers, cybersecurity analysts, prompt engineers, AI trainers, and AI auditors and bias testers. The World Economic Forum projected 85 million jobs lost but 97 million new jobs created by AI, with net positive employment effects [23][24].

## 5. Wage Effects and Income Inequality

### 5.1 Automation and Wage Inequality: The U.S. Evidence

Acemoglu and Restrepo (2022) examine the role of automation in driving U.S. wage inequality from 1980 to 2016. Using a task-based framework where automation displaces workers from routine tasks, they find that automation accounts for 50–70% of changes in the U.S. wage structure over the period. Empirically, they measure task displacement using industry labor share declines linked to automation proxies (robots, software, machinery) and show a robust negative relationship between displacement and wage changes for 500 demographic groups. General equilibrium analysis reveals that automation explains 50% of wage structure changes and 80% of the college premium rise, while reducing real wages for high-school dropout men by 8.8% and women by 2.3%, yet contributing only a modest 3.4% TFP increase. Workers in the top quintile of task displacement experienced a 12% wage decline, while those least exposed saw 26% growth [25].

Acemoglu and Restrepo (2025) further examine how automation affects wages through rent dissipation—workers displaced from jobs where wages exceeded opportunity costs by about 35%. The loss of rents accounts for one-fifth of the overall relative wage decline for groups exposed to automation. In general equilibrium, automation explains 52% of the rise in between-group wage inequality since 1980, with cost savings from automation increasing TFP by only 3% over the period, as inefficient targeting of high-rent tasks offsets 60–90% of potential gains [26].

### 5.2 AI and the Skill Premium: A Potential Reversal?

Bloom, Prettner, Saadaoui, and Veruete (2024) analyze how AI (e.g., ChatGPT) affects the skill premium—the wage gap between high-skill and low-skill workers. They develop a nested CES production function that distinguishes three types of capital: traditional physical capital, industrial robots (which substitute for low-skill workers), and AI (which substitutes for high-skill workers). AI reduces the skill premium if and only if the elasticity of substitution between AI and high-skill workers exceeds the elasticity of substitution between low-skill and high-skill intensive tasks. In other words, when AI is more substitutable for high-skill workers than low-skill workers are for high-skill workers, deploying AI lowers wage inequality. Numerical simulations using U.S. data from 2019 show that the skill premium falls from 2.00 (no AI) to 1.52 (AI double the value of industrial robots). The authors conclude that AI could reverse recent inequality trends, but caution that reliable estimates of elasticities are needed [27].

### 5.3 AI and Wage Inequality Across OECD Countries

Georgieff (2024) examines the relationship between AI and wage inequality across 19 OECD countries from 2014 to 2018. Using a measure of occupational AI exposure derived from Felten, Raj, and Seamans (2019), the study finds no evidence that AI affected wage inequality between occupations during this period. However, higher AI exposure is associated with a reduction in wage inequality within occupations, consistent with findings that AI can reduce productivity differentials among workers. Possible mechanisms include low-performing workers benefiting more from AI (since AI systems are trained on high-performer practices) or a selection effect where low performers exit the occupation. The authors caution that the study period predates the recent rapid advances in generative AI, so findings may not fully extrapolate to the current context [28].

### 5.4 Firm-Level Evidence on AI and Wages

A study published in the International Review of Economics & Finance (2024) examines the impact of AI on wage dynamics and inequality using data from Chinese listed firms from 2014 to 2022. The authors propose a text-mining-based measure of AI application at the firm level. They find that AI raises overall wages through three mechanisms: productivity improvements, displacement of routine low-wage jobs, and creation of high-wage creative and social jobs. Importantly, AI does not increase executive pay but raises pay for regular employees through job restructuring, thus narrowing the wage gap between executives and regular employees. This contrasts with traditional automation technologies, which tend to widen wage inequality [29].

### 5.5 Generative AI and Heterogeneous Wage Effects

Freund and Mann (2026) develop a general-equilibrium model of automation-driven job transformation, estimating the distribution of multi-dimensional skills using NLSY79 data and O*NET tasks processed via NLP and LLMs. They project that moderate occupational exposure to generative AI raises wages by ~4%, while high exposure reduces them by up to 35%, with large within-occupation dispersion. AI raises returns to social and non-routine manual skills but lowers returns to analytical skills. The effects are mildly progressive: low-earners gain ~2% on average, high-earners see little change. Job transformation—the reorganization of task content within occupations—is the key mechanism driving these heterogeneous outcomes [30].

## 6. Sectoral Shifts: Industry-Level Impacts of AI

### 6.1 Cross-Industry Employment Effects

Autor and Salomons (2018) investigate whether automation—measured as total factor productivity (TFP) growth—displaces labor in the aggregate, using harmonized cross-country and industry data from 19 developed countries spanning 1970–2007. They quantify four channels: direct own-industry effects, indirect input-output effects across industries, between-industry compositional shifts, and final demand effects. TFP growth reduces employment and labor share in the industries where it originates (direct effects). However, employment losses are more than offset by indirect gains via downstream customer industries and aggregate demand expansion, making automation net employment-augmenting. In contrast, labor share declines are not recouped elsewhere, so automation has been net labor-share-displacing over the four decades. The labor share-displacing effects of productivity growth, which were absent in the 1970s, have become more pronounced over time, largely because of a weakening wage response [31].

### 6.2 Sectoral Projections for AI

PwC (2019) estimates the potential impact of AI on UK employment over 5, 10, and 20-year time horizons. The base case suggests that around 7% of existing UK jobs face a high probability of automation within 5 years, rising to 18% within 10 years and nearly 30% within 20 years. The report assumes that total job creation from AI will match job displacement in the long run (based on historical trends), resulting in a broadly neutral net employment effect. However, distributional impacts are significant: net employment gains are expected in health and social work, professional and scientific services, and information/communications sectors, while net losses are projected in wholesale/retail, finance, public administration, and transport/logistics. Higher-skilled, higher-earning occupations see positive net effects; lower-skilled clerical and process-oriented roles see negative effects, suggesting continued skill-biased technological change. By region, London and the South East are expected to fare better than parts of Northern England and the Midlands [32].

### 6.3 Manufacturing and the Robot Effect

Acemoglu and Restrepo (2020) show that robot adoption is concentrated in specific industries: automotive (39% of industrial robots), electronics (19%), metal products (9%), and plastic and chemicals (9%). The negative employment and wage effects are concentrated in manufacturing, routine manual/blue-collar occupations, and workers with less than college education [9].

### 6.4 Sectoral Evidence from Systematic Reviews

A systematic literature review (2026) analyzing 432 studies finds that manufacturing faces 30% displacement risk, customer service 25%, retail 20%, transportation 15%, and agriculture 10%. New job creation is concentrated in high-skill areas: machine learning (30% of new roles), data science (25%), cybersecurity (20%), AI ethics (15%), and human-AI collaboration (10%). Skill requirements are shifting: technical skills demand up 40%, soft skills 30%, lifelong learning 20%, and adaptability and creativity each 10% [24].

## 7. Gaps, Contradictions, and Debates in the Literature

### 7.1 The Frey and Osborne Debate: Overestimation of Risk

The most prominent contradiction in the literature concerns the magnitude of automation risk. Frey and Osborne (2017) estimate 47% of US jobs at high risk, while Arntz, Gregory, and Zierahn (2016) find only 9% of OECD jobs are fully automatable. Coelli and Borland (2019) demonstrate that FO's predictions have no significant explanatory power for occupation-level employment changes once standard routine/non-routine categories are controlled for. The Routine Task Intensity (RTI) index outperforms FO's predictions. This debate highlights the critical distinction between task automation and full job automation—a distinction that is often conflated in public discourse [7][8].

### 7.2 Contradictory Findings on AI Exposure and Employment

Kolko (2026) identifies three key contradictions in early AI research. First, contradictory findings on labor demand: Brynjolfsson et al. found employment fell more for young workers in high-AI-exposure occupations using ADP data, while Eckhardt and Goldschlag found unemployment rose less for those workers using CPS data. Iscenko and Millet found job postings declined in AI-exposed occupations starting in 2022 (before ChatGPT), suggesting macroeconomic factors rather than AI are the cause. Second, weak signals about the future: LLM diffusion is too recent for lasting economic effects to appear, AI capabilities are advancing rapidly, and research methods are immature with no consensus on definitions of exposure, usage, or adoption. Third, narrow research focus: research focuses disproportionately on labor demand while under-exploring productivity effects, labor supply effects, and transition dynamics. Kolko also warns of 'narrator's bias' (researchers, journalists, and consultants are themselves more exposed to AI than past automation waves) and 'streetlamp bias' (research topics well lit by available data may point to different conclusions than those in the dark) [33].

### 7.3 The Three Views Debate

Tawil (2026) synthesizes the academic debate into three camps. The Alarmed (e.g., Dario Amodei, Anton Korinek) predict rapid, widespread job displacement within a decade, citing GPT-5.4 outperforming humans on 83% of expert tasks and a 16% relative employment decline for early-career workers in AI-exposed occupations. The Patient (e.g., Arvind Narayanan, Daron Acemoglu) argue that AI will diffuse slowly over decades due to a capabilities gap (AI systems only complete 4.17% of complex Remote Labor Index tasks), a reliability gap (persistent hallucinations requiring human verification), and an adoption gap (organizational redesigns will take years). The Excited (market optimists like Marc Andreessen, conditional optimists like David Autor) believe AI will create more jobs than it destroys via task recomposition, income effects, and AI-driven business models. The disagreements hinge on the pace of AI progress and the strength of job creation [34].

### 7.4 The Skill-Biased Technical Change Debate

Card and DiNardo (2002) critically examine the SBTC hypothesis, finding several problems: wage inequality rose sharply in the early 1980s but stabilized in the 1990s, despite continued advances in computing and the Internet; SBTC fails to account for trends in gender and racial wage gaps, and for age-related returns to education. The authors conclude: "the SBTC hypothesis falls short as a unicausal explanation for the evolution of the U.S. wage structure in the 1980s and 1990s" [35]. Acemoglu and Autor (2011) propose a richer task-based framework that can account for a broader set of empirical developments, including real wage declines for low-skill workers, non-monotone wage changes, job polarization, and offshoring [36].

### 7.5 The Automation vs. Institutional Factors Debate

Bivens and Zipperer (2024) argue that the primary threat to workers is not AI or technological change, but the extreme imbalance of power between employers and employees. They contend that sluggish wage growth and rising inequality over recent decades stem from intentional policy choices—such as the decline of unions, erosion of the minimum wage, and shifts in macroeconomic priorities—not from technology. The report states: "The root causes of sluggish wage growth for most workers are intentional policy decisions that have led to an extreme imbalance of power between employers and typical workers–technological advances, like AI, have little to do with this and are too frequently invoked as a distraction from these deeper problems" [37].

### 7.6 Research Gaps: What Remains Understudied

Chandar (2025) identifies major gaps in the literature. Aggregate employment effects of AI are currently small but may grow; entry-level hiring may be declining in AI-exposed occupations; AI exposure measures are validated by real-world usage but still imperfect; very little evidence exists for non-US countries; firm-level AI adoption data is needed, as current measures vary widely; future employment trends are highly uncertain; it is unknown which jobs will have growing demand for retraining; AI's impact on education is understudied; personalized AI learning could transform retraining but effects are unknown; post-AI worker tasks are not well measured; AI's effects on job matching are ambiguous; income and wealth distribution effects are unclear; and rigorous modeling of policy responses is needed [38].

The mismatch between supply and demand for research in this topic is "unlike anything I have seen" [38].

## 8. Conclusion

The literature on AI's restructuring impact on the labor market reveals a complex, nuanced picture that defies simple narratives of either mass unemployment or universal prosperity. Several key conclusions emerge:

First, the task-based framework provides the most robust analytical foundation for understanding AI's effects. AI automates specific tasks, not entire jobs, and its impact depends critically on whether it is deployed for automation (displacement) or augmentation (reinstatement). The balance between these forces determines whether AI expands or contracts labor demand.

Second, empirical evidence suggests that AI's aggregate labor market impacts remain modest but are growing. Robots have demonstrably reduced employment and wages in manufacturing, particularly for less-educated workers. AI exposure is associated with reduced hiring in exposed establishments, but aggregate effects are not yet detectable at the occupation or industry level.

Third, polarization of employment and wages—the hollowing out of middle-skill jobs—has been a dominant trend since the 1980s, driven by routine-biased technological change. AI may either exacerbate or reverse this trend depending on whether it substitutes for high-skill workers (reducing the skill premium) or complements them (increasing it).

Fourth, AI creates new jobs and augments existing ones, particularly in roles requiring human-AI collaboration. The net employment effect is likely positive in the long run, but distributional effects are significant, with winners and losers across industries, occupations, and skill levels.

Fifth, wage effects are heterogeneous. AI may reduce inequality within occupations by boosting low-performer productivity, but it may increase between-group inequality unless deployment patterns favor reinstatement over displacement.

Sixth, significant gaps and contradictions remain. The pace of AI diffusion, the magnitude of displacement risk, and the strength of job creation mechanisms are all contested. Research is concentrated in the US and on labor demand, with insufficient attention to productivity effects, labor supply, transition dynamics, and non-US contexts.

The evidence suggests that AI's impact on the labor market is not technologically determined but shaped by policy choices, institutional frameworks, and the direction of AI development. The creation of new tasks, investment in complementary skills, and policies that support worker transitions are critical to ensuring that AI contributes to broadly shared prosperity rather than exacerbating inequality.
