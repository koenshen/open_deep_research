# Avian Migratory Navigation: A Comprehensive Synthesis of Sensory Mechanisms, Internal Processes, and Environmental Disturbances

## Introduction

The ability of migratory birds to navigate with remarkable precision across thousands of kilometers between breeding and wintering grounds represents one of the most extraordinary feats in the natural world. This capacity relies on a complex, multi-layered system of sensory inputs and internal processing mechanisms that has fascinated biologists for decades. Research has revealed that birds integrate multiple compass systems—geomagnetic, solar, stellar, and polarized light—with a sophisticated map sense that allows them to determine their position relative to distant goals. The neural processing of this information, particularly in the hippocampal formation and specialized visual regions like Cluster N, enables birds to make real-time navigational decisions. However, this finely tuned system is increasingly vulnerable to anthropogenic disturbances including light pollution, electromagnetic noise, climate change, and habitat fragmentation, which can disrupt migratory behavior and population dynamics.

## Sensory Cues for Navigation

### The Geomagnetic Field

The Earth's magnetic field provides two distinct types of information for migratory birds: directional cues for compass orientation and positional cues for the navigational map. The magnetic field is a dipole that acts like a large magnet, with its poles relatively near the geographic poles. The magnetic vector provides directional information, while variations in intensity and inclination provide geographic position information [31].

**Magnetic Inclination Compass:** The avian magnetic compass is fundamentally an inclination compass—birds use the angle at which magnetic field lines intersect the Earth's surface, not the polarity of the field. This means that birds can distinguish "poleward" from "equatorward" but cannot distinguish magnetic north from magnetic south. A seminal study published in *Science* (January 27, 2022) led by Joe Wynn analyzed nearly eight decades of banding data from nearly 18,000 Eurasian Reed Warblers. The researchers compared capture locations with three magnetic field measures—intensity, declination, and inclination—and found that only magnetic inclination acts as a 'stop sign' for spring migrants: birds follow an inherited migratory route until they detect the correct inclination angle, which remains relatively stable compared to other cues. This contrasts with fall migration, where warblers use a combination of magnetic cues [52].

**Magnetic Declination and the Longitude Problem:** Magnetic declination, the angle between magnetic north and true north, varies from east to west across the Earth's surface, providing a potential cue to longitude. A groundbreaking study published in *Current Biology* (2017) by Chernetsov and colleagues from St. Petersburg State University discovered that migrating birds—specifically Eurasian reed warblers—can measure magnetic declination to determine their longitude, solving the long-standing "longitude problem" in animal navigation. In experiments at the Curonian Spit, researchers rotated the magnetic field by 8.5° anticlockwise, causing birds to behave as if they were displaced to southeast Scotland, turning southeast to correct their course. Young inexperienced birds became disoriented, indicating the ability is learned. The finding reveals that the bird's magnetic map combines two compass systems (magnetic and astronomical), challenging the traditional separation of map and compass [46].

**Magnetic Intensity and the Bi-Coordinate Map:** For true navigation, birds likely use a bi-coordinate grid map based on magnetic cues. The concept of a "bi-coordinate magnetic map" using inclination and intensity as coordinates has been proposed. A study published in *Proceedings of the Royal Society B* (2024, DOI: 10.1098/rspb.2024.1363) by Packmor, Kishkinev, Zechmeister, Mouritsen, and Holland challenged long-held theories. Researchers exposed Eurasian reed warblers to artificially altered magnetic inclination and declination values (simulating a geographic displacement) while keeping total magnetic intensity unchanged. The birds adjusted their migratory routes as if they were in the new location, demonstrating that they can extract positional and directional information solely from inclination and declination, without needing the total intensity of Earth's magnetic field. This contradicts the assumption that all magnetic field components are essential for navigation and supports the idea of a flexible internal navigation system [49, 50, 51].

### Celestial Cues

**The Sun Compass:** Discovered by Gustav Kramer in 1950 through orientation-cage experiments with starlings, the sun compass allows birds to set a course relative to the sun's azimuth while compensating for its approximately 15° per hour westward motion using an internal circadian clock [1]. The sun compass was discovered by G. Kramer in caged birds showing migratory restlessness. Subsequent experiments with caged birds employing directional training and clock shifts, carried out by Hoffmann and Schmidt-Koenig, showed that the sun azimuth is used, and the sun altitude ignored [3, 6].

Clock-shift experiments provide some of the cleanest evidence for the sun compass. In a classic experiment, birds' circadian rhythms were phase-shifted by 6 hours (light starting at midnight) for two weeks. When returned to the cage, the birds oriented to the east bin instead of south, even though food remained only in the south bin. This 90-degree error indicated that the birds' internal clocks, now set to noon at actual sunrise, led them to seek food toward the sun, which was in the east [5].

A study in *Movement Ecology* (2018, DOI: 10.1186/s40462-018-0126-4) assessed the feasibility of various sun and magnetic compass mechanisms for avian long-distance migration. Key findings showed that the time-compensated sunset compass closely approximates great circle routes at high latitudes but is problematic at polar regions and for spring departures from the tropics due to sensitivity to small course differences. The study concludes that routes following a single compass course are feasible for many populations, but birds likely need to reorient or use map information to reach their destination [4].

**The Star Compass:** In the 1960s, Stephen Emlen published his landmark study of how Indigo Buntings learn to navigate using stars. He raised baby Indigo Buntings in a planetarium where the artificial night sky rotated about Betelgeuse, a star in Orion, instead of the Pole Star. This made the stars move slowest and arc most tightly around Betelgeuse. When the baby Indigo Buntings grew up and the time for migration approached, they felt impelled to fly away from Betelgeuse—they behaved as if they had formed a concept of 'south' as the direction opposite the place that Betelgeuse occupied in the sky [20, 21].

In the pioneering 1967 experiment, when the starfield of the Milky Way was projected onto the planetarium ceiling, the footprints left on the cage floor were concentrated to the side opposite of the North Star, Polaris. These observations led to the conclusion that the Indigo Buntings were explicitly aware of specific stars in the sky and were able to consistently orient themselves in relation to these stars. The buntings oriented themselves using star patterns that appear to rotate the least—especially the North Star, Ursa Major, and Cassiopeia [22]. Emlen found that if the stars were set at a different time of night than it actually was, the birds were still able to orient themselves, meaning they were not solely reliant on their biological clock [7, 48].

**Polarized Light Patterns:** Despite convincing evidence that birds use polarized light cues for orientation, direct experimental evidence for PL sensitivity is still lacking. Instead, cue-conflict and cue-calibration experiments have shown that the skylight polarization pattern near the horizon at sunrise and sunset provides birds with a seasonally and latitudinally independent compass calibration reference. The primary role of PL cues is as a non-time-compensated calibration reference at sunrise and sunset, which allows birds to recalibrate their magnetic compass. This calibration is independent of season and latitude [29].

A study published in *PNAS* (2016, DOI: 10.1073/pnas.1513391113) using zebra finches demonstrated that the magnetic compass is only operational when overhead polarized light is aligned parallel, but not perpendicular, to the magnetic field. These findings are the first behavioral evidence for a direct interaction between polarized light and the light-dependent magnetic compass in an animal. The study showed that birds could orient to magnetic cues under unpolarized light, but when trained with both magnetic cues and overhead polarized light aligned parallel to the magnetic field, they became disoriented when either cue was presented alone [30].

### Olfactory Landmarks

The olfactory navigation hypothesis proposes that birds use their sense of smell to navigate. Two principal versions exist: Papi's mosaic model, which proposes that pigeons construct a map from the distribution of environmental odours within a radius of 70–100 km, and Wallraff's gradient theory, which overcomes the problem of distance limitation by proposing the existence of long-range, stable atmospheric odour gradients [1].

A 2011 *PLOS ONE* study by Gagliardo and colleagues tested the olfactory navigation hypothesis in homing pigeons. Three groups of pigeons were transported in air-tight containers to release sites 53 and 61 km from home: Controls breathed natural environmental air; the Pure Air group received filtered air; and the Artificial Odour group received filtered air plus puffs of artificial scents. All pigeons received nasal anaesthesia before release and were tracked with 1 Hz GPS data loggers. Results showed that only Control pigeons oriented well and homed efficiently. Both the Pure Air and Artificial Odour groups showed impaired navigation (tortuous paths, low homeward components, poor efficiency) within the first hour after release. The Artificial Odour group was particularly impaired, likely due to masking of environmental odours. The study thus provides strong evidence against an activational role of odours and supports the olfactory navigation hypothesis [5].

A 2020 study published in *Scientific Reports* investigated potential chemical navigational cues by measuring volatile organic compounds (VOCs) at the birds' home-loft, in selected regional forest environments, and from an aircraft at 180 m. The measurements showed clear regional, horizontal and vertical spatial gradients that can form the basis of an olfactory map for marine emissions (dimethyl sulphide, DMS), biogenic compounds (terpenoids) and anthropogenic mixed air (aromatic compounds). The Spearman ranking test highlighted that the deviation from west is positively correlated to the sea/land ratio of the air masses trajectories, consistent with a role of the DMS gradient decreasing along the West–East axis [7].

**Seabird Olfactory Navigation:** A study on Cory's shearwaters (*Calonectris borealis*) nesting in the Azores investigated the nature of the cues exploited for oceanic navigation. Birds were displaced and released in open ocean at about 800 km from their colony, after being subjected to sensory manipulation. While magnetically disturbed shearwaters showed unaltered navigational performance and behaved similarly to unmanipulated control birds, the shearwaters deprived of their sense of smell were dramatically impaired in orientation and homing. The data show that seabirds use olfactory cues not only to find their food but also to navigate over vast distances in the ocean [9].

A study on Scopoli's shearwaters (*Calonectris diomedea*) breeding at Pianosa Island, Italy, investigated sensory mechanisms for oceanic navigation. Birds were displaced 400 km from the colony. Magnetically disturbed and control birds oriented homeward even without visual coastlines and homed quickly. Anosmic birds (olfaction blocked) oriented differently over open sea but after reaching a coastline changed to homeward-oriented flight and eventually returned home. The results confirm that magnetic cues are unimportant for seabird navigation, while olfaction is crucial; anosmic birds can use topographical landmarks as a backup mechanism [10].

A study led by Oliver Padget of Oxford University provides strong evidence that seabirds use their sense of smell to navigate over open water. Researchers captured 32 Scopoli's shearwaters on Minorca and divided them into three groups: a control, a magnet-fitted group, and a group whose sense of smell was temporarily disabled via zinc sulfate irrigation. All birds were tracked with GPS. The smell-impaired birds showed markedly poorer orientation on long-range trips over the sea, flying in straight lines without adjusting their path [13].

### Visual Landmarks

Birds rely on visual landmarks (coastlines, rivers, mountains) and other landscape features for navigation. Visual landmarks such as coastlines and rivers play a crucial role in navigation, especially for species migrating over shorter distances. Birds will often follow the topography of a coastline to their nesting sites. Geese navigate based on experience, using landmarks including rivers, coastlines and mountain ranges, and may also use celestial cues such as the sun and stars [20, 21, 22].

A study published in *Life* (MDPI) investigated whether homing pigeons use distant visual landmarks for navigation. Ten trained pigeons were released from a plane approximately 400 m above familiar ground sites under three conditions: clear air (control), above thick ground fog with distant alpine mountain tops visible, and above fog with no distant landmarks. When released above fog without landmarks, pigeons showed poor orientation, tortuous flight, and descended through the fog within 4 minutes, advancing only 0.1 km toward home. They waited for solar cues to continue homing. In contrast, when distant landmarks were visible, pigeons maintained homeward orientation, flew for 20 minutes, and covered 96% of the distance to home, similar to ground-level controls. The experiment excluded olfactory cues (above the atmospheric boundary layer) and local visual landmarks, leaving only magnetic cues and distant landmarks available. The results demonstrate that pigeons rely on distant visual landmarks for efficient homing when familiar olfactory and local visual cues are absent, and that magnetic cues alone are insufficient for navigation [23].

## Internal Mechanisms

### The Map-and-Compass Model

Kramer proposed his 'Map-and-Compass' model in 1953, assuming that birds establish the direction to a distant goal with the help of an external reference, a compass. The model describes homing as a two-step process: (1) determining the direction to the goal as a compass course (the map step), and (2) locating this course with the help of a compass (the compass step). Compass orientation thus forms the backbone of the avian navigational system [38, 47, 51].

The framework deconstructs maps into three components: (1) cues, the nature of the environmental properties that animals use to determine goalward directions; (2) structure, the organization of information used to determine the goalward direction, distinguishing discrete and continuous map structures; and (3) implementation, encompassing strategies relating to how animals approach their goals and how animals combine multiple information sources. Path integration may play a role in map learning across diverse animal taxa [40, 52].

**The Clock-and-Compass Concept for First-Time Migrants:** The most widely accepted theory explaining how young birds of species that migrate solitary and do not follow experienced conspecifics find their way to wintering grounds is the 'clock-and-compass' concept. According to this concept, naïve migrants follow a certain compass direction for a pre-defined period. In the simplest case, when the program runs out, they find themselves in their species-specific non-breeding range [53].

Classic displacement experiments (e.g., Perdeck with starlings, Thorup with white-crowned sparrows) show that juveniles fail to compensate for displacement while adults do, supporting the clock-and-compass model. However, mounting evidence challenges this view. Displacement experiments on common cuckoos, whitethroats, and garden warblers show that young birds can correct for large deviations, suggesting an innate map-like system [17, 53].

### Magnetoreception Mechanisms

**The Radical Pair Mechanism (Cryptochrome-Based in the Retina):** The radical pair mechanism (RPM) is currently the only plausible way in which weak magnetic fields can affect chemical reactivity. The energy of interaction of a molecule with a ≈50-μT magnetic field is >6 orders of magnitude smaller than the average thermal energy k_B T, which in turn is 10–100 times smaller than the strength of a chemical bond [8, 12].

The mechanism involves photo-induced electron transfer in cryptochrome to form a spin-correlated radical pair (e.g., [FAD•− TrpH•+]). Singlet-triplet interconversion, driven by hyperfine interactions (especially anisotropic ones), is modulated by the external magnetic field direction, affecting product yields. Key requirements for compass function include: (1) the radical pair must live long enough (≥~700 ns at 50 μT) to allow Larmor precession; (2) spin relaxation must be slow; (3) at least one radical must have anisotropic hyperfine interactions; and (4) the cryptochromes must be aligned and immobilized, e.g., in photoreceptor cell membranes [8, 15].

In 2000, Ritz, Adem, and Schulten published a seminal paper titled "A Model for Photoreceptor-Based Magnetoreception in Birds" in *Biophysical Journal* (DOI: 10.1016/S0006-3495(00)76629-X). This paper investigated the possibility that magnetoreception involves radical-pair processes governed by anisotropic hyperfine coupling between unpaired electrons and nuclear spins. The model proposed cryptochrome, a photopigment in the eye, as the radical pair candidate molecule [13].

Cryptochromes are blue-light photoreceptors evolutionarily related to DNA photolyases, found in plants, animals, and birds. The radical pair flavin adenine dinucleotide-tryptophan (FAD•−–Trp•+) in cryptochromes is a prime candidate for magnetic sensing in migratory animals. The process is blue-light-dependent and disrupted by artificial magnetic fields [1, 11].

Five cryptochromes are found in bird retinas: Cry1a, Cry1b, Cry2, Cry4a, and Cry4b [4]. Cry1a, the most likely candidate for the magnetoreceptor, is located at the membranes of the disks in the outer segments of the UV-cones in chickens and robins. Its activation spectrum matches the compass light-dependence, and its spatial arrangement across the retina can produce a direction-sensitive activation pattern [2, 4]. A 2025 study published in the *Journal of the Royal Society Interface* (rsif.20250176) titled "Cryptochrome 4b protein is probably irrelevant for radical pair-based magnetoreception" suggests Cry4b is probably irrelevant for the radical-pair mechanism [12].

The definitive review of the radical-pair mechanism, "The Radical-Pair Mechanism of Magnetoreception," was published by P.J. Hore and Henrik Mouritsen in the *Annual Review of Biophysics* (2016, Vol. 45, pp. 299–344). The authors state: "The leading hypothesis centers on radical pairs—magnetically sensitive chemical intermediates formed by photoexcitation of cryptochrome proteins in the retina." Although it has been known for almost half a century that migratory birds can detect the direction of the Earth's magnetic field, "the primary sensory mechanism behind this remarkable feat is still unclear." The radical pair mechanism is "unquestionably genuine," but "what is not yet proven is whether it lies at the heart of avian magnetoreception" [13, 14, 63, 68].

**The Magnetite-Based Mechanism (In the Beak or Inner Ear):** The magnetite-based mechanism involves iron mineral particles (magnetite, Fe₃O₄) that act as tiny compass needles, exerting torque on mechanoreceptors. Crystals of magnetite roughly 50 nm in size are attached to mechanoreceptors within specific cells and behave as small compass needles [31].

The magnetite-based receptors in the upper beak of birds and their role in avian navigation were reviewed by Wiltschko et al. (2012). Key findings include: the ophthalmic branch of the trigeminal nerve mediates magnetic information; this information is probably the information on magnetic intensity involved in the navigational 'map'; from the behavioral point of view, it is interesting that only experienced, but not juvenile birds are affected, as this suggests that the mechanism involved is not innate, but based on experience; all these findings clearly show that the magnetite-based receptors are not involved in the avian magnetic compass—their natural function appears to be to provide birds with magnetic information as one factor in the multi-factorial navigational 'map' [19].

Short, strong magnetic pulses (0.5 Tesla) disrupt these receptors, causing temporary deviations in orientation only in experienced birds, indicating a learned, map-based function rather than an innate compass. The pulse effect is reversible within ~10 days, suggesting superparamagnetic particles rather than single-domain magnetite [19, 31].

The magnetic compass systems of birds and mammals differ in their functional modes and are based on different physical principles. The inclination compass of birds is not sensitive to polarity; it is light-dependent; with the direction indicated by spin-chemical processes in the photo-pigment cryptochrome. The polarity compass of mammals works also in total darkness and is based on magnetite. Kirschvink et al. (2001) suggested that magnetoreception based on magnetite was the primary form of magnetoreception and the starting point for the development of sensory systems providing magnetic information [22].

A study in *Scientific Reports* (2018, DOI: 10.1038/s41598-018-30477-8) demonstrates that trigeminal input is essential for the birds' magnetic map sense. Eurasian reed warblers with bilaterally ablated ophthalmic branches of the trigeminal nerve (V1) were not able to reorient towards their breeding grounds after a virtual magnetic displacement. Sham-operated birds compensated for the virtual magnetic displacement by reorienting their compass orientation towards NW, while V1-ablated birds continued to orient northeast. The results show that trigeminally mediated sensory information is necessary for the correct function of the reed warblers' magnetic positioning system [46].

**Inner Ear Lagena: A Third Sensory System?** A study published in the journal *Interface* (2026, DOI: 10.1098/rsif.2026.0129) presents experimental results indicating that the previously known, but poorly understood effect of disorientation of birds by weak radiofrequency magnetic fields is a manifestation of a third sensory system, aimed at detection of time-dependent magnetic perturbations caused by solar flares or thunderstorms [51].

Nordmann et al. (2025) hypothesized that pigeons could detect magnetic fields in the inner ear. Their analysis revealed that the vestibular nuclei of the brain were activated by changes in the magnetic field. This traces pigeons' magnetic sense to hair cells in the inner ear, showing electromagnetic induction in the semicircular canals activates vestibular nuclei, midbrain, and hippocampus, independent of light [39, 47]. Wu and Dickman (2012) have shown that single vestibular brainstem neurons encode the direction, intensity, and polarity of an applied magnetic field in pigeons, consistent with a ferrimagnetic particle receptor [47].

### Neural Processing

**Cluster N - The Brain Processing Center:** A brain area called "Cluster N" (in the anterior forebrain/visual Wulst) was identified as a site where magnetic compass information is processed in night-migratory songbirds. Cluster N activity is light-dependent and shows right-hemisphere dominance in European robins. When Cluster N is surgically inactivated, night-migratory songbirds cannot use their magnetic compass, whereas their sun and star compasses remain functional [37, 41].

Night-migratory songbirds possess an extensive bilateral night-activated Cluster N region in the forebrain. The region is highly conserved in nocturnal migrants, occupying nearly one third of the central dorsal forebrain, encompassing the posterior hyperpallium, dorsal mesopallium, and hippocampal formation [40].

**The Avian Hippocampal Formation:** The hippocampus of birds is a brain region particularly well suited for implementing navigation based on the map-like representation of familiar landmarks. Unit recording reveals 'location cells' (near goals) and 'path cells' (along routes), with lateralization: right HF more involved in global/distal cues and location cells, left HF in path computation and stable representations. Both sides work together for navigation [42].

The hippocampus integrates all map and compass information to compute navigational goals. The caudolateral nidopallium (NCL) is proposed as a higher-order integration and decision-making center that weighs conflicting cues and adjusts behavior [41].

### Multimodal Integration

Birds navigate using multiple redundant systems, including visual cues (sun, stars, landmarks), magnetic fields, and olfactory cues. They can use the sun as a compass, compensating for its movement, and stars for night navigation. Navigation skills improve with experience; young birds initially navigate poorly [38].

The belief that most birds utilize multiple cues, some of them repetitive, necessitates changes in our evaluations and interpretations of previous studies. No longer should evidence for the importance of one directional cue be taken to imply, even indirectly, a lack of importance for an alternate cue. In all probability, a differential weighting of several directional cues occurs. These weightings should be expected to vary not only between species but, for any individual bird, with changing meteorological conditions and at different points along the migratory path [37].

A 2025 study published in *Ecological Informatics* (Volume 90) investigated navigational strategies of greater white-fronted geese (*Anser albifrons*) migrating between the Arctic and central Europe. Using a decade of GPS tracking data from 117 individuals, the researchers integrated high-resolution geomagnetic data (including solar-wind-induced variations), wind conditions, and potential visual cues. Hierarchical cluster analysis revealed multi-modal, interrelated navigation strategies that flexibly adapt to environmental conditions. Under favourable tailwinds, geese maintained stable headings consistent with geomagnetic loxodrome and magnetoclinic routes, enabling efficient flights toward stopovers and final destinations. Geese also combined visual landmarks with geomagnetic information, adjusting reliance on landmarks between day and night [40].

Birds combine the sun's position with an internal circadian clock. Experiments have shown that when scientists alter a bird's internal clock, the bird flies confidently in the wrong direction. Inside a bird's eyes are light-sensitive proteins called cryptochromes, potentially creating a faint visual pattern like an overlay that shifts as the bird changes direction. It's not a single compass, but an entire navigation network built into a brain smaller than a walnut [39].

## Natural Disturbances

### Weather and Atmospheric Conditions

**Fog and Low Clouds:** A study published in 2019 by Panuccio and colleagues investigated the effect of fog and low clouds on diurnal bird migration, using radar and visual observations at the Strait of Messina (Italy) during spring 2014. The study found that fog was the most important factor reducing bird passage rates, as detected by an X-band radar, indicating that birds actively avoid flying through fog. Wind direction also significantly affected migration intensity, with lower numbers during southerly tailwinds and higher numbers during westerly crosswinds. Air pressure positively influenced bird passage. The researchers suggest that fog avoidance may be due to visual limitations or the lack of thermal updrafts needed for soaring flight [41].

**Cloud Cover and Celestial Navigation:** The U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service notes that the problem of light pollution disorienting birds is worst on cloudy or foggy nights during spring (April–May) and fall (August–October) migration. When celestial cues are obscured, birds may rely more heavily on magnetic cues, but if those are also disrupted (e.g., by geomagnetic storms or anthropogenic electromagnetic noise), disorientation can result.

A review article published in *Journal of Comparative Physiology A* (2017, DOI: 10.1007/s00359-017-1181-9) synthesizes research (2000–2016) on how atmospheric conditions influence bird migration. It introduces microscale, mesoscale, and synoptic phenomena (e.g., thermals, low-level jets, trade winds) from a bird's perspective and examines individual responses—when to take off, where to fly (altitude), and how to fly (speed, orientation, flight mode). Modern methods such as biologging, weather radar networks, and mechanistic models have revealed that birds exhibit more flexible responses to weather than previously thought. Key findings include that departure from stopover sites is often triggered by supportive winds, rain can suppress migration, and birds adjust flight strategies based on intrinsic factors (body condition, age) and external landscape features [44].

### Space Weather and Geomagnetic Disturbances

A study published in *PNAS* (October 2023, DOI: 10.1073/pnas.2306317120) by Gulson-Castillo et al. provides correlational evidence that space weather disrupts nocturnal bird migration. Researchers analyzed 23 years of data from 37 NEXRAD Doppler weather radar stations in the U.S. Great Plains and concurrent magnetometer measurements. They found a 9%–17% reduction in the number of migrating birds during severe geomagnetic disturbances, in both spring and fall. Birds that migrated during such events experienced more difficulty navigating, especially under overcast conditions in autumn, with a 25% reduction in effort flying against the wind. The study controlled for weather, time, and geographic variables, using 1.7 million radar scans from fall and 1.4 million from spring. The effects were strongest during the largest geomagnetic disturbances (occurring on 2–3% of nights) and were more pronounced in fall, possibly due to inexperienced juvenile migrants [42].

A study published in *Scientific Reports* (2023, DOI: 10.1038/s41598-022-26586-0) by Tonelli, Youngflesh, and Tingley investigated the link between geomagnetic disturbances and avian vagrancy (rare birds appearing outside their normal range) in migratory landbirds. The researchers analyzed 2 million captures of 152 species across North America over 60 years. They found a strong association between disruptions to Earth's magnetic field and increased vagrancy during fall migration. For the typical species, an increase in geomagnetic disturbance of two standard deviations above average conditions is associated with a 24% increase in the median predicted vagrancy index and a 250% increase in the number of spatiotemporally rare banding records. Long-distance migrants showed higher vagrancy rates because a small navigational error amplifies over thousands of kilometers. Interestingly, increased solar activity, which disrupts birds' magnetoreceptors, counteracts this effect by potentially preventing birds from using the magnetic field for orientation, thereby reducing misorientation [43].

## Anthropogenic Disturbances

### Light Pollution (Artificial Light at Night, ALAN)

Light pollution has increased at 2.2% per year over the past decade, affecting both nocturnal and diurnal migrants across three spatial scales. At the local scale, collisions with lit structures cause massive mortality: an estimated 1 billion birds die annually in the U.S. from building collisions. Historical events include the 1937 Washington Monument incident (576 birds in 90 minutes) and modern examples (e.g., 400 birds in Galveston, 2017). At the regional scale, light pollution acts as an ecological trap, drawing birds, insects, and bats into urban areas while fragmenting connectivity. At the macroscale, satellite data show migratory birds face higher light exposure during migration than in breeding or wintering grounds, with 125 U.S. cities ranked by threat [2].

A study published in *Scientific Reports* (PMC5818656) examined the association between artificial light at night (ALAN) and the geographic ranges of 298 nocturnally migrating bird species worldwide. Using 2015 VIIRS satellite nighttime light data and the Birds of the World geodatabase, the researchers analyzed mean ALAN intensity across breeding, migration, and non-breeding ranges. The key finding: light pollution is significantly higher within migration passage areas compared to stationary breeding and non-breeding ranges. Contrary to expectations, shorter-distance migrants and species with smaller ranges experience higher light pollution, likely because they remain in temperate, highly urbanized regions. Nearly all species (298 of 299) had some degree of light pollution in all phases [1].

**The Tribute in Light Study:** A study published in *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences* (2017, DOI: 10.1073/pnas.1708574114) by Van Doren et al. quantified the effects of the National September 11 Memorial & Museum's 'Tribute in Light' (TiL) in New York City on nocturnally migrating birds over seven years (2008-2016, excluding 2009 and 2011). Using radar, acoustic sensors, visual observations, and simulations, researchers found that the powerful ground-based light installation dramatically altered bird behavior under clear skies, even in a heavily photo-polluted urban environment. An estimated 1.1 million birds were affected over the seven study nights. Under illumination, birds aggregated at densities up to 20 times baseline (and up to 150 times in some years), flew slower, followed circular paths, and increased vocalization rates. Effects were observed up to 4 km altitude. When lights were extinguished (for ~20 minutes each shutdown), behaviors rapidly returned to normal [3].

**Light Pollution as a Predictor of Stopover Density:** A study published in *Nature Communications* (2023, DOI: 10.1038/s41467-023-43046-z) by Horton et al. used over 10 million weather radar observations to map bird stopover density across the United States. Researchers found that artificial light at night is the second-highest predictor (after elevation) of where migrating birds stop to rest, and the top human-caused factor. Light pollution lures birds into cities, which act as ecological traps due to building collisions, scarce food, and predators like cats. A notable example: nearly 1,000 songbirds died in one night in October 2023 after colliding with Chicago's McCormick Place Convention Center. In over 70% of models, skyglow was identified as a highly influential and consistently positive predictor of bird migration stopover density across the United States [7].

Horton et al. (2019, *Frontiers in Ecology and the Environment*) quantified exposure of nocturnally migrating birds to ALAN across the contiguous US, using weather surveillance radar (1995–2017) and satellite-derived radiance (VIIRS). The authors mapped cumulative migration activity, light pollution, and an exposure index, focusing on the 125 largest urban centers. Key findings: Fall migration shows 53.8% higher total exposure, and 13.1% higher after standardizing for activity, because birds take more easterly (more light-polluted) routes. Half of each season's migration passes each radar site in just 6–7 nights. Chicago, Houston, and Dallas have the highest exposure risk, with magnitudes 19–21 times the median of other cities [6].

Monitoring efforts at Chicago's McCormick Place convention center revealed more than 40,000 fatal bird collisions across 40 years, with illuminated windows on the building seeing increased collision rates. Turning off interior lights on a heavily windowed building has been shown to reduce fatal collisions by up to 60%. In response, the building installed a $1.2 million dotted window film that made glass visible to birds, reducing fatalities by 95% – from hundreds to just 18 during the following fall migration [10].

### Electromagnetic Noise and Radiofrequency Interference

**Foundational Study: Engels et al. (2014):** A landmark study published in *Nature* (2014, DOI: 10.1038/nature13290) by Engels et al. demonstrated that anthropogenic electromagnetic noise disrupts the magnetic compass orientation of migratory European robins (*Erithacus rubecula*). In unscreened wooden huts on the University of Oldenburg campus, birds could not orient magnetically. When placed in electrically grounded, aluminum-screened huts that attenuated electromagnetic noise in the 50 kHz to 5 MHz range by about 100-fold, the birds regained their magnetic orientation ability. Removing the grounding or generating broadband noise inside the screened huts again disrupted orientation. The effect was not limited to a narrow frequency band, and birds tested far from noise sources oriented normally without screening. The experiments were fully double-blinded, providing reproducible evidence that low-level anthropogenic electromagnetic fields can affect the behavior of an intact vertebrate species [13]. The intensity of the interference was far below the limits defined by the International Commission on Non-Ionizing Radiation Protection and the WHO [14].

**Schwarze et al. (2016) - Broadband vs. Narrowband Fields:** A study published in *Frontiers in Behavioral Neuroscience* (2016, DOI: 10.3389/fnbeh.2016.00055) by Schwarze et al. investigated the effects of time-dependent electromagnetic fields on the magnetic compass orientation of European robins. The researchers built a specialized wooden laboratory with four electromagnetically shielded chambers (attenuation of ~10^5 at 10 kHz, >10^6 above 150 kHz) to eliminate external anthropogenic interference. Results showed that birds oriented significantly in their appropriate migratory direction under all narrow-band fields, even at high intensity. In contrast, the broadband field completely disrupted orientation. The study failed to replicate earlier Frankfurt findings of a specific Larmor-frequency resonance disruption, consistent with theoretical predictions that organic radical pairs (e.g., flavin-tryptophan in cryptochromes) should not exhibit such sensitivity. The authors conclude that weak broadband electromagnetic fields are more disruptive than strong narrow-band fields, supporting a radical-pair-based magnetoreception mechanism sensitive to a wide frequency range [15].

**Hiscock et al. (2017) - Computational Modeling:** A study published in *Biophysical Journal* (2017) by Hiscock et al. computationally examined the effects of radiofrequency (RF) electromagnetic fields on the radical-pair mechanism proposed for avian magnetoreception. The authors simulated realistic radical pairs under monochromatic and broadband RF fields. Their key finding: the standard radical-pair model cannot account for the observed behavioral effects unless unrealistically long spin-coherence lifetimes (~1 ms) and RF field strengths far larger than those used in experiments are assumed. Any significant dipolar coupling between radicals abolishes the Larmor-frequency resonance and the predicted 'spike' in singlet yield. They propose that an unknown amplification mechanism must be invoked [16].

**Kobylkov et al. (2019) - Frequency Range Specificity:** Kobylkov et al. (2019) found that electromagnetic noise in the 0.1–100 kHz range does not disrupt orientation in blackcaps, suggesting a spin coherence lifetime of less than 10 µs. This finding helps constrain the possible mechanisms of magnetoreception [19].

**Wiltschko et al. (2015) - RF Field Effects:** Wiltschko et al. (2015, *Journal of the Royal Society Interface*) investigated the effect of radio-frequency (RF) fields on magnetoreception in birds. The key finding: birds tested immediately after exposure to an RF field could still orient in the local geomagnetic field, indicating that RF fields interfere directly with the primary processes of magnetoreception, disabling the avian compass only while the field is present. The study supports the radical-pair mechanism as the basis for the avian magnetic compass. The paper also notes that the sensitivity threshold for oscillating magnetic fields lies around 2–3 nT [18].

### Climate Change

**Shifts in Migration Timing:** A study published in *Nature Climate Change* (December 2019) by researchers from Colorado State University, the Cornell Lab of Ornithology, and the University of Massachusetts used 24 years of weather radar data to examine bird migration timing across North America. The study found that spring migrants are now passing certain stops earlier than they did 20 years ago, with the greatest shifts in regions warming most rapidly. Fall migration showed less apparent changes. Lead author Kyle Horton noted the shift does not necessarily mean birds are keeping pace with climate change, raising concerns about mismatches with peak food availability [21].

A study published in *PNAS* (March 2024, DOI: 10.1073/pnas.2308433121) by Robertson et al. investigated the decoupling of bird migration from advancing spring green-up phenology across the Western Hemisphere. Using eBird citizen science data for 150 bird species over 20 years (2002-2021), the study found that green-up timing has changed along migration routes, but most bird species align their migrations more closely with long-term average green-up dates than with current conditions. Shifting green-up strongly influenced phenological mismatches, especially for longer-distance migrants. The findings indicate limited flexibility in bird migration to adjust to changing vegetation phenology, emphasizing a growing threat from climate change for migratory animals [22].

**Phenological Mismatch and Food Availability:** A study published in *Scientific Reports* (2017, DOI: 10.1038/s41598-017-02045-z) examined phenological asynchrony between spring green-up and migratory bird arrival for 48 passerine species across North America from 2001 to 2012. Using satellite-derived green-up dates as a proxy for peak resource availability (e.g., insect emergence) and citizen science data from eBird for bird arrival dates, the researchers found that both green-up and arrival generally shifted in the same direction. However, across all species, the interval between arrival and green-up increased by an average of 0.575 days per year, indicating that birds did not keep pace with changing vegetation phenology. Nine of 48 species showed statistically significant increases in phenological interval. Regional patterns emerged: eastern species increasingly lagged behind earlier green-up, while western species arrived earlier relative to later green-up [23].

A study published in *PNAS* (July 2023, DOI: 10.1073/pnas.2221961120) by Youngflesh et al. investigated the demographic consequences of phenological asynchrony for 41 species of North American songbirds. Using data from the Monitoring Avian Productivity and Survivorship (MAPS) program (nearly 150,000 captures) and satellite-derived vegetation green-up over 2001–2018, the authors found strong evidence for a phenological optimum where breeding productivity decreases in years with both particularly early or late phenology and when breeding occurs early or late relative to local vegetation phenology. On average, bird breeding phenology advanced only 0.28 days per 1-day advance in green-up, showing birds are not keeping pace with climate-driven shifts. The study projects that under future climate warming (e.g., a 25-day advancement in green-up), breeding productivity will decline for most species [24].

A study published in *Journal of Animal Ecology* (2025) by Belitz et al. investigated potential phenological mismatches across three trophic levels (plants, Lepidoptera, and birds) in eastern North America. Using over 15 years of data (2002-2017), the researchers found that plants and insects had similarly strong sensitivity to growing degree days (GDD) (~0.07-0.08 days advance per GDD unit), while birds had lower sensitivity (~0.01 days per GDD). The study concludes that the bird–insect link has greater potential for phenological mismatch than the insect–plant link, especially at higher latitudes, because birds are less responsive to temperature cues that drive lower trophic levels [25].

**Molt Timing Shifts:** A University of Utah study published in *The American Naturalist* (July 2025) reveals that migratory songbirds in the southwestern U.S. are advancing their fall feather molt by approximately one day per year due to climate warming. Using 13 years of data from 22,072 birds representing 134 species captured at the Bonderman Field Station in southeastern Utah, researchers documented that fall body and flight feather molt are shifting earlier, while spring molt shows no significant community-level change. The shift is likely linked to earlier breeding or delayed fall migration driven by climate change. Molt is critical for flight, migration, and survival, and timing mismatches could harm birds [31].

### Habitat Fragmentation and Loss of Stopover Sites

The Smithsonian Migratory Bird Center highlights that migration is perilous; half of all southbound migrants fail to return. While habitat fragmentation on breeding grounds and deforestation on wintering grounds have long been recognized, recent focus has shifted to the intermediate stopover stage. Birds need adequate food, shelter, and water at rest stops to replenish fat reserves. Key stopover hotspots include the northern Gulf Coast (for songbirds crossing the Gulf), the Atlantic coast bottlenecks (Cape May and Delmarva peninsulas), and inland sites such as shelter belts, desert oases, and riparian corridors. For shorebirds, critical sites include the Copper River Delta, Gray's Harbor, Bay of Fundy, Cheyenne Bottoms, Delaware Bayshore, and the prairie pothole region. The continued loss and degradation of stopover habitat is potentially the greatest threat of all [33].

A 2022 study by Deboelpaep et al. on wetland connectivity along four Palearctic-Afrotropical migratory flyways (the East-Atlantic Flyway and the Black Sea-Mediterranean Flyway) used high-resolution wetland maps and graph-theoretic metrics to model network connectivity. Fewer than 5% of wetlands accounted for over 70% of migration connectivity across all species. Simulating the removal of unprotected wetlands reduced connectivity, especially in flyways with initially high connectivity and for long-distance migrants. The study emphasizes that conservation planning must integrate migration ecology, habitat quality, and the spatial arrangement of stopover sites to protect flyway connectivity [34].

A study published in *Frontiers in Marine Science* (December 2022) examined the effects of habitat loss on migratory shorebird community structure at the Yellow River Delta, a key stopover site in China. Using land cover data and InVEST modeling, researchers quantified landscape metrics (1995–2020) and compared them with shorebird survey data. Results showed that habitat area, largest patch index, mean patch area, and aggregation index decreased while patch number and density increased. Total shorebird abundance plummeted by 90.14%, though richness and diversity indices slightly increased. The decline in mean patch area significantly drove abundance reduction, with larger-bodied species (body mass >250 g) more affected than smaller-bodied ones [35].

A study published in *Ecological Applications* (2019) by Xu et al. investigated the relationship between functional connectivity of migration networks and population declines in migratory birds. The authors analyzed eight waterfowl species wintering in the Yangtze River Basin and migrating along the East Asian-Australasian Flyway. From 2001 to 2014, non-breeding population sizes were estimated from annual counts. The results showed that functional connectivity of all eight species declined continuously after 2001. Population declines were significantly and positively correlated with reductions in functional connectivity, with a strongest lag effect of 4 years. No other species traits (body mass, breeding range size, clutch size, generation length, migratory dispersion, migration distance) were significant predictors. The authors conclude that loss of functional connectivity, driven by habitat loss and degradation, is a crucial driver of population declines in migratory birds [36].

The State of the World's Migratory Species: Interim Report (2026, prepared for CMS COP15) provides an update on the conservation status of CMS-listed species. Key findings: 24% of CMS-listed species are now globally threatened (up from 22%), and 49% have decreasing population trends (up from 44%). Among 386 re-assessed species, 34 changed IUCN Red List categories: 26 moved to more threatened categories (including 18 shorebirds), while 7 improved. Recent literature highlights alarming declines in migratory shorebirds, African-Eurasian raptors, freshwater fish (81% average decline since 1970), and sharks/rays (50% decline in abundance since 1970). Progress in identifying important habitats: 9,372 Key Biodiversity Areas (KBAs) support CMS-listed species (56% of all KBAs). However, only 52.6% of KBA area is protected, and substantial gaps remain in site networks and migratory pathway mapping [39].

## Conclusion

The navigational abilities of migratory birds represent one of the most sophisticated sensory-cognitive systems in the animal kingdom, integrating multiple compass mechanisms, map-based positional information, and neural processing centers that allow for flexible, real-time decision-making. The geomagnetic field provides both directional information through the inclination compass (mediated by cryptochrome-based radical pair mechanisms in the retina) and positional information through a bi-coordinate map system (likely involving magnetite-based receptors in the beak and trigeminal nerve). Celestial cues—the sun, stars, and polarized light patterns—provide additional compass references and calibration mechanisms. Olfactory cues and visual landmarks contribute to the navigational map, particularly in familiar areas and for species like seabirds that traverse featureless oceans.

However, this finely tuned system is increasingly vulnerable to anthropogenic disturbances. Light pollution attracts and disorients millions of birds annually, causing an estimated 1 billion building collisions per year in the U.S. alone. Anthropogenic electromagnetic noise, particularly broadband radiofrequency fields, can disrupt the radical-pair-based magnetic compass. Climate change is causing phenological mismatches that reduce breeding productivity, while habitat fragmentation and loss of stopover sites along migratory flyways threaten the very connectivity that enables successful migration. Natural disturbances, including geomagnetic storms and adverse weather, also impose significant challenges.

Understanding these mechanisms and disturbances is critical for conservation. Mitigation strategies—including lights-out programs during peak migration, reducing electromagnetic noise, protecting critical stopover habitats, and addressing climate change—are essential for preserving the remarkable phenomenon of avian migration.
