# The Role of Need for Closure in Misinformation Acceptance: A Comprehensive Synthesis

## Introduction

The Need for Closure (NFC)—defined as "the desire for a firm answer to a question and an aversion toward ambiguity" (Kruglanski & Webster, 1996)—has emerged as a central construct in understanding why people accept and spread misinformation. This comprehensive synthesis integrates findings from multiple meta-analyses, original empirical studies, and theoretical frameworks to examine the complex relationship between NFC and misinformation susceptibility across political, health, and scientific contexts, as well as across different populations.

The relationship between NFC and misinformation acceptance is not straightforward. While theoretical models predict that individuals high in NFC should be more susceptible to misinformation because they "seize" on simple, available explanations and "freeze" on them once formed, empirical evidence reveals a more nuanced picture. The effect of NFC is highly dependent on moderating factors (e.g., cognitive reflection, threat salience, source credibility) and operates through multiple mediating mechanisms (e.g., reduced analytic thinking, heuristic processing, motivated reasoning). Furthermore, the strength and direction of the NFC-misinformation link varies considerably across contexts and populations.

## Part 1: Theoretical Foundations of Need for Closure

### 1.1 The Construct and Its Measurement

The Need for Cognitive Closure (NFC) was introduced by Kruglanski (1989, 1990) and is conceptualized as a continuum from a strong need to attain closure to a high need to avoid closure [39][54]. The original 42-item Need for Closure Scale (NFCS) was developed by Webster and Kruglanski (1994) and possesses high internal consistency (Cronbach's α = .84) and test-retest reliability (r = .86 over 12–13 weeks) [42][45]. An updated 15-item brief version was validated by Roets and Van Hiel (2011) [47].

The NFCS comprises five subscales: (a) Preference for order and structure, (b) Preference for predictability, (c) Decisiveness, (d) Discomfort with ambiguity, and (e) Closed-mindedness [14][17][42]. A central debate exists regarding the dimensionality of the scale. Kruglanski and Webster view it as unidimensional, while Neuberg et al. (1997) argued it is multifactorial, with decisiveness forming a separate factor from the others (which they labeled "need for structure") [44][36]. Roets, Van Hiel, and Cornelis (2006) supported a two-factor structure, but found that the two dimensions did not map differentially onto seizing and freezing processes as cleanly as initially proposed [57][63][69].

### 1.2 Seizing and Freezing Processes

Kruglanski and Webster (1996) proposed that the consequences of NFC derive from two general tendencies: the urgency tendency (seizing) and the permanence tendency (freezing) [54]. **Seizing** refers to the tendency to look for quick answers, which then become resistant to change in a process called **freezing**. Before the point of belief crystallization, NFC increases the seizing tendency; after crystallization, it increases the freezing tendency.

High NFC leads to impulsive, biased decisions and seizing/freezing on answers—even incorrect ones—to reduce stress. Low NFC allows comfort with ambiguity but can hinder decision-making [46]. Heightened cognitive closure evokes impatience and impulsivity, making individuals leap to conclusions without sufficient evidence. Once frozen, confidence increases as stress decreases, leading to resistance to accepting that the right answer is still out there [46].

### 1.3 Theoretical Link to Misinformation Susceptibility

The theoretical rationale linking NFC to misinformation susceptibility is grounded in lay epistemic theory. Kruglanski's theory of motivated social cognition posits that individuals high in NFC are motivated to reduce uncertainty and ambiguity by forming quick judgments. They are apt to "jump to conclusions" in ambiguous situations and are less likely to be persuaded to change their opinion afterward [54].

Conspiracy theories offer simple answers to complex problems by providing explanations for uncertain situations. Thus, they should be attractive to individuals who are intolerant of uncertainty and seek cognitive closure [6][8]. The theoretical model of conspiracy beliefs identifies three classes of psychological motives: epistemic (desire for certainty, e.g., intolerance of uncertainty, cognitive styles), existential (desire for security, e.g., perceived threats, low control), and social (desire for positive self/group image, e.g., narcissism, social exclusion). NFC falls squarely within the epistemic motive category [20].

Philipp-Muller, Lee, and Petty (2022) identify that antiscience attitudes emerge when there is "a mismatch between the delivery of the scientific message and the epistemic style of the recipient," and specifically note that a mismatch in terms of need for closure can lead to rejection of scientific information [9].

## Part 2: Direct Relationships—Meta-Analyses and Effect Sizes

### 2.1 Meta-Analytic Evidence

**Bowes et al. (2023)** — "The Conspiratorial Mind: A Meta-Analytic Review of Motivational and Personological Correlates of Conspiratorial Ideation" — Published in *Psychological Bulletin*, this is the most comprehensive synthesis available, covering 170 studies, 257 samples, 52 variables, 1,429 effect sizes, and 158,473 participants [9][31]. The strongest correlates of conspiratorial ideation were found to be: (a) perceiving danger and threat, (b) relying on intuition and having odd beliefs and experiences, and (c) being antagonistic and acting superior. The review found that the strongest social correlates were low trust (negative) and social threat perception (positive) [7]. The authors conclude that a need for closure or control were not the strongest motivators; instead, social identity motives drive belief in specific event-based conspiracies, while a desire for uniqueness predicts belief in general conspiracy theories [14].

**Biddlestone et al. (2024)** — "Reasons to Believe: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analytic Synthesis of the Motives Behind Conspiracy Beliefs" — Published in *Psychological Bulletin*, this meta-analysis synthesizes 971 effect sizes from 279 studies (N = 137,406) [20]. Results showed significant, small-to-moderate relationships for all three motive classes: epistemic (k = 114, r = .14), existential (k = 121, r = .16), and social (k = 100, r = .16). Heterogeneity was high. Moderation analyses revealed weaker effects in experimental vs. correlational designs and differences depending on the type of conspiracy belief measure (e.g., conspiracy mentality vs. specific theories). The findings support the frustrated-motives perspective: people endorse conspiracy theories when their psychological needs are unmet [20].

### 2.3 Key Original Empirical Studies

**Leman and Cinnirella (2013)** — "Beliefs in Conspiracy Theories and the Need for Cognitive Closure" — Published in *Frontiers in Psychology*, this is one of the earliest and most cited studies [21][62]. Study 1 (N = 30, correlational) found no significant correlation between NFCC and BICT, but did find that alienation positively correlates with BICT, while interpersonal trust negatively correlates with BICT. Study 2 (N = 86, experimental) manipulated NFCC (via accountability instructions) and evidence type. A key interaction revealed that when NFCC was lowered (high accountability), the influence of prior BICT on attribution was nullified—both high and low BICT groups gave similar ratings after reading evidence. The authors conclude that NFCC does not directly correlate with conspiracy beliefs, but it moderates how evidence is processed [21][62].

**Marchlewska, Cichocka, and Kossowska (2017)** — "Need for Cognitive Closure and the Endorsement of Conspiracy Beliefs" — Two online experiments with Polish participants [2][56]. Experiment 1 (N = 245) focused on the refugee crisis in Poland. Results showed a significant interaction: NFCC positively predicted conspiracy beliefs in the conspiracy-salience condition (B = 0.97, p < .001) but not significantly in the control condition. Experiment 2 (N = 455) used a 2 (event certainty vs. uncertainty) × 2 (conspiracy-salience vs. control) design. A three-way interaction emerged (p = .03). In the conspiracy-salience condition, NFCC predicted higher conspiracy beliefs when the event was uncertain (marginally, B = 0.39, p = .08) and lower beliefs when the event was certain (B = -0.44, p = .04). The authors conclude that people high (vs. low) in NFCC seize on conspiratorial explanations for uncertain events when such explanations are situationally accessible, but freeze on official explanations when they are available, rejecting contradictory conspiracy theories [2][56].

**Hejniak, Szpitalak, Kękuś, Chylińska, and Woźniak (2019)** — "The Need for Cognitive Closure as a Determinant of Susceptibility to Misinformation and Proneness to False Confession" — Published in *Problems of Forensic Sciences* (Vol. 118, pp. 141-156) [65][66][67][68][71]. Experiment 1 (N = 174) replicated the classic misinformation effect and found a significant positive correlation between NCC and susceptibility to misinformation, with participants high in NCC more likely to conform to false information (r(133) = 0.25, p = 0.002). Experiment 2 (N = 103) used a computer crash paradigm to examine false confessions. Results showed that 87% of participants signed a false confession, and 29% internalized guilt. High NCC was associated with a greater tendency to internalize false guilt (χ² = 4.24, p = 0.039), while low NCC appeared to be a protective factor against compliant false confession (χ² = 3.86, p = 0.049) [68][71].

**Jedinger and Masch (2025)** — "Need for Cognitive Closure, Political Trust, and Belief in COVID-19 Conspiracy Theories" — Published in *Frontiers in Social Psychology* (January 22, 2025), this study used data from the GESIS Panel—a representative German survey (N = 2,883) [3][5]. NFC was measured pre-pandemic (2018-2019), political trust pre-pandemic (2018), and COVID-19 conspiracy beliefs and crisis trust in Aug-Oct 2020. Results: A small positive association between NFC and conspiracy beliefs (β = 0.08, p < .001). Strong negative associations for both pre-pandemic political trust (β = -0.33, p < .001) and concurrent crisis trust (β = -0.51, p < .001). The hypothesized interaction between NFC and trust was not supported. The authors conclude that political trust is a far more important factor than epistemic needs in explaining conspiracy beliefs [3][5].

## Part 3: Mediating Mechanisms—How NFC Leads to Misinformation Acceptance

### 3.1 Reduced Analytic/Critical Thinking and Cognitive Miserliness

The foundational mechanism linking NFC to misinformation susceptibility is the **cognitive miser** principle. The human mind tends to think and solve problems in simpler and less effortful ways rather than in more sophisticated ways, regardless of intelligence [10].

**Pennycook & Rand (2019)** — *Cognition* — "Lazy, not biased: Susceptibility to partisan fake news is better explained by lack of reasoning than by motivated reasoning." Across two studies with 3,446 participants, they found that higher CRT scores are negatively correlated with perceived accuracy of fake news and positively correlated with the ability to discern fake from real news, even for ideologically aligned headlines. The authors conclude that susceptibility to fake news is better explained by a lack of analytic reasoning ('lazy thinking') than by motivated partisan bias [12][18].

**Bago, Rand, & Pennycook (2020)** — *Journal of Experimental Psychology: General* — "Deliberation reduces belief in false (but not true) news headlines." Using a two-response paradigm, researchers tested 1,635 MTurk participants. Results support the classical reasoning account: deliberation reduced belief in false headlines but did not affect belief in true headlines, and did not increase political polarization. The findings suggest that encouraging deliberation can help combat misinformation, and that fast, intuitive processing is a key driver of belief in fake news [17].

**Ross, Rand, & Pennycook (2021)** — *Judgment and Decision Making* — "Beyond 'fake news': Analytic thinking and the detection of false and hyperpartisan news headlines." Two studies (total N = 1,973 Americans) consistently supported the 'classical reasoning account' over the 'motivated System 2 reasoning (MS2R) account': higher CRT scores were associated with lower belief in false and hyperpartisan headlines, regardless of political alignment, and with better discernment between true and misleading content [11][35].

**Greene et al. (2024)** — *PNAS* — "Susceptibility to online misinformation: A systematic meta-analysis of demographic and psychological factors." This meta-analysis (31 experiments, 11,561 US participants, 256,337 veracity judgments) used signal detection theory to separate discrimination ability from response bias. Key findings: Older age, identifying as Democrat, and higher analytical thinking (CRT) are associated with better discrimination ability. Older age and higher analytical thinking also correlate with a false-news bias (caution). Education shows no significant effect on discrimination but increases true-news bias [13].

**Martire et al. (2023)** — *Thinking & Reasoning* — "Thinking false and slow: Implausible beliefs and the Cognitive Reflection Test." This study tested the 'miserly thinking' explanation for why people endorse implausible beliefs. Comparing 162 endorsers and 502 non-endorsers, endorsers had lower reflective (correct) scores and higher intuitive (lure) scores, but they also took significantly longer to complete the CRT. The results contradict the idea that endorsers are simply lazy, intuitive thinkers, suggesting that CRT performance differences may reflect 'mindware' gaps (cognitive ability, knowledge) rather than lack of effort [7].

### 3.2 Heuristic Processing and Reduced Information Verification

**Kossowska & Bar-Tal (2012)** — *British Journal of Psychology* — "Need for closure and heuristic information processing: The moderating role of the ability to achieve the need for closure." Three experiments found that NFC positively correlated with heuristic processing only when participants had high ability to achieve closure (AAC). For individuals with low AAC, the relationship was negative. The findings demonstrate that motivation-cognition interactions influence information-processing style [22][23][8][1].

**Kruglanski (1997)** — *European Review of Social Psychology* — Reviewed research on NFC, noting that high NFC leads to reduced information processing, increased confidence, preference for prototypical over diagnostic information, greater use of heuristics, reduced empathy toward dissimilar others, and more stereotypical judgments [22][6].

### 3.3 Reliance on Epistemic Authority

**Baldner, Pierro, & Kruglanski (2019)** — *TPM* — "The Role of Need for Cognitive Closure." This review examines how NFC drives individuals to seek stable knowledge, often turning to stereotypes and ingroup norms, which fosters group-centrism, authoritarianism, and conservative attitudes. Crucially, the article highlights that NFC can paradoxically reduce prejudice if a trusted epistemic authority provides positive information about the outgroup [27].

**Kaufman, Broukhim, & Haupt (2025)** — *Proceedings of the ACM on Human-Computer Interaction* — "WARNING This Contains Misinformation: The Effect of Cognitive Factors, Beliefs, and Personality on Misinformation Warning Tag Attitudes." Two studies found that warning tag attitudes were significantly predicted by NFCC, political orientation, Trust in Medical Scientists, Trust in Religious Leaders, and traits of Openness and Conscientiousness. The study suggests that personalizing warning tags based on these individual characteristics could improve their effectiveness in mitigating misinformation [25][9][22][43].

### 3.4 Retrieval-Induced Forgetting and Misinformation Effects

**Coman, Mello, & Hirst (2014)** — *Social Cognition* — "The Role of Need for Cognitive Closure in Retrieval-Induced Forgetting and Misinformation Effects in Eyewitness Memory." In three experiments, it was shown that NFC augments retrieval-induced forgetting which in turn magnifies misinformation effects in eyewitness situations [14][24][27].

**Hejniak (2019)** — *Problems of Forensic Sciences* — Found that NCC was positively correlated with misinformation susceptibility (r = 0.25, p = 0.002), with the Decisiveness subscale being the strongest predictor [20]. High-NCC participants were more likely to rely on misleading post-event information [21][20].

### 3.5 Motivated Reasoning and Conspiracy Mentality as Mediating Pathways

**Staszak et al. (2022)** — *International Journal of Environmental Research and Public Health* — "The Relationship between the Need for Closure and Coronavirus Fear: The Mediating Effect of Beliefs in Conspiracy Theories about COVID-19." Data were collected from 380 Polish adults. Results showed that belief in COVID-19 conspiracy theories fully mediated the relationship between the fear of the coronavirus and avoidance of ambiguity, as well as closed-mindedness. A partial mediation between the fear of the coronavirus and the need for predictability was also found. The findings suggest that endorsing conspiracy theories may help reduce fear in individuals with high levels of these cognitive traits, by providing a stable, simplified explanation for an uncertain situation [1][11][55].

**Biddlestone et al. (2025)** — *Psychological Bulletin* — "Reasons to believe: A systematic review and meta-analytic synthesis of the motives associated with conspiracy beliefs." This meta-analysis of 279 independent studies (N = 137,406, 971 effect sizes) found significant relationships between conspiracy beliefs and three classes of motivation: epistemic (r = .14), existential (r = .16), and social (r = .16). Considerable heterogeneity was observed across all motives [54].

### 3.6 Cognitive Dissonance, Confirmation Bias, and Fluency

The First Draft News (2020) article explains that the psychological feature that makes us most vulnerable to misinformation is that we are "cognitive misers." Key concepts include: cognitive dissonance (rejecting contradictory info), confirmation bias (favoring belief-consistent info), motivated reasoning (rationalizing desired beliefs), fluency (easier processing increases believability), and bullshit receptivity (accepting meaningless pseudo-profound statements) [18].

A *Frontiers in Psychology* article (2024) proposes that the persistence of misinformation is driven by motivational and cognitive biases in information processing, including deliberate motivations (value, control, truth; accuracy vs. defense/impression motivations), automatic motivations (cognitive consistency theories, self-affirmation, social judgment theory), and cognitive fallacies (epistemic egocentrism, choice blindness, confirmation bias, illusory correlation, continued influence effect) [12].

## Part 4: Moderating Factors—What Strengthens or Weakens the NFC-Misinformation Link

### 4.1 Cognitive Reflection and Analytic Thinking (CRT)

**Pennycook & Rand (2019)** — *Cognition* — Found that CRT scores were negatively correlated with perceived accuracy of fake news and positively correlated with the ability to discern fake from real news, even for ideologically aligned headlines. The authors conclude that "falling for fake news is more a result of a lack of thinking than partisanship" [12].

**Ross, Rand, & Pennycook (2021)** — *Judgment and Decision Making* — Found no evidence that analytic thinking was associated with judging politically consistent hyperpartisan or false headlines to be accurate and unbiased. Instead, analytic thinking was, in most cases, associated with an increased tendency to distinguish true headlines from both false and hyperpartisan headlines [11].

**Greene et al. (2024)** — *PNAS* meta-analysis — Confirmed that higher analytical thinking (CRT) is associated with better discrimination ability and a false-news bias (caution) [13].

**Bago et al. (2020)** — *Journal of Experimental Psychology: General* — Deliberation reduced belief in false headlines but did not affect belief in true headlines, and did not increase political polarization [17].

**Martire et al. (2023)** — *Thinking & Reasoning* — Challenged the "miserly" interpretation, finding that endorsers of implausible beliefs took longer to complete the CRT and did not differ in the proportion of intuitive errors [7].

A study in *Scientific Reports* (2025) investigated the development of media truth discernment from adolescence to adulthood with 432 adolescents aged 11–14 and 132 adults. Results showed that media truth discernment improved linearly with age, first emerging at age 12. The age-related improvement was partially mediated by CRT performance, especially for detecting fake news (not real news) [1].

A study in *Scientific Reports* (2026) with 300 Italian university students found that cognitive reflection (CRT) reduced the association between familiarity and accuracy (weakening the illusory truth effect) but strengthened the contrast between news with and without images (amplifying the truthiness effect). Numeracy improved detection of fake news but paradoxically amplified the illusory truth effect [4].

### 4.2 Need for Cognition (NFCog)

The **Need for Cognition Scale**, developed by Cacioppo and Petty in 1982, measures an individual's tendency to engage in and enjoy thinking [32].

**Borah (2022)** — *International Journal of Communication* — "The Moderating Role of Political Ideology." Using a survey of 797 U.S. MTurk participants (April 2020), the researcher found that NFC reduced misperceptions for both liberals and conservatives, but the effect was stronger among conservatives. MLOC increased misperceptions among conservatives but had no effect on liberals. Misinformation efficacy reduced misperceptions for liberals and centrists, but not for conservatives [7].

A 2025 *PLOS ONE* study (N = 508 American adults) found that across all responses, need for cognition was the only significant factor for successful detection of health disinformation. When investigating each social media post individually, need for cognition was key for 35% of posts, while political affiliation was key for 15% (specifically, posts about politically charged COVID-19 topics) [13].

### 4.3 Cognitive Ability and Education

**Greene et al. (2024)** — *PNAS* meta-analysis — Found that education showed no significant effect on discrimination ability between true and false news, but did show a small true-news bias (i.e., more educated individuals were slightly more likely to judge headlines as true) [13].

**Cosgrove et al. (2026)** — *Personality and Individual Differences* — "When education fails: Narcissism, uniqueness, and need for cognitive closure." This study found that narcissism, and less consistently need for cognitive closure, offset the negative effects of education on generic conspiracy beliefs. Positive slopes increase with each education level, indicating the relationship between need for closure and conspiracy beliefs increases as education does [18][50].

**Hejniak (2019)** — *Problems of Forensic Sciences* — Found that NCC was positively correlated with misinformation susceptibility (r = 0.25, p = 0.002), with the Decisiveness subscale being the strongest predictor [20].

The *Annual Review of Developmental Psychology* (2024) notes that cognitive ability, analytical thinking, numeracy, and metacognitive scrutiny are protective against misinformation, but content knowledge alone is insufficient. Instead, gist plausibility (based on fuzzy-trace theory) determines acceptance [5][34][53].

The APA (2024) states that "Educational attainment, analytical reasoning, and numeracy skills can increase resistance to misinformation, while anxiety increases a person's likelihood of believing it" [38].

### 4.4 Time Pressure

**Kruglanski & Webster (1996)** — *Psychological Review* — Identified time pressure as a key situational inducer of NFC [13].

The *Encyclopedia of Social Psychology* notes that NFC can be triggered by situational factors such as time pressure, boredom, or uncomfortable environments [20].

**Schumpe et al. (2017)** — *Personality and Individual Differences* — "Need for Cognitive Closure decreases risk-taking and motivates discounting of delayed rewards." Three studies found that higher NCC was associated with lower risk-taking and greater discounting of delayed rewards [37].

A 2025 study in *Acta Psychologica* examined the effects of need for cognitive closure and time pressure on individual risky decision-making using the Balloon Analog Risk Task (BART) with 91 college students. Results showed a significant interaction: individuals with low cognitive closure need exhibited significantly more risk-taking in the absence of time pressure compared to its presence. For those with high cognitive closure need, time pressure did not significantly affect risk behavior [36].

### 4.5 Mood, Emotion, and Affect

**Adeeb & Mirhoseini (2023)** — *Social Sciences* — "The Impact of Affect on the Perception of Fake News on Social Media: A Systematic Review." This systematic review of 31 empirical articles found that affect is a relevant but understudied factor; the most common theoretical frameworks are dual-process theory, motivated reasoning, and cognitive dissonance; emotions can mediate or moderate fake news processing [51].

The APA (2024) notes that "People are more likely to believe false statements that appeal to emotions such as fear and outrage. ... anxiety increases a person's likelihood of believing it" [38].

The survey article from *Humanities & Social Sciences Communications* (2023) identified emotions (anxiety, anger) as key psychological distractors that can cause belief and retransmission of false information [33].

**Marchlewska et al. (2022)** — *IJERPH* — Found that fear of COVID-19 was associated with conspiracy beliefs, and belief in COVID-19 conspiracy theories fully mediated the relationship between fear and avoidance of ambiguity, as well as closed-mindedness [23][44].

### 4.6 Threat Salience and Perceived Uncertainty

**Golec de Zavala, Cislak, & Wesolowska (2010)** — *Political Psychology* — "Need for Cognitive Closure, Political Conservatism and Intergroup Hostility." Study 1, conducted during a real Polish-German conflict, found that NFC was positively related to preference for aggressive actions only among self-identified conservatives (b = 0.62, p < .007), not among liberals. Study 2, set in the context of terrorist threat in Poland, replicated this finding and further showed that the interaction was significant only for participants who perceived a realistic threat to their in-group (three-way interaction: NFC × conservatism × threat, b = 0.17, p < .05) [1].

**Baldner et al. (2019)** — *TPM* — Review of NFC and prejudice notes that NFC drives individuals to seek stable knowledge, often turning to stereotypes and ingroup norms, which fosters group-centrism, authoritarianism, and conservative attitudes [27].

A study in *Current Psychology* (2023) used an evolutionary framework to explain the rise of populist "strongmen" leaders. Two studies with Italian participants (Study 1: N = 1,496, COVID-19 threat; Study 2: war in Ukraine concern) found that concern about ecological threats activates NFC, which in turn increases desire for cultural tightness (DCT) and ultimately the need for a strong leader. Serial mediation analyses confirmed the sequential pathway: ecological threat → NFC → DCT → need for strong leadership [38].

### 4.7 Source Credibility

**Hejniak (2019)** — *Problems of Forensic Sciences* — Experiment 1 found that high-NCC participants were more likely to rely on misleading post-event information. The need for cognitive closure can be defined as a manifestation of an aversion to ambiguity and as a drive to possess clear and certain knowledge [21][20].

**Jedinger et al. (2025)** — *Frontiers in Social Psychology* — Using data from the representative GESIS Panel in Germany (N = 2,883), the study measured NCC and general political trust before the pandemic and conspiracy beliefs during the pandemic. Results showed that NCC had a weak positive association with conspiracy beliefs (β = 0.08, p < 0.001), while political trust and crisis-specific trust had strong negative associations (β = -0.33 and -0.51, respectively). Contrary to expectations, no interaction between NFC and trust was found [15][10][2][21].

**Mattavelli et al. (2025)** — *Journal of Experimental Social Psychology* — "Repeating Statements Increases Source Credibility." Four preregistered experiments (N = 90, 65, 180, 435) tested whether repeating statements increases the credibility of the source delivering them. Experiment 1 found a significant direct effect. Experiment 2 showed a significant downstream effect (sources previously associated with repeated statements were judged more credible for novel statements). The authors conclude that repetition influences not only perceived truth of information but also the credibility of the source [33][35].

### 4.8 Message Characteristics: Repetition, Fluency, and Familiarity

**Henderson et al. (2021)** — *Nature Communications* — "Systematic review and meta-analysis of the evidence for an illusory truth effect and its determinants." This meta-analysis of 182 studies (366 effect sizes, N = 31,184 participants) from 1977 to 2025 found that after correcting for publication bias, the bias-adjusted overall effect size is small (g = 0.37, 95% CI [0.30, 0.44]). Key moderators include: item type (standard statements show larger effects than headlines); exposure-phase task (truth judgments during exposure reduce the effect); presence of veracity cues (false cues decrease the effect, true cues increase it); and presentation duration during exposure (longer than 5 seconds yields larger effects) [41].

**Unkelbach et al. (2021)** — *WIREs Cognitive Science* — "The effects of repeating false and misleading information." Repetition increases belief in false information, even when the misinformation contradicts prior knowledge [36].

**Hassan & Barber (2021)** — *PMC* — "The effects of repetition frequency on the illusory truth effect." Two experiments tested how larger numbers of repetitions (up to 9 in Experiment 1 and up to 27 in Experiment 2) affect truth judgments. The relationship between repetition frequency and perceived truth was logarithmic—the largest increase occurred from the first to the second exposure, with diminishing returns for further repetitions [37].

A study in *Scientific Reports* (2026) with 300 Italian university students found that cognitive traits moderate the effects of images and familiarity on judgments of news accuracy. Results replicated the truthiness effect (images increased perceived accuracy) and the illusory truth effect (familiarity increased perceived accuracy). Higher cognitive reflection reduced the association between familiarity and accuracy (weakening the illusory truth effect) but strengthened the contrast between news with and without images (amplifying the truthiness effect) [4].

### 4.9 Social Media Context: Algorithmic Curation and Echo Chambers

**Cinelli et al. (2021)** — *PNAS* — "The echo chamber effect on social media." This study analyzed over 100 million pieces of content on controversial topics (gun control, vaccination, abortion) across four platforms: Gab, Facebook, Reddit, and Twitter. Results showed that Facebook and Twitter exhibit strong echo chambers, with users clustering in homophilic groups and information predominantly spreading within those groups. Platforms organized around social networks and feed algorithms (like Facebook and Twitter) are more likely to foster echo chambers [26].

The *Oxford Research Encyclopedia* entry on "Dogmatism and the Need for Closure" (2020) notes that NFC contributes to group centrism, supporting rigid group structure, authoritarian leadership, and suppressing dissent. The conclusion states that strict adherence to belief structures is associated with denial of facts, prejudice, and support for illiberal ideals, and that NFC can create echo chambers [2].

**Kruglanski's lab** research shows that groups whose participants experience a high need for cognitive closure tend to develop a syndrome referred to as "group centrism." This includes the development of autocratic leadership, conservatism with regards to group norms, rejection of opinion deviates, conformity, and the tendency toward ingroup favoritism and outgroup derogation [26][29].

A 2018 MIT study (Vosoughi, Roy, & Aral) found that false news was retweeted 70% more than true news on Twitter. False stories reached 1,500 users six times faster than true ones. The study determined that humans, not bots, were primarily responsible for the spread of false information on the platform [20].

## Part 5: Need for Closure Across Different Contexts

### 5.1 Political Contexts

#### 5.1.1 NFC and Political Conspiracy Theories

**Marchlewska, Cichocka, & Kossowska (2017)** — "Addicted to answers: Need for cognitive closure and the endorsement of conspiracy beliefs" (*European Journal of Social Psychology*) — This highly cited paper investigates the relationship between NFC and conspiracy beliefs. **Experiment 1 (N=245 Polish internet users):** NFC positively predicted conspiracy beliefs, especially when conspiratorial explanations were made salient (interaction significant). **Experiment 2 (N=455):** A three-way interaction showed that in the conspiracy-salience condition, NFC positively predicted conspiracy beliefs for the uncertain event (marginally significant) and negatively predicted for the certain event (significant). The findings suggest that people high in NFC "seize" on salient conspiratorial explanations for uncertain events, but "freeze" on official explanations when they are available, rejecting conspiracy theories [45][49][55].

**Leman & Cinnirella (2013)** — "Beliefs in conspiracy theories and the need for cognitive closure" (*Frontiers in Psychology*) — Two studies examined the relationship between NFC, levels of belief in real-world conspiracy theories (BICT), and the attribution of conspiracy theories to explain events. **Study 1 (N=30)** found no direct relationship between NFC and BICT, but did find that alienation positively correlated with BICT, and interpersonal trust negatively correlated with BICT. **Study 2 (N=86)** experimentally manipulated NFC (through accountability instructions) and evidence type. When NFC was lowered (high accountability), the effect of prior BICT disappeared, suggesting that systematic processing reduces the influence of pre-existing beliefs [4][50][52][56].

**Umam, Muluk, & Milla (2017)** — "The need for cognitive closure and belief in conspiracy theories" — This study with 211 participants found that NFC predicts belief in conspiracy theories, but only specifically for the "control of information" conspiracy theme (i.e., the notion that authorities conceal important information). No significant interaction between NFC and religious fundamentalism was found in predicting conspiracy beliefs [48].

#### 5.1.2 NFC and COVID-19 Conspiracy Beliefs (Political Context)

**Jedinger & Masch (2025)** — "Need for cognitive closure, political trust, and belief in COVID-19 conspiracy theories" (*Frontiers in Social Psychology*, January 22, 2025) — Using data from the representative GESIS Panel in Germany (N = 2,883), the authors measured NFC and general political trust before the pandemic (2018–2019) and COVID-19 conspiracy beliefs and specific crisis trust during the second wave of the pandemic (August–October 2020). Results: At the bivariate level, NFC showed a weak positive association with conspiracy beliefs (r = 0.12), while both political trust (r = -0.37) and crisis-specific trust (r = -0.55) were strongly negatively correlated. In regression models, NFC remained a significant but small positive predictor (β = 0.08), whereas political trust (β = -0.33) and crisis trust (β = -0.51) were the strongest negative predictors. The hypothesized interaction between NFC and trust was not supported [1][47][53].

**Wintterlin (2025)** — "Not so different after all? Antecedents of believing in misinformation and conspiracy theories on COVID-19" (*Harvard Kennedy School Misinformation Review*, October 2025) — Using a representative cross-sectional survey in Germany (N = 2,953, March 2024), the study measured belief in five COVID-19 conspiracy theories and five pieces of misinformation. Results showed that conspiracy mentality and the belief that "truth is political" are the strongest predictors of both types of belief. Cognitive factors such as intuitive thinking and actively open-minded thinking did not significantly predict belief in either type of deceptive content [2].

#### 5.1.3 NFC, Political Ideology, and Authoritarianism

**Jost, Glaser, Sulloway, & Kruglanski (2003)** — "Political Conservatism as Motivated Social Cognition" (*Psychological Bulletin*) — This landmark meta-analysis integrated theories of personality, epistemic and existential needs, and ideological rationalization to explain political conservatism. The meta-analysis included 88 samples from 12 countries totaling 22,818 participants. Key psychological predictors of political conservatism included: death anxiety (r = .50), system instability (.47), dogmatism-intolerance of ambiguity (.34), low openness to experience (-.32), low uncertainty tolerance (-.27), high need for order/structure/closure (.26), low integrative complexity (-.20), fear of threat/loss (.18), and low self-esteem (-.09) [43][44][45][50][52][55].

**De keersmaecker et al. (2017)** — "Need for closure and perceived threat as bases of right-wing authoritarianism: A longitudinal moderation approach" (*Social Cognition*, Vol. 35, pp. 433-449) — Using a representative Dutch sample (N = 588) and a longitudinal design, the study found a significant interaction: NFC positively predicted right-wing authoritarianism (RWA) only when perceived threat was low; when threat was high, NFC had no significant relationship with RWA [46].

**Crowson, DeBacker, & Thoma (2004)** — "The role of need for closure in conservative beliefs and racism" (*Personality and Social Psychology Bulletin*) — Using structural equation modeling across two student samples (N=399, N=330) and one adult sample (N=379), the model where need for closure drives authoritarianism (Model 2) consistently fit better, supporting the view that authoritarianism is a set of generalized beliefs rather than a personality trait [42].

**Berggren, Akrami, Bergh, & Ekehammar (2019)** — "Motivated Social Cognition and Authoritarianism" (*Journal of Individual Differences*, Vol. 40, No. 4) — Across two studies, results confirmed that the full motivated social cognition scales (e.g., NFC, intolerance of ambiguity, need for cognition) did not significantly predict authoritarianism beyond the closed-mindedness items, indicating that the link is fully captured by closed-mindedness [41][53].

### 5.2 Health Contexts

#### 5.2.1 NFC and Health Misinformation / COVID-19 Misinformation

**Nowak, B., et al. (2022)** — "The Relationship between the Need for Closure and Coronavirus Fear: The Mediating Effect of Beliefs in Conspiracy Theories about COVID-19" (*International Journal of Environmental Research and Public Health*) — A sample of 380 adults (mean age 33.4) completed online versions of the Fear of COVID-19 Scale, a short Need for Closure Scale, and a newly developed Conspiracy Theories about the Coronavirus Scale. Using structural equation modeling, the authors found that belief in conspiracy theories fully mediated the relationship between coronavirus fear and two dimensions of need for closure: avoidance of ambiguity and closed-mindedness. A partial mediation was found for the need for predictability. The results suggest that conspiracy theories may provide a simplified, stable framework that reduces anxiety for individuals with certain cognitive styles [3][1].

**Jedinger & Masch (2025)** — As detailed above, this study found that NFC plays only a minor role in COVID-19 conspiracy beliefs (β = 0.08), while political trust (β = -0.33) and crisis-specific trust (β = -0.51) are much stronger predictors [1][47].

**Wintterlin (2025)** — As detailed above, this study found that conspiracy mentality and the belief that "truth is political" are the strongest predictors of COVID-19 misinformation and conspiracy beliefs, while cognitive factors such as intuitive thinking and actively open-minded thinking did not significantly predict belief [2].

#### 5.2.2 Vaccine Hesitancy and Misinformation

**Kricorian et al. (2022)** — "COVID-19 vaccine hesitancy: misinformation and perceptions of vaccine safety" (PMC) — This national online survey of 1,950 U.S. adults (fielded January 4–10, 2021) found that only 41% believed the vaccine was safe; 59% did not. Those who believed it unsafe were significantly less likely to intend to get vaccinated, more likely to delay vaccination, and more likely to endorse misinformation. They also knew less about COVID-19 transmission and were less likely to trust scientific sources like the CDC. The authors conclude that low health literacy and misinformation drive hesitancy [17].

**Zimmerman et al. (2023)** — "Misinformation and COVID-19 vaccine hesitancy" (*Vaccine*) — Two qualitative studies. Study 1 (October–November 2020) conducted semi-structured interviews with 30 older adults (65+ years) in the US. Study 2 (May–June 2021) surveyed 718 unvaccinated adults (including 319 older adults) via open-ended questions, revealing six categories of misinformation fueling hesitancy: medical, scientific, political, media, religious, and technological. Six reasons for not getting vaccinated were identified: unforeseen future effects, doubts about effectiveness, commercial profiteering, preference for natural immunity, personal freedom, and COVID-19 denial [13][21].

**Cleveland Clinic Journal of Medicine (2024)** — "Meeting the challenge of vaccine hesitancy" — Defines vaccine hesitancy per WHO SAGE as refusal or delay in accepting vaccines. Notes that hesitancy is multifactorial, influenced by environmental, personal, social, and safety/vaccine-related factors. Emphasizes healthcare providers' critical role in building trust through tailored communication, active listening, and community engagement. Notes that a recent study of 1,967 US adults demonstrated a strong link between trust in one's physician and vaccination rates [11].

**CSIS (2020)** — "The Risks of Misinformation and Vaccine Hesitancy within the Covid-19 Crisis" — Argues that misinformation and vaccine hesitancy are urgent national security matters. At the time, only 50% of Americans planned to get a vaccine. Identifies four national security dimensions: (1) COVID-19 disproportionately impacts minorities and gig workers, aggravating social inequities; (2) lack of outbreak control undermines U.S. competitiveness; (3) prolonged school closures damage U.S. education and research; (4) drops in routine immunization coverage risk outbreaks of other diseases [12].

### 5.3 Science Contexts

#### 5.3.1 Climate Change Denial and NFC

**Cook, J. (2020)** — "Deconstructing Climate Science Denial" — Summarizes the state of climate science denial, emphasizing that while there is overwhelming scientific consensus on human-caused global warming, a small minority of contrarian scientists and organizations disseminate misinformation. The landscape of climate misinformation is categorized into five overarching claims: it's not real, it's not us, it's not bad, solutions won't work, and experts are unreliable. The rhetorical strategies are grouped under the FLICC framework: Fake experts, Logical fallacies, Impossible expectations, Cherry picking, and Conspiracy theories. Each denial technique is linked to underlying psychological biases (e.g., confirmation bias, disconfirmation bias, conspiratorial thinking) [34][26].

**Cook, J. (Skeptical Science)** — "A history of FLICC: the 5 techniques of science denial" — Provides a detailed history and taxonomy of the FLICC framework. FLICC stands for Fake experts, Logical fallacies, Impossible expectations, Cherry picking, and Conspiracy theories. The concept originated from Mark Hoofnagle's 2007 blog post identifying five denialist tactics, later formalized by Pascal Diethelm and Martin McKee in 2009. John Cook adapted these into the FLICC acronym in 2013 [30].

**Jacques, P. (2025)** — "Foundations of climate change denial: Anti-environmentalism and anti-science" (*PLOS One*) — A 12-year analysis of over 100 books rejecting climate science. The study coded arguments into four categories: Trend Denial (81% of books claim global warming never occurred or data is unreliable), Attribution Denial (94% say humans are not the primary cause), Impact Denial (76% argue warming/CO2 will be beneficial or not harmful), and Policy Denial (94% claim mitigation policies are ineffective/harmful). The paper concludes opposition is driven by "anti-reflexivity"—resistance to change—and is most organized in economically powerful nations with entrenched fossil fuel industries, especially the U.S. [33][40][25].

**University of Michigan Study (2024)** — "Nearly 15% of Americans deny climate change, study finds" (*Scientific Reports*, February 14, 2024) — Using AI to analyze 7.4 million geocoded tweets from 2017-2019, researchers found that 14.8% of Americans deny climate change. Denialism is highest in central and southern U.S. (e.g., Oklahoma, Mississippi, Alabama, North Dakota). Political affiliation is the strongest predictor, followed by lower education levels, lower COVID-19 vaccination rates (a proxy for science skepticism), higher carbon intensity of the regional economy, and lower income. Network analysis revealed two distinct communities—deniers and believers—with almost no cross-communication (only 0.02% of co-retweets involved opposing views) [30][36].

**Hayhoe, K. (2026)** — "Is climate denialism on the rise?" (Radio Davos podcast, May 7, 2026) — Despite increased online rhetoric and amplified false information due to social media algorithm changes, polling data shows public worry about climate change has actually increased worldwide, driven by direct experience of extreme weather. Hayhoe explains that while a small minority remains dismissive, most people are concerned but lack a connection between knowledge, values, and action. Key data: MIT study found false info spreads six times faster than truth; 10% of US population is dismissive [43][38].

**APS (Australian Psychological Society)** — "The psychology of climate change denial" — Notes that while the vast majority of people claim to be concerned about the climate, large numbers of people also avoid, minimise, switch off, or distance themselves from effectively engaging with the problems. A small but noisy minority actively denies that there even is a problem [41][29].

#### 5.3.2 Evolution Denial

**Rutjens & Većkalov (2022)** — "Conspiracy beliefs and science rejection" (*Current Opinion in Psychology*) — Reviews the relationship between conspiracy beliefs and science rejection. Notes that rejecting evolutionary theory is predicted by religiosity, while skepticism about GMOs is reliably predicted by low science knowledge. Conspiracy beliefs about science are fueled by perceptions that science is a social enterprise, policy implications clash with personal values, and science is inherently uncertain. The authors discuss pathways to curb rejection, including communicating scientific consensus, improving scientist portrayals, and the psychological distance to science (PSYDISC) model [3][6].

**Wikipedia** — "Level of support for evolution" — Nearly all (around 98%) of the scientific community accepts evolution as the dominant scientific theory of biological diversity. 87% accepting that evolution occurs due to natural processes. A 2009 poll by the Pew Research Center found that "Nearly all scientists (97%) say humans and other living things have evolved over time—87% say evolution is due to natural processes." The dominant position among scientists is shared by only about a third (32%) of the public [9].

#### 5.3.3 GMO Skepticism

**Rutjens & Većkalov (2022)** — Notes that skepticism about GMOs is reliably predicted by low science knowledge, in contrast to evolution rejection which is predicted by religiosity [3][6].

**Tagliabue (2016)** — "The necessary 'GMO' denialism and scientific consensus" (*JCOM*) — Discusses the nature of GMO denialism [1].

## Part 6: Need for Closure Across Different Populations

### 6.1 Cross-Cultural Comparisons

**Fu, J.H.Y., Morris, M.W., Lee, S.L., Chao, M.C., & Chiu, C.Y. (2007)** — "Epistemic motives and cultural conformity: Need for closure, culture, and context as determinants of conflict judgments" (*Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*) — Three studies support the proposal that need for closure (NFC) involves a desire for consensual validation that leads to cultural conformity. The research found that NFC leads attributors to respond to an ambiguous social event by increasing reliance on implicit theories received from their cultural environment. This means that NFC amplifies culturally normative responses—individuals high in NFC conform more strongly to their culture's typical conflict resolution styles [4][7][19][20][21][24][27].

**Kossowska, M., Van Hiel, A., et al. (2002)** — "The Need for Cognitive Closure Scale: Structure, Cross-Cultural Invariance, and Comparison of Mean Ratings between European-American and East Asian Samples" (*Psychologica Belgica*) — This study examined the factor structure and cross-cultural invariance of the NFCS across four samples: Polish (N=340), Flemish (N=623), Korean (N=429), and American (N=240). Results support a two second-order factor model, consistent with prior studies. The scale demonstrated structural invariance, partial metric invariance, and partial scalar invariance across samples, indicating that the need for cognitive closure has a comparable meaning across cultures. Mean scores were significantly higher in American and Korean samples compared to Flemish and Polish samples [4][7][19][20][21][24][27].

### 6.2 Age Differences

**Hejniak et al. (2019)** — Found that NCC was positively correlated with misinformation susceptibility (r = 0.25, p = 0.002) in a sample of young adults [20].

A study in *Scientific Reports* (2025) investigated the development of media truth discernment from adolescence to adulthood with 432 adolescents aged 11–14 and 132 adults. Results showed that media truth discernment improved linearly with age, first emerging at age 12. The age-related improvement was partially mediated by CRT performance, especially for detecting fake news (not real news) [1].

**Greene et al. (2024)** — *PNAS* meta-analysis — Found that older age is associated with better discrimination ability and a false-news bias (caution) [13].

### 6.3 Education Level

**Cosgrove et al. (2026)** — *Personality and Individual Differences* — "When education fails: Narcissism, uniqueness, and need for cognitive closure." This study found that positive slopes increase with each education level, indicating the relationship between need for closure and conspiracy beliefs increases as education does. Similarly, in Study Two, narcissism, and less consistently need for cognitive closure, offset the negative effects of education on generic conspiracy beliefs [29][32].

**Greene et al. (2024)** — *PNAS* meta-analysis — Found that education showed no significant effect on discrimination ability between true and false news, but did show a small true-news bias [13].

### 6.4 Political Ideology and Authoritarianism

**Jost, Glaser, Sulloway, & Kruglanski (2003)** — *Psychological Bulletin* meta-analysis — Found that high need for order/structure/closure is a significant predictor of political conservatism (r = .26) [43][44][45][50][52][55].

**De keersmaecker et al. (2017)** — *Social Cognition* — Found that NFC positively predicted right-wing authoritarianism (RWA) only when perceived threat was low [46].

**Berggren, Akrami, Bergh, & Ekehammar (2019)** — *Journal of Individual Differences* — Found that the link between NFC and authoritarianism is fully captured by closed-mindedness, not broader cognitive styles [41][53].

## Part 7: Facet-Specific Findings

The five subscales of the NFCS show differential relationships with misinformation acceptance:

**Staszak et al. (2022)** found that belief in COVID-19 conspiracy theories fully mediated the relationship between the fear of the coronavirus and avoidance of ambiguity, as well as closed-mindedness. A partial mediation between the fear of the coronavirus and the need for predictability was also found [1][11][55].

**Cosgrove (2026)** found that positive slopes increase with each education level, indicating the relationship between need for closure and conspiracy beliefs increases as education does. This suggests that the decisiveness and closed-mindedness facets may interact with education in important ways [29].

**Umam, Muluk, and Milla (2017)** found that NFC predicts belief in conspiracy theories only for the "control of information" conspiracy theme, suggesting that certain facets of NFC (perhaps closed-mindedness and discomfort with ambiguity) are more relevant to specific conspiracy content than others [24][58].

**The Predicting Misinformation Beliefs Across Four Countries study** tested closed-mindedness, need for predictability, and need for order as individual difference factors. It found that social trust and perceived area unsafety were consistently linked to higher misinformation susceptibility across countries; narcissism and conspiracy mentality predicted susceptibility in three of four countries; relative deprivation and external locus of control were significant in the pooled sample [59].

**The Gendi et al. (2023) meta-analysis** demonstrated that the ambiguity, predictability, and decisiveness subscales showed negative meta-analytic correlations with the ability to achieve closure (average effect sizes ranged from r = -0.38 to r = -0.26), and the closed-mindedness subscale showed a much smaller negative correlation (mean effect size estimate r = -0.06). The need for order showed either no significant relationship or a weak positive relationship depending on analysis [34].

**Kossowska (2002)** suggested that the decisiveness facet of NFCS is related to the seizing on notions affording immediate closure, whereas the remaining cluster—including preference for order, preference for predictability, discomfort with ambiguity, and closed-mindedness—is related to the freezing tendency [45]. However, Roets, Van Hiel, and Cornelis (2006) found that the two dimensions did not map differentially onto seizing and freezing as cleanly as this proposal suggests [57].

**Van Hiel (2006)** found that the two-factor structure of the NFCS does not map cleanly onto seizing and freezing, and that Decisiveness may reflect cognitive ability rather than pure motivational need, complicating its interpretation [43][57].

**Sollár and Vanečková (2012)** found that the NFCS subscale Discomfort with ambiguity was specifically associated with information-seeking behavior in threatening situations, while other NFCS subscales were not [39].

## Part 8: Bullshit Receptivity and Related Constructs

**Pennycook, Cheyne, Barr, Koehler, and Fugelsang (2015)** — "On the Reception and Detection of Pseudo-profound Bullshit" (*Judgment and Decision Making*) — Across four studies, the authors found that: (1) a majority of participants rated bullshit statements as at least somewhat profound; (2) higher BSR was strongly associated with lower analytic thinking (heuristics & biases, CRT, cognitive ability), higher intuitive cognitive style (Faith in Intuition), higher ontological confusions, and stronger religious and paranormal beliefs; (3) these associations were specific to bullshit—profundity judgments for conventionally profound or mundane statements did not show the same pattern; (4) a "bullshit sensitivity" measure (difference between ratings for profound quotes and bullshit) was positively correlated with analytic thinking [52].

**Pennycook and Rand (2020)** — "Who Falls for Fake News? The Roles of Bullshit Receptivity, Overclaiming, Familiarity, and Analytic Thinking" (*Journal of Personality*) — Using three online surveys with 1,606 participants, the authors found that individuals who are more receptive to pseudo-profound bullshit and those who overclaim their knowledge are more likely to judge fake news as accurate. Additionally, analytic thinking is negatively associated with perceived fake news accuracy. The authors conclude that belief in fake news is partly driven by a general tendency toward "reflexive open-mindedness"—an overly accepting stance toward weak claims [44][57].

**Littrell (2026)** — "Corporate Bullshit Receptivity" (*Personality & Individual Differences*, March 20, 2026) — Found that individuals who are more receptive to vague, jargon-filled corporate language (termed 'corporate bullshit') tend to have weaker leadership and decision-making skills. Higher CBSR scores were associated with lower analytic thinking, lower fluid intelligence, and worse performance on workplace decision-making tasks [36][37].

**Littrell (2021)** — "The Psychology of Bullshitting" — This PhD thesis from the University of Waterloo presented nine studies on the psychology of bullshitting. The Bullshitting Frequency Scale (BSF) was developed measuring two factors: persuasive bullshitting and evasive bullshitting. Persuasive bullshitting positively predicted susceptibility to various types of bullshit (e.g., pseudo-profound statements, fake news), even after controlling for cognitive ability and analytic cognitive style [38].

## Part 9: Conclusion and Implications

The relationship between Need for Closure and misinformation acceptance is complex, context-dependent, and moderated by numerous factors. The evidence from meta-analyses and original studies reveals several key conclusions:

1. **Direct effects are small but robust**: Meta-analyses show that NFC has a small but significant positive association with conspiracy beliefs and misinformation susceptibility (β ≈ 0.08, r ≈ 0.14 for epistemic motives). However, NFC is not the strongest predictor—other factors like political trust, social identity motives, and threat perception often have larger effects.

2. **Context is critical**: The effect of NFC on misinformation acceptance is highly dependent on situational factors. NFC increases conspiracy beliefs primarily when conspiratorial explanations are salient and event uncertainty is high. When official explanations are available and credible, high NFC individuals may actually be less likely to endorse conspiracy theories, as they "freeze" on the available authoritative explanation.

3. **Mediating mechanisms are well-established**: NFC leads to misinformation acceptance through multiple pathways, including reduced analytic thinking, increased heuristic processing, reliance on epistemic authority, retrieval-induced forgetting, and motivated reasoning through conspiracy mentality.

4. **Numerous moderators shape the relationship**: Key moderators include cognitive reflection (CRT), need for cognition, cognitive ability and education, time pressure, mood and emotion, threat salience, source credibility, message characteristics (repetition, fluency), and social media context (echo chambers, algorithmic curation).

5. **Contextual domains show distinct patterns**: In political contexts, NFC interacts with political ideology and trust in institutions. In health contexts, NFC operates through fear and conspiracy beliefs. In science contexts, NFC contributes to rejection of scientific consensus, but its effects are often mediated by other factors like political ideology and trust in science.

6. **Population differences are important**: Cross-cultural research shows that NFC amplifies culturally normative responses. Age, education, and political ideology all moderate the relationship between NFC and misinformation acceptance.

7. **Facet-specific effects vary**: Different subscales of the NFCS show differential relationships with misinformation acceptance. Closed-mindedness and discomfort with ambiguity appear most consistently linked to conspiracy beliefs, while decisiveness may reflect cognitive ability rather than pure motivational need.

8. **Interventions should target multiple pathways**: Effective interventions to reduce misinformation acceptance should not only address NFC directly but also target the mediating mechanisms (e.g., promoting analytic thinking, building trust in institutions, reducing uncertainty) and moderating factors (e.g., reducing time pressure, enhancing source credibility, using prebunking strategies).

A comprehensive understanding of the role of NFC in misinformation acceptance requires moving beyond simple main effects to consider the complex interplay of dispositional, situational, and contextual factors that shape when and how epistemic needs translate into belief in false information.
