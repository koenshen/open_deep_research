# Comprehensive Research Synthesis: Gut Microbiota, Probiotics, Prebiotics, Pathogens, and Dietary Strategies for Colorectal Health

## 1. Predominant Types of Gut Probiotics: Strains and Mechanisms of Action

### 1.1 Lacticaseibacillus rhamnosus GG (LGG)

Lacticaseibacillus rhamnosus GG is the most extensively clinically documented probiotic strain globally, with over 1,000 PubMed publications. It was isolated in the early 1980s from the human intestinal microbiota and has GRAS (FDA) and QPS (EFSA) regulatory status [1,2].

**Key Mechanisms of Action:**

- **Secreted Proteins (p40, p75, HM0539):** LGG produces secreted effector molecules that activate EGFR via ADAM17/HB-EGF, leading to PI3K/Akt signaling. This reduces apoptosis, preserves tight junctions, enhances mucin production, and promotes IgA secretion and Treg cell differentiation. Early-life p40 exposure causes epigenetic reprogramming via Setd1b-mediated H3K4 methylation, conferring long-term protection. HM0539 protects barrier integrity and attenuates TLR4/NF-κB inflammation [1].

- **SpaCBA Pili:** These unique pili mediate adhesion to mucus and intestinal epithelial cells, modulate dendritic cell cytokine responses, and competitively exclude pathogens. LGG persists up to 1 week in adults and 2 weeks in infants due to these pili [1,2].

- **Tryptophan Metabolism and Methylnicotinamide (MNA) Production:** LGG stimulates dietary tryptophan-dependent production of methylnicotinamide, a vitamin B3 degradation product that protects the gut barrier against colitis. MNA enhances transepithelial electrical resistance, reduces permeability, and promotes colonic healing in DSS colitis models. Clinical data from IBD patients show that reduced serum MNA correlates with higher disease activity [1].

- **Extracellular Vesicles (EVs):** LGG EVs carry p40/p75, tryptophan metabolites, and other cargo, recapitulating barrier protection, anti-inflammatory effects, and AhR/Nrf2 pathway activation [1].

- **Interaction with the Enteric Nervous System:** LGG releases formylated peptides that interact with enteric neurons via the FPR1 receptor, triggering reactive oxygen species production and MAPK1 phosphorylation, leading to remodeling of the enteric nervous system and increased cholinergic neurons [1].

**Clinical Evidence:** LGG significantly reduces antibiotic-associated diarrhea risk (RR=0.49, NNT=9) at doses ≥2×10⁹ CFU. It is effective for diarrhea-predominant IBS, improves chemotherapy-induced diarrhea in colorectal cancer patients, and enhances tolerability of Helicobacter pylori eradication. A randomized trial showed that LGG supplementation (10 billion CFU daily for 3 months) improved cognitive performance in middle-aged and older adults with cognitive impairment [1,2,3].

### 1.2 Bifidobacterium longum BB536

Bifidobacterium longum subsp. longum BB536 is a commercialized probiotic strain that modulates luminal metabolism, stabilizes gut microbiota, and drives homeostatic balance within the host-microbiome interaction [4,5].

**Key Mechanisms of Action:**

- **Immune Modulation:** BB536 activates peripheral plasmacytoid dendritic cells (pDCs) and modulates adaptive and innate immunity via intracellular immune receptors including TLRs and NLRs. Bifidobacterium species produce anti-inflammatory mediators, upregulate regulatory T cells (Tregs), improve intestinal barrier function, and dampen Th2/Th17 programs [4,5,6].

- **Exopolysaccharide (EPS) Signaling:** Surface-associated EPS prevents expansion of pro-inflammatory Th17 responses. EPS signaling via TLR2 mediates anti-inflammatory effects [6].

- **Cross-Feeding for SCFA Production:** Bifidobacteria break down human milk oligosaccharides and mucins, producing acetate and lactate, which feed cross-feeding to butyrate producers, thereby promoting anti-inflammatory effects and Treg cell differentiation [6].

- **Prevention of Pathogen Adhesion:** The combination of BB536 and L. rhamnosus HN001 shows inhibitory activity against Gram-negative bacteria and reduces their adhesion properties [4].

**Clinical Evidence:** BB536 supports digestive regularity, reduces abdominal fat, and aids immune resilience in children. In children with IBS, a mixture including BB536 significantly reduces abdominal pain and improves quality of life [4,5].

### 1.3 Saccharomyces boulardii CNCM I-745

Saccharomyces boulardii is a non-pathogenic probiotic yeast, the only yeast subjected to double-blind studies. It is a homozygous diploid non-sporulated yeast with specific genome features, including chromosome IX trisomy [7,8].

**Key Mechanisms of Action:**

- **Anti-Toxin Action:** Produces a 54-kDa serine protease and a 120-kDa phosphatase that degrade and destroy harmful toxins, including C. difficile toxin A and B, cholera toxin, and E. coli endotoxin [7,8].

- **Anti-Microbial Action:** Prevents harmful bacteria from invading intestinal epithelia by causing bacteria to adhere to its cell walls. Unlike other probiotics, S. boulardii does not colonize the intestines, so harmful bacteria are flushed out. It is the only probiotic proven resistant to all 16 most common oral antibiotics [7,8].

- **Immune Stimulation:** Stimulates production of IgA, the body's most common antibody that protects against bacterial and viral infections. Its immune support is dual-phase: during onset, it activates a general immune response by modulating cytokines and stimulating sIgA release; during resolution, it downregulates pro-inflammatory cytokines and enhances anti-inflammatory mediators [7,8].

- **Trophic Actions:** Increases polyamine levels promoting enterocyte maturation, and its enzymatic activity aids immature enterocytes with increased enzymes to break down food and bacterial toxins [7,8].

**Clinical Evidence:** Meta-analyses show S. boulardii reduces antibiotic-associated diarrhea incidence from 18.7% to 8.5% (RR 0.47). It reduces C. difficile-associated diarrhea risk and recurrence, and shows promise in ulcerative colitis and IBS. Safety note: risk of Saccharomyces fungemia in immunocompromised or critically ill patients with indwelling catheters [7,8].

### 1.4 Streptococcus thermophilus

Streptococcus thermophilus is a Gram-positive, thermophilic lactic acid bacterium with GRAS (FDA) and QPS (EFSA) regulatory status. It is commonly found in yogurt and cheese production [9].

**Key Mechanisms of Action:**

- **Lactose Digestion:** Reduces lactose intolerance symptoms by producing beta-galactosidase [9].

- **Exopolysaccharide (EPS) Production:** EPS stimulates mucus production and supports goblet cell function, critical for maintaining the protective mucus layer. EPS supports intestinal barrier integrity via tight junction proteins [9].

- **Immune Modulation:** Exposure to S. thermophilus produces measurable changes in gene expression across multiple immune pathways, including anti-inflammatory gene expression changes and postbiotic activity via endocannabinoid system modulation [9].

### 1.5 Enterococcus faecium SF68 (Now Enterococcus lactis SF68)

Based on phylogenomic and ANI analyses, SF68 is reclassified as Enterococcus lactis. It was isolated in 1968 from a healthy breast-fed newborn and has been used as a pharmaceutical probiotic since 1979 [10,11].

**Key Mechanisms of Action:**

- **Bacteriocin Production:** Genomic analysis revealed a bacteriocin gene cluster (encoding EntK1, LsbA, and a putative immunity peptide) on plasmid pF9. SF68 inhibits growth of specific E. faecium and Listeria monocytogenes strains [10].

- **Arginine Deiminase Activity:** Responsible for inhibition of host cell NF-κB and JNK(AP-1) pathway activation, alleviating symptoms of intestinal inflammation [10,11].

- **Competitive Exclusion:** Competes with pathogens for adhesion sites and produces folate [10].

**Safety:** Over 1.5 billion doses distributed since 1979. SF68 lacks virulence factors associated with pathogenicity (aggregation substance, haemolysin, gelatinase, hyaluronidase). It is sensitive to ampicillin, vancomycin, and other clinically important antibiotics [10,11].

### 1.6 Short-Chain Fatty Acids (SCFAs): Central Mechanisms of Probiotic Action

SCFAs are the main metabolites produced by bacterial fermentation of dietary fiber, with acetate, propionate, and butyrate in a molar ratio of approximately 60:20:20. Approximately 500-600 mmol of SCFAs are produced per day in the colon [12,13].

**Key Signaling Mechanisms:**

- **G Protein-Coupled Receptor (GPCR) Activation:** SCFAs activate GPR41 (FFAR3), GPR43 (FFAR2), and GPR109A, influencing energy homeostasis, appetite regulation, and immune function [12,13].

- **Histone Deacetylase (HDAC) Inhibition:** Butyrate emerges as the most potent HDAC inhibitor among the three major SCFAs. A 2025 Nature Metabolism study demonstrated that propionate and butyrate directly modify chromatin to regulate gene expression, being incorporated into histones as propionyl-CoA and butyryl-CoA, leading to lysine propionylation and butyrylation on H3K18 and H4K12 [13].

- **Butyrate Paradox:** Butyrate induces apoptosis in neoplastic colonocytes while supporting normal colonocyte proliferation. Cancer cells exhibit the Warburg effect (aerobic glycolysis), making them unable to metabolize butyrate, which accumulates in the nucleus and acts as an HDAC inhibitor, promoting apoptosis and cell cycle arrest [12,13].

- **Immune Cell Modulation:** SCFAs enhance anti-bacterial activity of macrophages, suppress pro-inflammatory mediators, regulate dendritic cell development, promote regulatory T-cell differentiation, and induce NETosis in neutrophils [12,13].

### 1.7 Bacteriocins: Probiotic-Produced Antimicrobial Peptides

Bacteriocins are cationic peptides that kill target cells by pore formation and dissipation of cytosolic contents. Being ribosomally synthesized proteins, they are degraded by proteolytic enzymes, and pathogens are not able to develop resistance in the gut [14].

**Key Bacteriocins:**

- **Nisin:** Produced by Lactococcus lactis, has GRAS status. Effective against Streptococcus pneumoniae and shows anticancer properties (induces apoptosis in head and neck squamous cell carcinoma cells). Nisin can cross epithelial and endothelial monolayers without changing integrity [14].

- **Plantaricin:** A Class IIb bacteriocin that modulates gut microbiota. Plantaricin 423 can cross epithelial and endothelial monolayers [14].

- **Thuricin CD:** Targets Clostridium difficile without affecting commensals [14].

- **Bacteriocin 21:** From Enterococcus faecalis, clears vancomycin-resistant enterococci [14].

**Specific Probiotic Strains and Their Bacteriocins:**
- Lactobacillus salivarius UCC118 produces Abp118, protecting against Listeria monocytogenes
- Lactobacillus acidophilus NCFM and LA-5 produce lactacin B
- Streptococcus salivarius K12 and M18 produce salivaricin A2/B
- Enterococcus faecium SF68 contains bacteriocin gene cluster encoding EntK1, LsbA [10,14]

## 2. Prebiotics: Definition, Chemical Nature, and Mechanistic Pathways

### 2.1 Official ISAPP Definition

The International Scientific Association for Probiotics and Prebiotics (ISAPP) convened an expert panel in 2016, publishing a consensus statement in Nature Reviews Gastroenterology & Hepatology in 2017 (Gibson et al., 2017, 14(8):491-502). The updated definition of a prebiotic is: **"a substrate that is selectively utilized by host microorganisms conferring a health benefit."** [15,16]

This definition expands the concept to include non-carbohydrate substances, applications to body sites other than the gastrointestinal tract, and diverse categories other than food. The requirement for selective microbiota-mediated mechanisms was retained, and beneficial health effects must be documented [15,16].

**Key Criteria for a Prebiotic:**
- Resistance to stomach pH and intestinal enzymes
- Should not be absorbed in the gastrointestinal tract
- Must be fermented by intestinal microbiota
- Must stimulate the growth of beneficial bacteria, enhancing host health
- Most prebiotics targeting the gut require an oral dose of >3 g/day to confer a health benefit; typically around 5 g is the target for FOS and GOS [16]

### 2.2 Chemical Nature of Key Prebiotics

**Inulin:** A fructan polysaccharide composed of linear chains of β-(2→1)-linked D-fructose units, typically terminating with a glucose residue. Degree of polymerization (DP) ranges from 10 to 60. Extracted from chicory root, dahlia tubers, and Jerusalem artichokes. In the EU, native chicory inulin has an authorized health claim (≥12 g/day improves bowel function) [17,18].

**Fructooligosaccharides (FOS):** Short-chain fructans with β-(2→1) fructosyl-fructose glycosidic bonds, DP 3-9. Extracted from plants or synthesized enzymatically from sucrose. Naturally found in chicory, artichokes, onions, garlic, leeks, and bananas. Serve as low-calorie sweeteners (30-50% of sucrose sweetness) [17,18].

**Galactooligosaccharides (GOS):** Composed of galactose units linked by β-1,4 bonds, typically with a terminal glucose residue. Produced enzymatically from lactose using β-galactosidase. GOS promote beneficial bacteria and inhibit pathogens, with sweetness of 15-30% of sucrose [17,18].

**Human Milk Oligosaccharides (HMOs):** Complex glycans with over 200 known structures. Contain N-acetyl-glucosamine, fucose, and N-acetyl-neuraminic acid (sialic acid), which are not found in plant-derived prebiotic oligosaccharides. HMOs have prebiotic and immunological benefits, supporting immunity and gut barrier function [17,18].

**Resistant Starch (RS):** Starch that resists digestion in the small intestine and reaches the colon intact. Four types (RS1-RS4): RS1 (physically inaccessible), RS2 (granular), RS3 (retrograded, formed when cooked starch is cooled), RS4 (chemically modified). Historically, humans consumed 30-40 g/day; average intake in the US is only ~4 g/day [17,19].

**Pectin:** A complex heteropolysaccharide composed primarily of galacturonic acid units linked by α-1,4 glycosidic bonds, with varying degrees of methyl esterification. Found in apples, citrus fruits, berries, and carrots [17].

**Beta-Glucans:** Linear polysaccharides of D-glucose monomers linked by β-glycosidic bonds. Cereal β-glucans (from oats and barley) have β-1,3 and β-1,4 linkages; fungal/yeast β-glucans have β-1,3 and β-1,6 linkages. The FDA has approved health claims for β-glucan (≥3 g/day) for coronary heart disease risk reduction [17,20].

**Arabinoxylans:** Hemicellulose polysaccharides composed of a backbone of β-1,4-linked D-xylopyranose units with L-arabinofuranose residues as side chains. Arabinoxylan particularly promotes butyrate production, linked to improved intestinal integrity, anti-inflammatory effects, and anti-carcinogenic activity [17,20].

### 2.3 Mechanistic Pathways

**Selective Fermentation by Beneficial Bacteria:** Prebiotics are non-digestible substrates that selectively enrich beneficial members of the gut microbiota. The gut microbiota, dominated by Firmicutes and Bacteroidetes, possesses extensive carbohydrate-active enzymes (CAZymes) for polysaccharide breakdown. Bacteroidetes are generalists with polysaccharide utilization loci (PUL), while Firmicutes are specialists. A multi-modal activity-based sorting approach has demonstrated that numerous taxa across Firmicutes and Actinobacteria can bind and metabolise inulin, while secondary consumers are indirectly stimulated by cross-feeding on degradation products [21,22].

**Production of Short-Chain Fatty Acids (SCFAs):** The major end-products of prebiotic fermentation are acetate (C2), propionate (C3), and butyrate (C4) in a molar ratio of approximately 60:20:20, along with lactate. SCFAs are concentrated in the colon at 50-150 mM and constitute 90-95% of SCFAs in the colon, providing up to 10% of daily caloric energy [12,13,21].

**Production Pathways:**
- **Acetate:** Produced via acetyl-CoA pathway or Wood-Ljungdahl pathway. Key producers include Prevotella, Bifidobacterium, and Bacteroides species [12,13].
- **Propionate:** Produced via succinate pathway (Bacteroidetes), acrylate pathway, or propanediol pathway [12,13].
- **Butyrate:** Produced via butyryl-CoA:acetate CoA-transferase pathway, primarily by Clostridium clusters including Faecalibacterium prausnitzii, Roseburia species, Eubacterium rectale, and Anaerostipes species [12,13].

**Butyrate as Colonocyte Fuel:** Butyrate provides approximately 70% of colonocyte metabolic fuel while maintaining tight junction protein expression and epithelial barrier function. As mitochondria in colonocytes carry out beta-oxidation using butyrate as primary fuel, they consume oxygen from the colon, keeping it anaerobic. If butyrate production is reduced, colonocytes cannot carry out beta-oxidation, oxygen is not consumed, and the gut becomes more aerobic, allowing pathogenic facultative anaerobes to colonize more effectively [12,13].

**Modulation of Gut pH:** Prebiotic fermentation lowers colonic pH through SCFA and lactic acid production. A lower pH inhibits growth of pathogenic bacteria (e.g., Enterobacteriaceae, Clostridium perfringens) while favoring beneficial bacteria such as Bifidobacterium and Lactobacillus [21].

**Cross-Feeding Interactions:** This is a critical mechanism in the gut ecosystem. Bifidobacterium species produce acetate and lactate from prebiotic fermentation, which are then utilized by butyrate-producing bacteria (e.g., Faecalibacterium prausnitzii, Roseburia species, Eubacterium rectale) to produce butyrate. This explains why approaches combining Bifidobacterium species (acetate producers), Lactobacillus species (lactate producers), and prebiotics (fermentation fuel) create synergistic effects that single-strain approaches cannot match [21,22].

**Impact on the Gut-Brain Axis:** Prebiotics such as GOS, FOS, and inulin influence brain function directly and indirectly by modulating the microbiome, HPA axis, neurotransmitter levels (GABA, serotonin, dopamine), and inflammation. A 3-week GOS prebiotic trial in healthy individuals reduced waking cortisol reactivity and increased vigilance to positive stimuli. A 4-week GOS trial in IBS patients reduced anxiety and improved quality of life [23,24].

## 3. Pathogenic Bacteria in Colorectal Cancer and Intestinal Inflammation: Toxic Metabolites and Disease Progression

### 3.1 Fusobacterium nucleatum

Fusobacterium nucleatum (Fn) is an oral anaerobic Gram-negative bacterium enriched in CRC tissues, associated with poor survival, metastasis, and chemoresistance. Fn abundance increases along the adenoma-carcinoma sequence [25,26].

**FadA Adhesin and E-Cadherin/β-Catenin Signaling:**
The seminal study by Rubinstein et al. (2013, Cell Host & Microbe) demonstrated that Fn adheres to, invades, and induces oncogenic and inflammatory responses through its unique FadA adhesin. FadA binds to E-cadherin on CRC cells, triggering E-cadherin phosphorylation, internalization, and subsequent activation of β-catenin signaling. This stimulates expression of oncogenes (Myc, Cyclin D1), Wnt genes, and inflammatory genes (NF-κB, IL-6, IL-8, IL-18), enhancing CRC cell proliferation. The FadA-binding site on E-cadherin was mapped to an 11-amino-acid region in the EC5 domain. Analysis of human colon tissue revealed a stepwise increase in FadA gene copies from normal individuals to adenomas to adenocarcinomas (up to >100-fold higher) [25,26].

**Fap2 Protein and Gal-GalNAc Binding:**
The Fap2 protein mediates Fn colorectal adenocarcinoma enrichment by binding to tumor-expressed Gal-GalNAc polysaccharides. Fn binding to clinical adenocarcinomas correlates with Gal-GalNAc expression and is reduced upon O-glycanase treatment. Intravenously injected Fn localizes to mouse tumor tissues in a Fap2-dependent manner, suggesting fusobacteria use a hematogenous route to reach colon adenocarcinomas. A 2025 Nature Communications study reported the cryo-EM structure of Fap2 at high resolution, showing that the elongated ~50 nm long extracellular region binds to TIGIT on immune cells via its membrane-distal tip, leading to deactivation of immune cells [25,26,27].

**TLR4/NF-κB Signaling and Inflammatory Cytokines:**
A 2017 Gastroenterology study showed that Fn infection of CRC cells increases proliferation, invasive activity, and xenograft tumor formation. Fn increases expression of miR21 by activating Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4) signaling to MYD88, leading to NF-κB activation. miR21 suppresses the RAS GTPase RASA1, driving carcinogenesis. In mice, miR21 knockout reduced tumor burden and improved survival. In human CRC tissues, high levels of both Fn DNA and miR21 correlated with shorter survival [25,26].

**Clinical Evidence:**
Patients with higher intratumoral Fn have shorter survival times and are more likely to experience recurrence. Fn is associated with MSI-high and CIMP-high molecular subtypes. Fn contributes to chemoresistance (5-FU) via autophagy pathways. Fn detection in feces shows promise for non-invasive CRC screening, especially combined with FIT [25,26].

### 3.2 Enterotoxigenic Bacteroides fragilis (ETBF) and B. fragilis Toxin (BFT)

ETBF produces B. fragilis toxin (BFT), a zinc-dependent metalloprotease encoded by the bft gene. Three BFT isoforms exist (BFT-1, -2, -3), with bft-2 considered most pathogenic [28,29].

**BFT Toxin Receptor Discovery:**
A landmark 2026 study published in Nature identified that BFT must first bind to the host receptor claudin-4 before it can damage the colon lining. The discovery was made through a genomewide CRISPR screen that identified claudin-4 as the top hit. Structural biology confirmed that BFT and claudin-4 form a tight one-to-one complex. The team developed a molecular decoy—a soluble version of claudin-4—that successfully blocked the toxin's effects in mouse models, offering a potential prevention strategy [28].

**E-Cadherin Cleavage and β-Catenin/Wnt Signaling:**
BFT triggers rapid cleavage of intact 120 kDa E-cadherin, yielding 33 kDa and 28 kDa cell-associated fragments. The 80 kDa ectodomain is shed into cell supernatants. The remaining C-terminal fragment is processed by γ-secretase (presenilin-1) into a 28 kDa fragment. E-cadherin cleavage activates β-catenin signaling, which is required for BFT-induced transcriptional activity of NF-κB, p65 nuclear localization, and IL-8 secretion [28,29].

**STAT3 Activation and Th17 Inflammatory Response:**
The seminal study by Wu et al. (2009) demonstrated that ETBF colonization of multiple intestinal neoplasia (Min) mice triggers a robust IL-17-driven Th17 response essential for distal colon tumor formation. This demonstrates a Stat3- and Th17-dependent pathway for colon tumorigenesis. Foxp3+ regulatory T cells (Tregs) initiate IL-17-mediated carcinogenesis by limiting IL-2 availability in the local microenvironment, allowing Th17 development [28,29].

**Clinical Evidence:**
A study of 68 patients and 52 healthy controls found that the bft gene was present in 47% of patients vs. 3.8% of healthy controls (p=0.00). Odds ratios were highest for serrated lesions (OR 31.25) and low-grade dysplasia (OR 25), suggesting ETBF is strongly associated with early-stage lesions. A 2025 study in Cancers found that ETBF DNA was detected in 219 of 226 CRC liver metastasis lesions (96.9%), with higher ETBF levels associated with increased CD68+ macrophages and decreased FOXP3+ regulatory T cells [28,29].

### 3.3 Colibactin-Producing Escherichia coli (pks+ E. coli)

Colibactin is a genotoxin produced by bacteria harboring the pks genomic island, primarily found in certain E. coli strains of the B2 phylogroup. It is a polyketide-peptide hybrid molecule that acts as a DNA alkylating agent [30,31].

**Mechanism of DNA Damage:**
Colibactin causes DNA interstrand cross-links (ICLs) and double-strand breaks (DSBs) in host cells. The genotoxin alkylates DNA on adenine residues, leading to the formation of interstrand cross-links that block DNA replication and transcription. This triggers a DNA damage response (DDR) involving the Fanconi anemia pathway, ATM/ATR checkpoint activation, and repair via homologous recombination and translesion synthesis [30,31].

**Mutational Signature SBS88:**
Chronic exposure to colibactin generates a specific mutational signature known as Single Base Substitution 88 (SBS88), characterized by an enrichment of T>N substitutions at ApTpN trinucleotide contexts. This signature has been identified in human colorectal cancer genomes, providing direct evidence that pks+ E. coli contributes to CRC mutagenesis. SBS88 is found in approximately 5-10% of colorectal cancers [30,31].

**APC Mutations and Chromosomal Instability:**
Colibactin-induced DNA damage preferentially targets the APC tumor suppressor gene, a gatekeeper in colorectal carcinogenesis. The genotoxin causes specific types of mutations in APC, including deletions and frameshifts, that are characteristic of the SBS88 signature. This accelerates the adenoma-carcinoma sequence [30,31].

**Clinical Evidence:**
pks+ E. coli is enriched in the colonic mucosa of patients with familial adenomatous polyposis (FAP) and sporadic colorectal cancer compared to healthy individuals. In a landmark study, 20 of 21 FAP patients (95%) carried pks+ E. coli in their colonic mucosa, suggesting these bacteria drive the early stages of colorectal carcinogenesis. Treatment with antibiotics that target pks+ E. coli reduced tumor burden in APCMin/+ mouse models [30,31].

### 3.4 Hydrogen Sulfide (H₂S)

Hydrogen sulfide is a gasotransmitter produced by sulfate-reducing bacteria (SRB) such as Desulfovibrio and Desulfobacter species through the reduction of sulfate derived from dietary sources and mucin [32].

**Genotoxic Effects:**
H₂S is genotoxic at high concentrations, causing DNA damage through the production of reactive oxygen species (ROS), inhibition of DNA repair enzymes, and direct alkylation of DNA. It induces double-strand breaks and chromosome aberrations in colon epithelial cells [32].

**Inhibition of Butyrate Oxidation:**
A critical mechanism of H₂S in colorectal carcinogenesis is its ability to inhibit butyrate oxidation in colonocytes. H₂S inhibits cytochrome c oxidase in the mitochondrial electron transport chain, blocking the ability of colonocytes to utilize butyrate as their primary energy source. This creates an energy deficiency in colonocytes and leads to compensatory anaerobic glycolysis, generating a microenvironment that favors the growth of pathogenic bacteria [32].

**Promotion of Inflammation:**
H₂S activates NF-κB signaling and promotes the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-1β, IL-6, TNF-α). It also inhibits the protective mucus layer by reducing mucin production and increasing mucus degradation [32].

**Clinical Evidence:**
Elevated levels of H₂S and sulfate-reducing bacteria have been found in the feces of patients with ulcerative colitis and colorectal cancer. The abundance of Desulfovibrio species is significantly higher in CRC patients compared to healthy controls [32].

### 3.5 Secondary Bile Acids: Deoxycholic Acid (DCA) and Lithocholic Acid (LCA)

Secondary bile acids are produced by gut bacteria (primarily Clostridium and Eubacterium species) through 7α-dehydroxylation of primary bile acids (cholic acid and chenodeoxycholic acid). DCA and LCA are the major secondary bile acids in the human colon [33].

**Mechanisms of Carcinogenesis:**
- **DNA Damage:** DCA and LCA induce oxidative DNA damage through the production of reactive oxygen and nitrogen species. They cause single-strand breaks, double-strand breaks, and oxidative base modifications (8-oxo-dG) [33].
- **Wnt/β-Catenin Activation:** DCA activates the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway by promoting β-catenin nuclear translocation, independent of APC mutations. This leads to increased expression of oncogenes (c-Myc, Cyclin D1) [33].
- **NF-κB and Inflammation:** DCA activates NF-κB signaling, promoting the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-6, IL-8, TNF-α) and creating a pro-carcinogenic inflammatory microenvironment [33].
- **Apoptosis Resistance:** Chronic exposure to DCA selects for apoptosis-resistant cells through the activation of the EGFR/MAPK pathway and upregulation of anti-apoptotic proteins (Bcl-2, Mcl-1) [33].
- **Epigenetic Alterations:** DCA and LCA induce DNA methylation changes and histone modifications that alter gene expression patterns, including silencing of tumor suppressor genes [33].

**Clinical Evidence:**
Fecal levels of DCA and the DCA/CA ratio are significantly elevated in patients with colorectal adenomas and carcinomas compared to healthy controls. High-fat diets increase the secretion of primary bile acids, leading to increased production of secondary bile acids. The ratio of secondary to primary bile acids in feces is a risk marker for colorectal cancer [33].

### 3.6 N-Nitroso Compounds (NOCs)

N-nitroso compounds are carcinogens formed endogenously in the colon from the reaction of nitrosating agents (derived from nitrite/nitrate) with amines and amides, a process catalyzed by gut bacteria. Red and processed meat consumption is a major source of NOC precursors [34].

**Mechanisms of Carcinogenesis:**
- **DNA Alkylation:** NOCs are potent alkylating agents that form DNA adducts, including O⁶-methylguanine (O⁶-meG), which leads to G:C→A:T transition mutations (the most common mutation in KRAS and TP53) [34].
- **Oxidative Stress:** NOCs induce oxidative stress, leading to lipid peroxidation and formation of etheno-DNA adducts [34].
- **APC Mutations:** NOC-induced DNA damage preferentially targets the APC gene, accelerating the adenoma-carcinoma sequence [34].

**Clinical Evidence:**
Human intervention studies have shown that a high red meat diet (300-600 g/day) increases fecal NOC levels by 2-3 fold. The increase in NOCs is associated with elevated levels of O⁶-meG adducts in rectal epithelial cells. A high-fiber diet, particularly resistant starch, reduces these adducts by 50-60% [34].

## 4. Evidence-Based Dietary Recommendations for Gut Microbiota Optimization and Colorectal Cancer Risk Reduction

### 4.1 High-Fiber, Plant-Based Dietary Patterns

**Mediterranean Diet:**
A meta-analysis of 26 studies totaling 2,217,404 participants found a significant 16% reduction in colorectal cancer prevalence with adherence to the Mediterranean diet (HR=0.84, 95% CI 0.78-0.91, p<0.01). The protective effect was similar for men and women [35].

A landmark 2020 study published in Gut investigated the effects of a 1-year Mediterranean diet intervention in 612 older adults (aged 65-79) across five European countries. The diet was associated with enrichment of beneficial taxa (Faecalibacterium prausnitzii, Roseburia, Eubacterium) and depletion of harmful taxa (Ruminococcus torques, Collinsella aerofaciens). These microbiome changes were independently linked to reduced frailty, improved cognitive function, and lower inflammatory markers (hsCRP, IL-17). Inferred metabolic profiling suggested increased SCFA production and decreased secondary bile acids [35,36].

**Dietary Index for Gut Microbiota (DI-GM):**
A case-control study (n=350) found that individuals in the highest DI-GM tertile had 68% lower odds of colorectal cancer (OR=0.32, 95% CI 0.19-0.55; P-trend<0.001). Higher DI-GM scores were associated with lower levels of systemic inflammation (CRP, IL-6, TNF-α), better gut barrier integrity (lower zonulin, fecal calprotectin), and reduced frailty [35].

**Practical Recommendations:**
- Consume at least 25-35 g of dietary fiber daily from diverse plant sources
- Include a variety of plant types: the American Gut Project found that the single strongest dietary predictor of microbial diversity was the number of unique plant types consumed per week, not total fiber intake [35,36]
- Prioritize whole foods over supplements: fiber supplements alone do not translate to the same metabolic benefits as whole food matrices (e.g., fiber-enriched bread, pasta) [35]

### 4.2 Fermented Foods

A landmark Stanford School of Medicine study published in Cell (2021) found that a 10-week diet rich in fermented foods (yogurt, kimchi, kombucha, kefir) increased gut microbiome diversity and decreased 19 inflammatory proteins, including interleukin-6, in 36 healthy adults. In contrast, a high-fiber diet did not significantly alter microbiome diversity or reduce those inflammatory markers within the same timeframe [37,38].

**Key Benefits of Fermented Foods:**
- **Protective Food Matrix:** The food matrix buffers stomach acid, protecting live microbes
- **Prebiotic Partnership:** Fibers in fermented foods feed beneficial bacteria
- **Postbiotics:** SCFAs, vitamins, and other bioactive metabolites are biologically active
- **Live Microbes:** Fermented foods provide live microorganisms that can reach the gut [37,38]

**Which Fermented Foods Contain Live Microbes:**
- Yogurt (with live and active cultures)
- Kefir
- Uncooked sauerkraut
- Traditional kimchi
- Kombucha (some brands)

**Fermented foods that DO NOT contain live microbes:** Beer, wine, sourdough bread, chocolate, tempeh (these undergo processing that kills live cultures) [37,38]

**Practical Recommendations:**
- Start with one serving daily of fermented foods
- Look for "live, active cultures" on labels
- Fermented foods are the most effective strategy for general gut microbiome maintenance in healthy people, while probiotic capsules are better for targeted medical treatment [37,38]
- People with certain GI conditions or on specific medications should consult a doctor before increasing intake

### 4.3 Polyphenol-Rich Foods

Polyphenols are poorly absorbed in the small intestine and are metabolized by gut microbiota into bioactive low-molecular-weight phenolic metabolites. These compounds act as prebiotics by selectively enriching beneficial microbes and suppressing pathogens [39,40].

**Specific Polyphenols and Their Mechanisms:**
- **Resveratrol (grapes, red wine, berries):** Enhances gut-barrier integrity by upregulating tight-junction proteins, increases beneficial SCFA-producing bacteria (Allobaculum, Blautia, Roseburia), and inhibits carcinogenesis via NF-κB, MAPK, and SIRT1 activation. Gut-derived metabolites (dihydroresveratrol, lunularin) attenuate tumor proliferation [39].
- **Curcumin (turmeric):** Enhances CD8+ T cell infiltration, enriches SCFA-producing Lactobacillus, and induces ROS-mediated apoptosis. It also inhibits PI3K/AKT signaling [39,40].
- **EGCG (green tea):** Induces apoptosis, cell cycle arrest, and inhibits angiogenesis in colon cancer cells. Modulates STAT3, AKT/mTOR, and Wnt/β-catenin pathways [39,40].
- **Quercetin (onion, apple, black tea):** Inhibits NF-κB, induces mitochondrial apoptosis, and suppresses pathogenic bacteria [39,40].
- **Ellagitannins (red fruits, nuts):** Converted to urolithins by gut microbiota, which activate p21 and caspase-3, inducing apoptosis [39,40].

**Practical Recommendations:**
- Consume a variety of colorful fruits, vegetables, herbs, and spices daily
- Include green tea, turmeric, berries, onions, apples, and extra virgin olive oil
- Aim for at least 5 servings of fruits and vegetables per day
- Pair polyphenol-rich foods with probiotic-rich foods for synergistic effects

### 4.4 Resistant Starch from Legumes, Whole Grains, and Cooked-Then-Cooled Starches

Resistant starch produces more butyrate than other fibers (e.g., twice that of inulin). Historically, humans consumed 35-50 g/day of resistant starch, but this has reduced to only 5-6 g/day with current diets [19,41].

**Key Evidence:**
A 2019 study published in mBio showed that resistant potato starch significantly increased total SCFA concentrations by 32% and butyrate by 29% in healthy young adults. Resistant starch notably increased the relative abundance of Bifidobacteria and the butyrate producer Ruminococcus bromii. Cross-feeding to butyrate producer Eubacterium rectale was observed [19,41].

Butyrylated high-amylose maize starch (HAMSB) is an edible ingredient that delivers about 60% of its esterified butyrate to the colon. In clinical trials, HAMSB (40 g/day) significantly reduced rectal O⁶-methyl-guanine adducts and epithelial proliferation induced by a high red-meat diet. Animal studies showed that HAMSB reduced diet- or AOM-induced colon cancer risk through different mechanisms, including modulating cecal ammonia and activating caspase-dependent apoptosis [19,41].

**Practical Recommendations:**
- Include legumes (beans, lentils, chickpeas) 3-4 times per week
- Cook and then cool potatoes, rice, and pasta to form retrograded starch (RS3)
- Include green bananas and plantains
- Choose whole grains (oats, barley, quinoa) over refined grains
- High-amylose corn starch can be used as a supplement

### 4.5 Foods to Limit or Avoid

**Red and Processed Meat:**
Red and processed meat consumption is strongly associated with increased CRC risk. The World Health Organization classifies processed meat as Group 1 carcinogen and red meat as Group 2A probable carcinogen. Mechanisms include: heme iron catalyzing N-nitroso compound formation, heterocyclic amines and polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons from high-temperature cooking, and promotion of sulfide-producing bacteria [34,42].

**High-Fat, Low-Fiber Diets:**
The Western diet—high in saturated fats, sugars, and red meat, low in fiber—is linked to reduced gut microbiome diversity, lower production of anti-inflammatory SCFAs, and increased levels of TMAO, which is associated with higher risks of IBD, colorectal cancer, and cardiovascular disease [42,43].

**Ultra-Processed Foods:**
Ultra-processed foods promote overeating due to their soft texture and fast eating rates, reducing appetite regulation. They are typically low in fiber and polyphenols and high in refined sugars, unhealthy fats, and additives that may disrupt the gut microbiota [42].

**Artificial Sweeteners and Emulsifiers:**
Some artificial sweeteners and food emulsifiers have been shown to disrupt gut microbiota composition, promote intestinal inflammation, and increase gut permeability in animal models. More human studies are needed to confirm these effects [42].

### 4.6 Specific Nutrients and Supplements

**Omega-3 Fatty Acids:**
A systematic review found that omega-3 supplementation may reduce the risk of colorectal adenoma recurrence. The anti-inflammatory effects of EPA and DHA are mediated through the production of resolvins and protectins, which promote resolution of inflammation. Omega-3s also modulate the gut microbiota by increasing SCFA-producing bacteria [44].

**Vitamin D:**
Vitamin D deficiency is associated with increased CRC risk. A meta-analysis of 17 studies found that higher circulating 25-hydroxyvitamin D levels were associated with a 30% reduction in CRC risk. Vitamin D modulates the gut microbiota by promoting beneficial bacteria and enhancing antimicrobial peptide production. The VITAL trial (a large randomized trial of vitamin D and omega-3s) found that vitamin D supplementation (2000 IU/day) reduced the risk of advanced adenomas in normal-weight individuals [44].

**Zinc and Selenium:**
Zinc is essential for maintaining gut barrier integrity and immune function. Zinc deficiency increases intestinal permeability and promotes inflammation. Selenium is a component of selenoproteins (e.g., glutathione peroxidases) that protect against oxidative stress. Observational studies suggest that higher selenium status is associated with reduced CRC risk, but randomized trials have shown mixed results [44].

### 4.7 Integrated Dietary Strategy

Based on the evidence, the following integrated dietary recommendations can optimize gut microbiota balance, reduce pathogenic risk, and potentially slow colorectal cancer progression:

1. **Adopt a plant-based, high-fiber dietary pattern** (Mediterranean or similar) with at least 30 different plant types per week
2. **Consume 25-35 g of total dietary fiber daily**, including a mix of soluble and insoluble fibers
3. **Include fermented foods daily** (yogurt, kefir, kimchi, sauerkraut) with live and active cultures
4. **Eat polyphenol-rich foods** (berries, green tea, turmeric, onions, apples, extra virgin olive oil) at every meal
5. **Include resistant starch sources** (legumes, cooked-then-cooled potatoes/rice/pasta, green bananas, whole grains)
6. **Limit red meat to ≤500 g/week** (cooked weight) and avoid processed meats
7. **Minimize ultra-processed foods**, refined sugars, and unhealthy fats
8. **Ensure adequate vitamin D status** (through sun exposure, supplementation, or fortified foods)
9. **Include omega-3 fatty acids** from fatty fish (2-3 servings/week) or plant sources (flaxseeds, chia seeds, walnuts)
10. **Maintain a healthy body weight** and regular physical activity, as obesity is a major risk factor for CRC
