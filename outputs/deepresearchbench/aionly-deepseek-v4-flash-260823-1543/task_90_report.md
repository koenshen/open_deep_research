# Liability Allocation in Accidents Involving ADAS-Equipped Vehicles: A Comprehensive Analysis of the Shared Human-Machine Driving Context

## 1. Introduction

Advanced driver-assistance systems (ADAS) have created one of the most consequential fault lines in modern tort law. Vehicles equipped with systems that can simultaneously control steering, braking, and acceleration are no longer experimental, yet the legal frameworks governing accident liability were designed for a world in which a single human driver exercised near-total control over the vehicle. The result is a rapidly developing "responsibility gap": drivers often believe the machine is in charge, manufacturers design systems that require human supervision but market them in ways that blur the distinction, and courts are left to apportion blame between fallible humans and imperfect software.

This analysis integrates the technical architecture of ADAS, existing legal frameworks, and the emerging body of case law to systematically map the boundary of responsibility between driver and system. It concludes with concrete regulatory recommendations for allocating liability in a manner that is both technically realistic and legally workable.

## 2. Technical Principles of ADAS

### 2.1 The SAE J3016 Taxonomy: Levels of Driving Automation

The foundational technical framework for understanding driving automation is SAE J3016, the taxonomy standard that defines six levels of driving automation (0–5) based on the respective roles of the human user, the driving automation system, and other vehicle systems in performing the **dynamic driving task (DDT)** — "all of the real-time operational and tactical functions required to operate a vehicle in on-road traffic" [SAE J3016_201806](https://www.sae.org/standards/j3016_201806-taxonomy-definitions-terms-related-driving-automation-systems-road-motor-vehicles). Key concepts include the **Operational Design Domain (ODD)** (the specific conditions under which a system is designed to function), **Object and Event Detection and Response (OEDR)**, the **DDT fallback** (response to system failures or out-of-ODD conditions), and the **Minimal Risk Condition (MRC)** (a stable, stopped condition that reduces crash risk) [SAE J3016 User Guide](https://users.ece.cmu.edu/~koopman/j3016).

The levels are defined as follows [The 6 Levels of Vehicle Autonomy Explained – Synopsys](https://www.synopsys.com/blogs/chip-design/autonomous-driving-levels.html):

- **Level 0 (No Driving Automation):** The human performs the entire DDT. Systems may issue warnings or momentarily intervene (e.g., automatic emergency braking, lane departure warning) but cannot sustain vehicle control.
- **Level 1 (Driver Assistance):** The system provides sustained support for *either* steering *or* speed control, but not both simultaneously. The driver performs all other DDT functions. Examples include adaptive cruise control and lane keep assist.
- **Level 2 (Partial Driving Automation):** The system controls *both* steering and speed simultaneously (e.g., lane centering plus adaptive cruise control), but the human must continuously monitor the driving environment and remain ready to take over. **Tesla Autopilot and GM Super Cruise are both Level 2 systems.** The driver remains fully responsible — a point of enormous legal significance.
- **Level 3 (Conditional Driving Automation):** The system performs the entire DDT within its ODD, but a human "fallback-ready user" must respond to takeover requests (transition demands) from the system. The driver may take eyes off the road but must remain receptive. Mercedes-Benz Drive Pilot (approved in Germany and the U.S.) and Honda Traffic Jam Pilot are among the few commercialized Level 3 systems.
- **Level 4 (High Driving Automation):** The system performs the entire DDT *and* the fallback within a defined ODD. No human attention is required within the ODD; the vehicle can reach a minimal risk condition on its own. Waymo, Cruise, and Zoox operate Level 4 robotaxis in limited areas.
- **Level 5 (Full Driving Automation):** The system performs the entire DDT and fallback under all conditions on public roads. No such systems exist commercially today.

Three clarifications from the J3016 standard are essential for legal analysis. First, **levels apply to features, not vehicles** — a vehicle may operate at Level 2 with Autopilot engaged and at Level 0 when the driver takes manual control. Second, J3016 is a **terminology standard, not a safety standard**; it describes what could be built, not what should be built, and says nothing about the safety of any particular system [SAE J3016 User Guide](https://users.ece.cmu.edu/~koopman/j3016). Third, the term "Level 2+" and fractional levels are expressly prohibited by section 8.3 of the standard — a prohibition frequently ignored in marketing materials. The standard's author, Philip Koopman, has also cautioned that the "jump from Level 2 to Level 3 is substantial from a technological perspective, but subtle if not negligible from a human perspective," precisely because the human's role changes from "monitor" to "fallback-ready" [Synopsys](https://www.synopsys.com/blogs/chip-design/autonomous-driving-levels.html).

### 2.2 Operational Design Domain (ODD)

The ODD is "the precise description of where and when an automated driving system is meant to work" [What is an Operational Design Domain? – Voxel51](https://voxel51.com/glossary/operational-design-domain-odd). Typical ODD dimensions include roadway type (highway, urban street), geography (defined cities or mapped areas), environmental conditions (weather, lighting), and dynamic conditions (speed ranges, traffic characteristics). The ODD is foundational to liability because a system can only be held responsible for conditions it was designed and validated to handle; operating outside the ODD is, by definition, outside the system's intended envelope.

Mercedes-Benz Drive Pilot's ODD illustrates the concept: it operates only on the Autobahn in heavy traffic, with at least two lanes, no tunnels, speeds up to 60 km/h, and visible lane markings, and it demands driver takeover during heavy rain, snow, heavy fog, adverse traffic conditions, and at construction sites [DLR ODD Presentation](https://elib.dlr.de/203917/1/SafeTRANS2024_ODD_in_AVs.pdf). Tesla Autopilot, by contrast, is designed for controlled-access highways but — as the *Benavides* litigation established — can be engaged on any road with clear lane markings, creating a central design-defect question: should a system be permitted to engage outside its ODD, and if so, who bears responsibility for the consequences?

### 2.3 Sensor Architectures and Sensor Fusion

ADAS perception relies on complementary sensor modalities [ADAS Sensor Fusion – Car ADAS](https://caradas.com/adas-sensor-fusion):

- **Cameras** detect visual features — lane markings, traffic signs, traffic lights, vehicles — but struggle with low light, sun glare, and precise distance estimation.
- **Radar** measures longitudinal distance and velocity reliably regardless of weather, using radio frequencies; short-range radar covers 10–30 m, medium-range 30–80 m, and long-range radar up to 200 m.
- **LiDAR** creates detailed 3D environmental maps using light pulses but can be degraded by weather; it is considered a key enabling sensor for Level 3+ systems.
- **Ultrasonic sensors** handle close-range detection for parking and low-speed maneuvering.

**Sensor fusion** combines these modalities to produce a more accurate, redundant environmental model than any single sensor could achieve. As Bosch describes it, "Sensor data fusion combines the benefits of different sensors and measuring principles in the most effective way possible, thus providing data that individual sensors working independently are unable to generate" [Bosch Sensor Data Fusion](https://www.bosch-mobility.com/en/solutions/sensors/sensor-data-fusion). Fusion architecture involves detection, segmentation, classification, and monitoring stages, with Kalman-filter-family tracking algorithms and deep-learning perception models [Sensor Fusion in ADAS](https://caradas.com/adas-sensor-fusion). Calibration issues matter for liability: repairs involving windshields, bumpers, suspension, or body panels can misalign sensors, potentially exposing repair shops to liability for improperly calibrated ADAS [Hurwitz Fine](https://www.hurwitzfine.com/blog/liability-for-defective-advanced-driver-assistance-systems).

### 2.4 Driver Monitoring Systems (DMS)

Driver monitoring is the linchpin of the shared human-machine driving context. Systems fall into two categories [Driver Monitoring Systems: Trends and Developments](https://www.autonomousvehicleinternational.com/features/feature-driver-monitoring-systems-trends-and-developments.html):

1. **Indirect monitoring** (steering wheel torque/pressure sensors) — used by Tesla. The NTSB concluded after the 2016 Williston, Florida crash that "monitoring steering wheel torque is a poor surrogate for determining driver engagement" (Safety Recommendation H-17-42), and Tesla drivers have notoriously circumvented the system with weighted objects.
2. **Direct monitoring** (camera-based eye gaze and head posture tracking) — used by GM Super Cruise, Ford BlueCruise, and others. AAA testing found camera-based systems alerted drivers after 4–8 seconds in hands-off scenarios, versus 38–79 seconds for indirect systems [Driver Monitoring Systems: Trends and Developments](https://www.autonomousvehicleinternational.com/features/feature-driver-monitoring-systems-trends-and-developments.html).

The empirical evidence on driver behavior is sobering: a 2022 IIHS survey of 600 drivers found 53% of Cadillac Super Cruise users, 42% of Tesla Autopilot users, and 12% of Nissan/Infiniti ProPILOT users treated their vehicles as fully self-driving, engaging in eating, drinking, and texting while the systems were active [IIHS](https://www.iihs.org/research-areas/advanced-driver-assistance). The EU's General Safety Regulation 2 made DMS mandatory for type approvals in Europe from July 2024, but the U.S. has no equivalent mandate — a regulatory gap discussed in Section 6.

### 2.5 Handoff and Takeover Protocols

The transition of control from system to human is the most safety-critical juncture in Level 3 driving. The UNECE Regulation No. 157 on Automated Lane Keeping Systems (ALKS) requires at least 10 seconds' takeover warning time and a Data Storage System for Automated Driving (DSSAD) that records when the system is activated, when transition demands are issued, and when lane changes are initiated [UN R157](https://regulations.ai/regulations/RAI-IO-UNECE-R157-2021). Research suggests that 10 seconds may be insufficient in complex scenarios: a study in *Accident Analysis & Prevention* found that takeover request lead times of 16–30 seconds significantly improved driver situation awareness when exiting freeways, with 16–18 seconds identified as most appropriate [The Effects of Takeover Request Lead Time – ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S000145752200029X).

Takeover performance degrades with visual/manual non-driving-related tasks, fatigue, alcohol, high traffic density, poor road geometry, and excessive trust in the system; multimodal alerts (auditory + visual + tactile) outperform single-modality warnings, and directional haptic signals can produce reaction times under one second [Analysis of Influencing Factors of Level 3 Takeover – MDPI](https://www.mdpi.com/2071-1050/16/19/8345).

### 2.6 System Limitations and Edge Cases

ADAS systems have documented, recurring failure modes that are directly relevant to liability:

- **Stationary object detection failures:** The NTSB's investigation of the January 2018 Culver City crash — a Tesla Model S on Autopilot striking a stationary fire truck — found that the forward collision warning activated only 0.49 seconds before impact and that "the system's detection of stationary objects was delayed because of the cut-out scenario and the system's requirement for radar and camera agreement" [NTSB Highway Accident Brief HAB1907](https://www.ntsb.gov/investigations/AccidentReports/Reports/HAB1907.pdf). Radar-based systems historically filtered stationary objects as noise (to avoid false braking for bridges and signs), meaning some systems may not reliably detect stopped emergency vehicles.
- **Phantom braking:** An analysis of 2.15 million NHTSA safety complaints over 2015–2025 documented a 1,280% surge in phantom braking complaints, with 2022 alone seeing 1,820 complaints. Tesla's phantom braking rate was 17.12% of its total complaints — 11 times higher than Mercedes-Benz, the next closest manufacturer — and the spike correlated with Tesla's transition to camera-only "Tesla Vision" systems [Cardog Phantom Braking Analysis](https://cardog.app/blog/phantom-braking-safety-systems).
- **Edge cases:** Cameras can be blinded by sun, radar confused by metal bridges, and systems can disengage without warning in heavy rain, snow, or with faded lane markings. A 2023 IIHS study found partial automation systems "struggle" after dark, and 40% of drivers misunderstand the functional limits of their braking systems [Clean Fleet Report](https://cleanfleetreport.com/adas-safety-limits-and-driver-responsibility-in-modern-vehicle-accidents) [IIHS](https://www.iihs.org/research-areas/advanced-driver-assistance).

NHTSA's Standing General Order data confirms the scale of the problem: between July 2021 and May 2022, manufacturers reported 367 crashes involving Level 2 ADAS (Tesla: 273; Honda: 90) and 130 crashes involving Level 3–5 ADS [NHTSA Standing General Order 2021-01](https://www.nhtsa.gov/press-releases/nhtsa-orders-crash-reporting-vehicles-equipped-advanced-driver-assistance-systems).

## 3. Existing Legal Frameworks

### 3.1 Tort Law: Negligence, Strict Liability, and Comparative Fault

Traditional tort liability for motor vehicle accidents centers on the driver's duty of care. As vehicles become more automated, "ADAS liability could focus on more than one actor": human drivers, vehicle manufacturers, component suppliers (sensors, cameras, radar, mapping software), repair shops, and potentially government entities responsible for infrastructure [Hurwitz Fine](https://www.hurwitzfine.com/blog/liability-for-defective-advanced-driver-assistance-systems).

**Driver negligence.** Drivers can be held liable for over-reliance on ADAS, ignoring warnings, misuse, distracted driving, or failing to supervise and override systems when required. Plaintiffs' attorneys characterize complete reliance on ADAS as "automation complacency." In most Level 2 systems, "you are still responsible even if your car has ADAS... Over-reliance on the technology could increase a driver's liability" [Lee Injury Law](https://www.leeinjurylaw.com/blog/2025/04/how-advanced-driver-assistance-systems-adas-impact-car-accident-liability).

**Strict liability.** Section 402A of the Restatement (Second) of Torts (1965) imposes strict liability on sellers for products that are defective and unreasonably dangerous, even if the seller exercised "all possible care." This doctrine, adopted in most U.S. jurisdictions, requires proof that (a) the defendant sold the product, (b) the product was defective and unreasonably dangerous when it left the defendant's hands, (c) it reached the user without substantial change, and (d) the defect proximately caused injury [Product Liability Issues in the U.S. – Springer](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-662-48847-8_26).

**Comparative fault.** Most U.S. states divide liability according to each party's percentage of fault. Texas uses proportionate responsibility (recovery barred if claimant's share exceeds 50%); Florida uses pure comparative fault [Reyes Law](https://www.reyeslaw.com/blog/product-liability-claims-self-driving-cars) [Lawlor, White & Murphey](https://www.lwmpersonalinjurylawyers.com/blog/impact-of-advanced-driver-assistance-systems-adas-on-florida-accident-claims). The operational question — whether the driver who ignored warnings or the manufacturer whose system failed bears the greater share — is now being decided case by case, as Section 4 details.

### 3.2 Product Liability Law

The Restatement (Third) of Torts: Products Liability (1998) defines three categories of defects that map directly onto ADAS technology [Restatement Third – Florida Bar Journal](https://www.floridabar.org/the-florida-bar-journal/the-restatement-third-of-torts-products-liability-the-tension-between-product-design-and-product-warnings):

1. **Manufacturing defects** — faulty sensors, cameras, or LiDAR components that fail to meet specifications. The "malfunction doctrine" allows plaintiffs to prove a defect through circumstantial evidence without identifying the specific flaw, which is particularly helpful in complex software cases [Gurney, "Sue My Car Not Me"](https://illinoisjltp.com/file/62/Gurney.pdf).
2. **Design defects** — evaluated through the "reasonable alternative design" and risk-utility balancing tests. In the ADAS context, plaintiffs have argued that a system's failure to detect stationary objects, its allowance of engagement outside its ODD, and inadequate driver monitoring constitute design defects (see *Benavides* in Section 4).
3. **Failure to warn** — inadequate disclosure of system limitations. The Third Restatement's Comment l expresses a clear preference for safer design over warnings: "when a safer design can reasonably be implemented and risks can reasonably be designed out of a product, adoption of the safer design is required over a warning."

The **crashworthiness (enhanced injury) doctrine** — established in *Larsen v. General Motors* (8th Cir. 1968) and codified in Restatement Third § 16 — holds manufacturers liable for injuries exacerbated by defects even when the defect did not cause the initial accident [Enhanced Injuries and the Restatement of Torts Third – FindLaw](https://corporate.findlaw.com/litigation-disputes/enhanced-injuries-and-the-restatement-of-torts-third.html). This doctrine is increasingly relevant as courts confront claims that a defective ADAS or occupant protection system aggravated injuries in a collision that a human driver may have caused.

The Brookings Institution has argued that "products liability law — developed over half a century — provides a time-tested framework well-equipped to handle autonomous vehicle liability questions," and has cautioned against broad new liability statutes protecting AV manufacturers [Brookings – Products Liability and Driverless Cars](https://www.brookings.edu/articles/products-liability-and-driverless-cars-issues-and-guiding-principles-for-legislation). Nonetheless, scholars acknowledge that the shift from "automated AI" (pre-programmed systems like Tesla Autopilot) to "autonomous AI" (machine-learning systems that act on their own judgment) strains traditional concepts of design and causation [Nature, Nurture, or Neither? – George Mason Law Review](https://lawreview.gmu.edu/print__issues/nature-nurture-or-neither-liability-for-automated-and-autonomous-artificial-intelligence-torts-based-on-human-design-and-influences).

### 3.3 Contract Law: Warranties, Disclaimers, and Arbitration Clauses

Purchase agreements for ADAS-equipped vehicles raise significant contract issues. Express and implied warranties under the Uniform Commercial Code (merchantability, fitness for a particular purpose) are subject to disclaimers, privity requirements, and notice rules. Consumer protection statutes (California's UCL, FAL, and CLRA; the Magnuson-Moss Warranty Act) provide additional avenues for economic-loss claims based on allegedly misleading ADAS marketing [In re Tesla ADAS Litigation class certification – Tavrn](https://www.tavrn.ai/blog/tesla-class-action-lawsuit).

Arbitration clauses are perhaps the most consequential contractual feature. In October 2023, U.S. District Judge Haywood Gilliam ruled that Tesla owners who purchased vehicles through the Tesla website must pursue false-advertising claims over Autopilot/FSD in individual arbitration, enforcing Tesla's online order agreement arbitration clause; a fifth plaintiff's claims were dismissed for exceeding the statute of limitations [Reuters – Tesla Owners Must Arbitrate](https://www.reuters.com/legal/tesla-owners-must-arbitrate-false-advertising-claims-over-autopilot-feature-2023-10-02). The Supreme Court's decisions in *AT&T Mobility v. Concepcion* (2011) and *American Express v. Italian Colors* (2013) make class action waivers in arbitration agreements broadly enforceable, which can render small-dollar ADAS consumer claims economically irrational to pursue [Consumer Arbitration – SJKP](https://www.daeryunlaw.com/us/practices/detail/consumer-arbitration). However, arbitration clauses in purchase agreements typically do not bar personal injury suits, particularly those brought by third parties (passengers, other drivers, pedestrians) who are not signatories.

### 3.4 Insurance Frameworks

The empirical evidence on ADAS and insurance claims is mixed. Front crash prevention systems with AEB reduce front-to-rear collision claim frequencies by roughly 50%; Volvo's City Safety reduced property damage liability claims by 15–16% and bodily injury claims by up to 33%, and Mercedes-Benz Distronic Plus reduced PDL claims by 14% [Brookings](https://www.brookings.edu/articles/products-liability-and-driverless-cars-issues-and-guiding-principles-for-legislation) [IIHS-HLDI/NAIC Presentation](https://content.naic.org/sites/default/files/call_materials/Book%20Club%20Pres%20ADAS%206.24.2025.pdf).

Yet IIHS has concluded that **partial automation is best understood as a convenience feature, not a safety technology**: "Everything we're seeing tells us that partial automation is a convenience feature like power windows or heated seats rather than a safety technology," said IIHS President David Harkey, with no significant difference in crash rates between vehicles with and without Level 2 systems [IEEE Spectrum](https://spectrum.ieee.org/partial-vehicle-autonomy-risk). Meanwhile, ADAS increases repair costs dramatically — windshield cameras cost $600–$1,000, front radar about $1,200, and bumper repairs with sensors can exceed $2,000, with an AXA study finding ADAS-equipped vehicles can cost up to 40% more to insure [Advanced Fleet Management Consulting](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ACBRycUJqOQ).

The most innovative insurance model is the United Kingdom's **Automated and Electric Vehicles Act 2018 (AEVA 2018)**, which creates an insurance-first, victim-compensation scheme: Section 2 makes the insurer directly liable for damage caused by an automated vehicle "when driving itself," with limited exclusions (unauthorized software alterations, failure to install safety-critical updates, contributory negligence) [AEVA 2018 s.2](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2018/18/section/2). Insurers, in turn, can pursue subrogated claims against manufacturers — a structure scholars describe as making insurers "indirect regulators" of automated vehicles [Tort and AV Accidents – York Research Database](https://pure.york.ac.uk/portal/en/publications/tort-and-autonomous-vehicle-accidents-the-automated-and-electric-). For Level 2 ADAS, however, traditional fault-based auto insurance remains the default, and subrogation in ADAS crashes "is no longer a simple question of who had the right-of-way and who failed to yield" but increasingly involves complex product liability litigation [MWL – The Future of Auto Subrogation](https://www.mwl-law.com/the-future-of-auto-subrogation-is-here-pursuing-claims-involving-automated-driving-systems).

### 3.5 Statutory and Regulatory Schemes by Jurisdiction

**United States.** There is no comprehensive federal statute governing ADAS or automated vehicle liability. NHTSA regulates vehicle safety through Federal Motor Vehicle Safety Standards (FMVSS) under a self-certification model. Key developments include:

- **FMVSS No. 127** (finalized May 2024): requires automatic emergency braking, including pedestrian AEB, on all light vehicles by September 2029, with vehicles required to avoid contact with a lead vehicle up to 62 mph and detect pedestrians in daylight and darkness [Federal Register – FMVSS 127](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2024/05/09/2024-09054/federal-motor-vehicle-safety-standards-automatic-emergency-braking-systems-for-light-vehicles).
- **NHTSA Standing General Order 2021-01**: requires manufacturers to report crashes involving Level 2 ADAS and Levels 3–5 ADS within one day for serious crashes and monthly for others [NHTSA SGO](https://www.nhtsa.gov/press-releases/nhtsa-orders-crash-reporting-vehicles-equipped-advanced-driver-assistance-systems).
- **State patchwork**: 34 states have AV statutes, ranging from permissive frameworks (Arizona, Texas, Florida) to restrictive regimes (New York requires a licensed driver physically present). There is no federal preemption of state tort law [Baker Donelson State AV Survey](https://www.bakerdonelson.com/autonomous-vehicle-statutes-and-regulations-across-the-50-states) [NCSL](https://www.ncsl.org/transportation/autonomous-vehicles).

**Germany.** Germany established the first comprehensive legal framework for automated driving through the **2017 amendment to the Road Traffic Act (StVG)**. Section 1a defines vehicles with highly or fully automated driving functions; Section 1b permits drivers to divert attention from traffic while the system is engaged but obliges them to "retake control of the vehicle without undue delay" if prompted or if they realize the conditions for intended use no longer exist [Gasser – StVG Modifications](https://www.connectedautomateddriving.eu/wp-content/uploads/2018/05/Tom-Michael-Gasser_Modifications_Germany.pdf) [Gleiss Lutz](https://www.gleisslutz.com/en/know-how/new-legal-rules-automated-driving). The maximum strict liability limits for vehicle keepers were doubled to €10 million for personal injury and €2 million for property damage, and Section 63a mandates data logging to enable liability traceability. A 2021 follow-up law extended the framework to Level 4 autonomous vehicles operating with a "technical supervisor" [CMS – AV Law in Germany](https://cms.law/en/int/expert-guides/cms-expert-guide-to-autonomous-vehicles-avs/germany).

**United Kingdom.** Beyond AEVA 2018, the **Automated Vehicles Act 2024 (AVA 2024)** creates a comprehensive regulatory regime: vehicles must satisfy a "self-driving test" (safe and legal autonomous travel with no individual monitoring), authorized automated vehicles must have an "authorized self-driving entity" (typically the manufacturer) responsible for how the vehicle drives, transition demands must be perceivable and provide sufficient time, and new marketing offences prohibit confusing consumers into believing an unauthorized vehicle can drive itself [Automated Vehicles Act 2024](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2024/10) [PACTS AV Briefing](https://www.pacts.org.uk/wp-content/uploads/20240529-AV-Briefing-1.pdf).

**European Union.** The EU combines type-approval regulation (General Safety Regulation 2019/2144, Regulation 2022/1426 on ADS type approval, UNECE Regulation 157 on ALKS) with the **revised Product Liability Directive (2024/2853)**, which explicitly extends liability to software, AI systems, and digital services, eases the burden of proof in complex cases, and extends the long-stop period to 25 years for latent damage [Simmons & Simmons – EU AV Legal Framework](https://www.simmons-simmons.com/en/publications/cmneudnyl0056us3gnmd4yeq2/autonomous-vehicles-the-eu-legal-framework) [EU PLD analysis](https://czasopisma.kul.pl/index.php/recl/article/download/19473/17339). The proposed AI Liability Directive, which would have created a rebuttable presumption of causation for AI-caused harm, was officially withdrawn in October 2025, leaving the EU's liability framework fragmented across national systems [AI Liability Directive Status](https://www.ai-liability-directive.com).

## 4. Relevant Case Law

### 4.1 Tesla Autopilot Civil Litigation

Tesla has been the primary litigation battleground for ADAS liability, given its market dominance, its Level 2 system branding ("Autopilot," "Full Self-Driving"), and its permissive ODD. The trajectory of cases reveals a shifting judicial and jury landscape.

**The early years: driver fault and system warnings.** The first fatal Autopilot crash — Joshua Brown's death in Williston, Florida (May 2016) — was investigated by the NTSB, which found that Autopilot "allowed the driver to avoid steering or watching the road for long periods," that Brown had his hands on the wheel for only 25 seconds over a 37-minute Autopilot engagement, and that the system displayed 13 warnings (7 visual, 6 audio). The NTSB cleared the system of malfunction — Brown could have seen the truck for 7 seconds — while criticizing Tesla for permitting use outside the system's design environment [Business Insider – NTSB Autopilot Findings](https://www.businessinsider.com/tesla-autopilot-fatal-crash-ntsb-2017-9). NHTSA closed its investigation finding no safety defect and citing a 40% reduction in Tesla crash rates.

The pattern continued. **Hsu v. Tesla** (2023) — a Los Angeles case involving alleged unintended swerving and airbag deployment — ended in a complete Tesla defense verdict, with jurors concluding that "Tesla clearly warned the software was not a self-piloted system" and that driver distraction was to blame [Reuters – Tesla Wins Bellwether Trial](https://www.reuters.com/legal/us-jury-set-decide-test-case-tesla-autopilot-crash-2023-04-21). **Molander v. Tesla** (October 2023) — the first jury trial over a fatal Autopilot crash — also ended in a Tesla victory, with the jury rejecting claims that a manufacturing defect caused a Model 3 to veer off a freeway; Tesla successfully argued the driver had been drinking and there was no evidence Autopilot was engaged [Bloomberg Law – Tesla Prevails](https://news.bloomberglaw.com/esg/tesla-prevails-in-first-jury-trial-over-fatal-autopilot-crash).

**Settlements before trial.** Tesla settled **Huang v. Tesla** (April 2024) as jury selection began, avoiding public airing of evidence about the 2018 Mountain View crash in which an Apple engineer died after his Model X steered into a highway median at 71 mph. The NTSB had found the probable cause was "the Tesla Autopilot system steering the sport utility vehicle into a highway gore area due to system limitations, and the driver's lack of response due to distraction" — a rare finding of dual causation [CNBC – Tesla Settles Huang](https://www.cnbc.com/2024/04/08/tesla-settles-wrongful-death-lawsuit-over-fatal-2018-autopilot-crash.html) [Minami Tamaki – Huang](https://www.minamitamaki.com/huang). In **Banner v. Tesla** (2025), the Florida Fourth District Court of Appeal reversed the trial court's punitive damages ruling in February 2025 — holding that aspirational statements about Autopilot's future could not support punitives — and Tesla then settled the case in July 2025 [Quinn Emanuel – Appellate Victory](https://www.quinnemanuel.com/the-firm/publications/appellate-victory-on-tesla-s-autopilot-technology).

**The watershed: Benavides v. Tesla (August 2025).** The first U.S. jury verdict holding Tesla liable for Autopilot arose from an April 2019 Key Largo crash: a Tesla Model S with Enhanced Autopilot engaged ran a stop sign at 60 mph, killing 22-year-old Naibel Benavides and injuring Dillon Angulo. The case is significant for several reasons:

- **Summary judgment (June 2025):** In a 70-page opinion, the court denied Tesla's motion on plaintiffs' key defect theories — (1) that Autopilot could be activated on roads outside its ODD (designed only for divided limited-access highways), and (2) that the steering-wheel-torque driver monitoring system was inadequate, given that a longer strikeout waiting period might have prevented the crash. The court dismissed manufacturing defect and negligent misrepresentation claims but allowed design defect claims under both the consumer-expectations and risk-utility tests, and permitted punitive damages [Marquette Law Faculty Blog](https://law.marquette.edu/facultyblog/2025/07/tesla-to-face-jury-trial-over-autopilot-defects-following-70-page-summary-judgment-opinion) [WSHB – Benavides Defense Perspective](https://www.wshblaw.com/publication-benavides-v-tesla-a-defense-side-perspective-on-floridas-landmark-autopilot-verdict).
- **Verdict:** The eight-person Miami federal jury found Tesla 33% responsible and the driver 67% responsible, awarding $129 million in compensatory damages (reduced proportionally to ~$42.5 million against Tesla) and the full $200 million in punitive damages — approximately $243 million total [CNBC – Tesla Must Pay $329 Million](https://www.cnbc.com/2025/08/01/tesla-must-pay-329-million-in-damages-in-fatal-autopilot-case.html) [NPR – Jury Orders Tesla to Pay](https://www.npr.org/2025/08/02/nx-s1-5490930/tesla-autopilot-crash-jury-240-million-florida).
- **Post-trial:** In February 2026, Judge Beth Bloom rejected Tesla's motion to overturn the verdict, writing that "evidence admitted at trial more than supports the jury verdict" [Reuters – Judge Upholds $243M Verdict](https://www.reuters.com/world/us-judge-upholds-243-million-verdict-against-tesla-over-fatal-autopilot-crash-2026-02-20).

The *Benavides* verdict's significance lies in its dual finding: the driver bore the majority of fault (for speeding, phone use, and failure to maintain attention), but Tesla's design choices — permitting ODD-external operation and using an inadequate driver monitoring system — independently contributed to the crash. Plaintiffs' counsel characterized it as holding Tesla accountable for using "public roads as test tracks," while Tesla called the verdict "wrong" and argued "no car in 2019, and none today, would have prevented this crash."

**The post-Benavides wave.** Since the *Benavides* verdict, Tesla has settled at least four additional Autopilot suits, including *Tran v. Tesla* (November 2025, a Model Y crash into a stationary police vehicle) [Electrek – Tesla Settles Fourth Lawsuit](https://electrek.co/2025/11/17/tesla-settles-another-lawsuit-autopilot-crash-4th-losing-trial). A June 2026 suit arising from a fatal Katy, Texas crash — in which a Tesla Model 3 allegedly operating with automated assistance crashed through a home's wall, killing 76-year-old Martha Avila — echoes the *Benavides* claims: design defect (failure to detect the end of the street), inadequate driver monitoring, and failure to warn, with the driver alleged to have told police the assistance system was engaged [Electrek – Tesla Sued Over Katy Crash](https://electrek.co/2026/06/24/tesla-sued-fatal-katy-fsd-crash) [NBC News – Katy Lawsuit](https://www.nbcnews.com/news/us-news/fatal-texas-tesla-automated-driving-assistance-crash-lawsuit-rcna351485).

### 4.2 Criminal Prosecutions

The criminal docket has begun to address whether a driver's reliance on ADAS can excuse or mitigate criminal culpability.

In **People v. Riad** (Los Angeles, 2019 crash), Kevin Riad's Tesla Model S ran a red light at 74 mph with Autopilot engaged, killing two people. Prosecutors filed the first felony charges in the U.S. against a driver using partial automation — two counts of vehicular manslaughter. In June 2023, Riad pleaded no contest and received two years' probation (with a suspended four-year prison sentence), 90 days of house arrest, and 100 hours of community service [Daily Breeze – Riad Sentencing](https://www.dailybreeze.com/2023/06/30/driver-of-tesla-on-autopilot-gets-probation-for-crash-that-killed-2-in-gardena). NYU Law professor Mark Geistfeld observed that a conviction "could really be the most disruptive, the most novel, the most groundbreaking precedent," but noted that criminal charges for such crashes are surprising outside drunk-driving contexts, and that the case blurred the line between complacency and culpable overreliance [NYU News – Who Is Responsible?](https://www.nyu.edu/about/news-publications/news/2022/march/when-a-tesla-on-autopilot-kills-someone--who-is-responsible--.html).

In **State v. Vasquez** (Arizona, 2018), the backup safety driver in an Uber Level 3 test vehicle was charged with negligent homicide after the vehicle struck and killed pedestrian Elaine Herzberg — the first recorded fatal crash involving a self-driving vehicle. The NTSB found Vasquez was visually distracted (streaming TV on her phone for 34% of the trip) and that Uber's inadequate safety culture, deactivated automatic emergency braking, and Arizona's insufficient oversight of AV testing were contributing factors [The Guardian – Vasquez Plea](https://www.theguardian.com/technology/2023/aug/01/uber-self-driving-arizona-deadly-crash). In July 2023, Vasquez pleaded guilty to endangerment and received three years' probation — a middle ground recognizing that the human was at fault but that the corporate system design bore substantial responsibility [NPR – Vasquez Plea](https://www.npr.org/2023/07/28/1190866476/autonomous-uber-backup-driver-pleads-guilty-death).

### 4.3 Cases Involving Other Systems

**GM Super Cruise.** GM's head of innovation Warwick Stirling stated in 2017 that for Super Cruise (Level 2), "the driver is still liable" because "the driver is still in the driver's seat, and they are supposed to be driving"; for Level 4 systems, liability would shift to "a combination of the fleet owner, OEM, and the service provider" [GM Authority – Liability Clarification](https://gmauthority.com/blog/2017/10/gm-clarifies-the-liability-question-with-self-driving-cars). GM has marketed Super Cruise as having logged hundreds of millions of miles without an at-fault crash, but the system's limitations (no steering around objects, no response to traffic controls, reliance on pre-mapped highways) have drawn scrutiny [Joe I. Zaid – Super Cruise Safety](https://joezaid.com/how-safe-is-gms-super-cruise-technology).

**Ford BlueCruise.** A NHTSA investigation (as of 2026) is examining two fatal 2024 crashes involving Ford Mustang Mach-E vehicles operating with BlueCruise active, both striking stationary vehicles on interstates; one driver (Dimple Patel) was charged with DUI homicide [Ethen Ostroff Law – Ford BlueCruise](https://ethenostrofflaw.com/ford-bluecruise-defect-lawsuit). The crashes underscore that hands-free Level 2 systems do not eliminate the driver's obligation to scan for stationary obstacles.

**GM Cruise.** The October 2023 San Francisco pedestrian crash — in which a Cruise robotaxi dragged a pedestrian 20 feet after she was struck by another vehicle — produced a $1.5 million NHTSA fine for failing to disclose the dragging, a $500,000 criminal fine for making false statements to federal investigators, and a securities class action alleging GM misrepresented Cruise's true autonomy level [Reuters – Cruise Fine](https://www.reuters.com/business/autos-transportation/gm-self-driving-unit-cruise-pay-15-million-fine-over-crash-disclosure-2024-09-30) [Labaton – In re GM Securities](https://www.labaton.com/cases/in-re-general-motors-company-securities-litigation). The case demonstrates that ADS operators — not just Level 2 drivers — face regulatory and criminal exposure for misleading safety reporting.

### 4.4 NHTSA Investigations and Recalls

NHTSA's enforcement actions have effectively created a de facto regulatory standard for ADAS design and disclosure.

**Recall 23V-838 (December 2023):** NHTSA compelled Tesla to recall approximately 2,031,220 vehicles (all Model S/X/3/Y equipped with Autosteer) after a nearly three-year investigation analyzing 956 crashes found that "Tesla's weak driver engagement system was not appropriate for Autopilot's permissive operating capabilities" and that there was a "critical safety gap between drivers' expectations of the system's operating capabilities and the system's true capabilities" [NBC News – Tesla Recall](https://www.nbcnews.com/news/us-news/tesla-issues-massive-recall-2-million-vehicles-autopilot-safety-concer-rcna129486) [NHTSA Part 573 – 23V-838](https://static.nhtsa.gov/odi/rcl/2023/RCLRPT-23V838-8276.PDF). The remedy added a five-strike penalty system that suspends Autopilot for drivers who repeatedly ignore warnings. NHTSA subsequently opened an investigation into whether the remedy was adequate after 20 post-remedy crashes [USA Today – NHTSA Recall Probe](https://www.usatoday.com/story/money/cars/recalls/2024/04/26/nhtsa-tesla-self-driving-recall-investigation/73473322007).

**Recall 23V-085 (February 2023):** Tesla recalled 362,758 vehicles with FSD Beta software that NHTSA found could exceed speed limits or traverse intersections in unlawful or unpredictable ways — including traveling straight through turn-only lanes and proceeding into intersections on stale yellow lights [PBS – FSD Recall](https://www.pbs.org/newshour/nation/regulators-force-tesla-to-recall-363000-full-self-driving-vehicles).

**California DMV false advertising ruling (December 2025):** An administrative law judge found Tesla's "Autopilot" and "Full Self-Driving" marketing deceptive, concluding that "a reasonable consumer likely would believe that a vehicle with Full Self-Driving Capability can travel safely without a human driver's constant, undivided attention. This belief is wrong — both as a technological matter and as a legal matter." The ruling ordered a 30-day suspension of Tesla's California sales license (stayed pending compliance), and Tesla subsequently renamed the product "Full Self-Driving (Supervised)" [CNBC – DMV Ruling](https://www.cnbc.com/2025/12/16/california-judge-says-tesla-engaged-in-deceptive-autopilot-marketing-.html) [Electrek – Tesla Sues DMV](https://electrek.co/2026/02/23/tesla-sues-california-dmv-reverse-fsd-false-advertising-ruling).

## 5. The Boundary of Responsibility Between Driver and System

### 5.1 Allocating Responsibility by Automation Level

The SAE taxonomy provides the starting point for responsibility allocation, and courts and regulators have largely adopted its logic:

- **Level 0–1:** The human is the "driver" in every meaningful sense; the system provides momentary assistance. Driver liability is essentially traditional.
- **Level 2:** The system controls both steering and speed, but the human must continuously monitor and be ready to intervene. NHTSA's Standing General Order describes Level 2 ADAS as providing "driver assist functions... where drivers must remain engaged and alert at all times" [NHTSA SGO](https://www.nhtsa.gov/press-releases/nhtsa-orders-crash-reporting-vehicles-equipped-advanced-driver-assistance-systems). Most courts have held drivers liable, but *Benavides* has established that the manufacturer's design choices (ODD enforcement, DMS adequacy) can create *shared* liability even at Level 2.
- **Level 3:** When the ADS is engaged within its ODD, the *system* is performing the DDT, and the manufacturer should bear responsibility for system-caused crashes; the *driver* bears responsibility for failing to respond to transition demands or engaging the system outside its ODD or under inappropriate conditions. The German StVG explicitly imposes a duty to retake control "without undue delay" [Gasser – StVG](https://www.connectedautomateddriving.eu/wp-content/uploads/2018/05/Tom-Michael-Gasser_Modifications_Germany.pdf). The critical unresolved question is what constitutes a "reasonable" takeover time and how to define the circumstances in which a driver "must realize" the system can no longer operate.
- **Level 4–5:** The manufacturer/ADS operator bears primary responsibility for system operation within the ODD. The UK's AEVA 2018 insurance model and Germany's technical supervisor framework both recognize that the human occupant is no longer a "driver" in the traditional sense [AEVA 2018 s.2](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2018/18/section/2).

### 5.2 Driver Duties: Supervision, Intervention, and ODD Compliance

The case law has articulated a consistent set of driver duties in Level 2 operation: (1) keep hands on the wheel where required (Tesla's torque-based system), (2) maintain visual attention on the road, (3) respond to warnings and takeover requests, and (4) do not use the system outside its intended ODD. The *Hsu* and *Molander* defense verdicts demonstrate that drivers who ignore clear warnings or engage systems in inappropriate conditions bear the consequences. The *Riad* and *Vasquez* prosecutions establish that criminal liability can attach when driver inattention rises to gross negligence.

But the driver-duty framework faces a fundamental human factors problem: the IIHS survey showing 42–53% of Tesla and Cadillac users treat Level 2 systems as fully self-driving, and the NTSB's finding that "monitoring steering wheel torque is a poor surrogate for determining driver engagement" [Driver Monitoring Trends](https://www.autonomousvehicleinternational.com/features/feature-driver-monitoring-systems-trends-and-developments.html). Humans are "very poor at periodically observing a process which is largely automated," as AAA's Matthew Lum has noted [Clean Fleet Report](https://cleanfleetreport.com/adas-safety-limits-and-driver-responsibility-in-modern-vehicle-accidents). A responsibility regime that blames drivers for predictable human limitations — while manufacturers design systems that make sustained attention impossible and market them as "self-driving" — is both unjust and practically unworkable.

### 5.3 Manufacturer Duties: Design, Warning, and Marketing

The emerging manufacturer-side duties include:

1. **ODD enforcement:** A system that can operate outside its ODD effectively delegates a design decision to the human at the exact moment the human is least equipped to make it. The *Benavides* court's rejection of Tesla's summary judgment on this theory signals that courts view ODD enforcement as a design responsibility, not merely a warning responsibility. Technical mechanisms exist — geofencing (GM Super Cruise), map-based restrictions — and their absence can constitute a design defect [Marquette Law Blog](https://law.marquette.edu/facultyblog/2025/07/tesla-to-face-jury-trial-over-autopilot-defects-following-70-page-summary-judgment-opinion).
2. **Adequate driver monitoring:** The NTSB's H-17-42 recommendation (camera-based engagement sensing) and the contrast between Super Cruise's infrared eye-tracking (4–8 second alerts) and Tesla's torque sensing (38–79 second alerts) define the technical state of the art [Driver Monitoring Trends](https://www.autonomousvehicleinternational.com/features/feature-driver-monitoring-systems-trends-and-developments.html).
3. **Truthful marketing:** The California DMV's finding that "Full Self-Driving" is "actually, unambiguously false and counterfactual" and the *Benavides* court's admission of Tesla marketing as evidence of conscious disregard mark a decisive shift. Marketing claims shape consumer expectations, which in turn define the standard of care under the consumer expectation test [California Defective Vehicle Law](https://www.1000attorneys.com/post/california-defective-vehicle-law).
4. **Post-sale duties:** Software update management, recall effectiveness, and data preservation. Tesla's alleged attempts to hide or erase crash data in the *Benavides* case, and Cruise's failure to disclose the pedestrian dragging, have generated separate enforcement actions and negative inferences [Telemetry – Tesla Data and Regulatory Inaction](https://www.telemetryagency.com/post/a-tesla-crash-missing-data-and-the-cost-of-regulatory-inaction).

### 5.4 The Ethical Dimension

The "responsibility gap" identified by NHTSA is fundamentally an ethical problem about the allocation of risk between actors with vastly different capabilities. Drivers cannot evaluate the reliability of perception algorithms or the validity of sensor-fusion models; manufacturers cannot predict every edge case but can design conservatively (geofencing, redundant sensing, camera-based DMS). The law's traditional answer — comparative fault — requires juries to weigh a driver's momentary inattention against a manufacturer's deliberate design choices made over years. The *Benavides* apportionment (33% manufacturer / 67% driver) suggests juries are capable of nuanced allocation, but it also underscores the need for clearer regulatory guidance so that responsibility does not depend on the rhetorical skills of trial counsel.

The deeper ethical question, articulated by scholars such as Philip Koopman, is whether it is ever reasonable to expect humans to serve as reliable fallback monitors for systems that are advertised as autonomous. The German approach — permitting the driver to divert attention while requiring "sufficient alertness" — has been criticized as contradictory [Winkler – Regulation in Germany and the US](http://arno.uvt.nl/show.cgi?fid=149595). The UK's AVA 2024 self-driving test, which requires that authorized automated vehicles achieve safety "equivalent to, or higher than, that of careful and competent human drivers," establishes an explicit ethical benchmark [AVA 2024](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2024/10).

## 6. Regulatory Recommendations

The following recommendations are grounded in the technical realities documented in Section 2, the legal frameworks analyzed in Section 3, and the judicial outcomes described in Section 4. They are designed to be actionable for regulators in the U.S., EU, UK, and other jurisdictions developing ADAS policy.

### 6.1 Data Access and Event Data Recorders

**Problem:** Liability determination depends on data that only the manufacturer controls. In *Benavides*, Tesla allegedly uploaded and attempted to erase data; in the Cruise case, the operator withheld key video from regulators for weeks [Telemetry](https://www.telemetryagency.com/post/a-tesla-crash-missing-data-and-the-cost-of-regulatory-inaction) [Reuters – Cruise](https://www.reuters.com/business/autos-transportation/gm-self-driving-unit-cruise-pay-15-million-fine-over-crash-disclosure-2024-09-30).

**Recommendations:**
- **Mandate a standardized ADAS Event Data Recorder (EDR) and Data Storage System for Automated Driving (DSSAD)** for all vehicles with Level 2 or higher automation, recording at minimum: (a) whether the automation feature was engaged at the time of and during the 30 seconds preceding the crash, (b) the system's ODD status and whether the vehicle was operating inside or outside its ODD, (c) driver monitoring system outputs (hands-on-wheel, gaze/attention state, warnings issued), (d) takeover request timing and driver response latency, (e) sensor data sufficient to reconstruct perception and decision-making (following the UN-R157 DSSAD model) [UN R157](https://regulations.ai/regulations/RAI-IO-UNECE-R157-2021).
- **Guarantee independent data access:** Crash investigators, plaintiffs, and regulators should have a statutory right to retrieve EDR/DSSAD data without manufacturer intermediation, following the German Section 63a StVG data-logging model [CMS – Germany](https://cms.law/en/int/expert-guides/cms-expert-guide-to-autonomous-vehicles-avs/germany). Data must be tamper-evident, and destruction or concealment of crash data should be a separate offense.
- **Standardize data formats** across manufacturers (building on EU Commission Delegated Regulation 2022/545 on EDRs) to enable cross-manufacturer research, insurance adjudication, and regulatory oversight [EU GSR – Connected Automated Driving](https://www.connectedautomateddriving.eu/regulation-and-policies/eu-level).

### 6.2 Driver Monitoring Standards

**Problem:** The NTSB determined in 2017 that torque-based monitoring is a "poor surrogate" for engagement, yet Tesla's system remained torque-based for years afterward, and no U.S. regulation mandates direct DMS [NTSB HAB1907](https://www.ntsb.gov/investigations/AccidentReports/Reports/HAB1907.pdf).

**Recommendations:**
- **Mandate direct (camera-based) driver monitoring for all Level 2 and Level 3 systems** that allow sustained hands-free operation, following the EU General Safety Regulation 2 model (mandatory DMS in new EU type approvals from July 2024) [Driver Monitoring Trends](https://www.autonomousvehicleinternational.com/features/feature-driver-monitoring-systems-trends-and-developments.html).
- **Require graduated escalation protocols:** alerts must escalate from visual through auditory to haptic within a defined maximum time (e.g., the 4–8 second response achieved by current camera-based systems), and the system must disable itself or resume a minimal risk maneuver after repeated non-response — mirroring the five-strike suspension NHTSA compelled in Tesla recall 23V-838 [NHTSA Part 573 23V-838](https://static.nhtsa.gov/odi/rcl/2023/RCLRPT-23V838-8276.PDF).
- **Prohibit driver-monitoring defeat mechanisms** — including any design that allows the system to operate with a covered camera or weighted steering wheel device.
- **Align Level 3 takeover time standards with the empirical evidence:** the 10-second UN-R157 requirement should be understood as a floor, not a ceiling; regulators should require context-dependent takeover timing (longer for high-complexity/high-speed scenarios) based on the 16–30 second lead times identified in takeover research [Takeover Lead Time Study](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S000145752200029X).

### 6.3 Disclosure and Marketing Requirements

**Problem:** The names "Autopilot" and "Full Self-Driving" have been found by a California administrative tribunal to be misleading and "counterfactual," and 53% of Cadillac Super Cruise users and 42% of Tesla Autopilot users treat Level 2 systems as fully self-driving [CNBC – DMV Ruling](https://www.cnbc.com/2025/12/16/california-judge-says-tesla-engaged-in-deceptive-autopilot-marketing-.html) [Driver Monitoring Trends](https://www.autonomousvehicleinternational.com/features/feature-driver-monitoring-systems-trends-and-developments.html).

**Recommendations:**
- **Prohibit misleading system names and marketing claims:** Systems that require continuous driver supervision (Level 2) should not be marketed using terms that reasonably imply full autonomy ("autopilot," "self-driving," "full self-driving"). The UK's AVA 2024 marketing offences — a "protected terms" offence restricting listed terms and a "confusion" offence covering any communication likely to mislead — provide a legislative model [GOV.UK – Protecting Marketing Terms](https://www.gov.uk/government/consultations/automated-vehicles-protecting-marketing-terms/automated-vehicles-protecting-marketing-terms).
- **Require plain-language ODD disclosures at point of sale:** Consumers should be informed, in clear non-technical language, where the system can and cannot operate, what conditions trigger disengagement, and the nature of the driver's monitoring obligations. Disclosure should occur before purchase and via standardized on-screen notices, not merely in a 200-page owner's manual.
- **Adopt standardized capability labeling:** A common, regulator-approved description of each system's capabilities (e.g., using SAE J3016 terminology supplemented by plain-language descriptors) would reduce the consumer expectation gap that underlies both litigation and unsafe driver behavior.

### 6.4 Insurance Design

**Problem:** The current fault-based auto insurance system is ill-equipped to handle crashes involving multiple potential defendants (driver, manufacturer, software developer, repair shop), and ADAS increases repair costs and claim complexity while IIHS data shows partial automation does not reduce crash rates [IEEE Spectrum](https://spectrum.ieee.org/partial-vehicle-autonomy-risk).

**Recommendations:**
- **Adopt an insurance-first, victim-compensation model for Level 3+ automation,** following the UK AEVA 2018 framework: the vehicle's insurer is directly liable for damage caused while the ADS is engaged, with limited exclusions (unauthorized software alterations, failure to install safety-critical updates, contributory negligence), and insurers pursue subrogation against manufacturers [AEVA 2018 s.2](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2018/18/section/2). This structure ensures prompt victim compensation while preserving manufacturer accountability through subrogated litigation [York – AEVA and the Insurance Solution](https://pure.york.ac.uk/portal/en/publications/tort-and-autonomous-vehicle-accidents-the-automated-and-electric-).
- **Require dual-coverage policies for Level 2 vehicles:** Policies should explicitly delineate coverage for driver-fault events (driver negligence) versus product-liability events (system defect), with insurers required to report subrogation data to regulators to build an evidence base for liability allocation.
- **Mandate insurance premium transparency for ADAS:** Given evidence that ADAS can increase repair costs and premiums by up to 40% [Advanced Fleet Management Consulting](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ACBRycUJqOQ), regulators should require insurers to disclose how specific ADAS features affect premiums, preventing perverse incentives that discourage ADAS adoption or conceal its true costs.
- **Develop pooled data infrastructure:** Encourage — or require — the creation of an anonymized ADAS-crash database (drawing on EDR/DSSAD data from Recommendation 6.1) to which insurers, manufacturers, and regulators contribute, enabling actuarially sound pricing and evidence-based safety regulation.

### 6.5 Notification, Reporting, and Post-Crash Protocols

**Problem:** The NHTSA Standing General Order has generated valuable data, but Consumer Reports has documented weaknesses: Ford did not learn of two fatal BlueCruise crashes for two weeks and one month respectively, and the amended SGO allows confidentiality claims on critical fields [Consumer Reports – SGO Comments](https://advocacy.consumerreports.org/research/consumer-reports-comments-to-nhtsa-on-incident-reporting-for-automated-driving-systems-and-level-2-adas).

**Recommendations:**
- **Strengthen mandatory crash notification:** Require real-time, automated crash notification from ADAS-equipped vehicles (using built-in telematics and EDR/DSSAD data) to a central national authority, with minimum notification windows measured in hours, not days — and with independent regulatory access to vehicle data (as GM's Cruise violation demonstrates, self-reporting alone is insufficient) [Reuters – Cruise Fine](https://www.reuters.com/business/autos-transportation/gm-self-driving-unit-cruise-pay-15-million-fine-over-crash-disclosure-2024-09-30).
- **Eliminate confidentiality carve-outs for safety-critical fields:** The SGO's permission for manufacturers to claim confidentiality over the automation feature version, ODD status, and crash narrative undermines the order's purpose; these fields should be presumptively public, subject to narrow, demonstrated trade-secret protection [Consumer Reports – SGO Comments](https://advocacy.consumerreports.org/research/consumer-reports-comments-to-nhtsa-on-incident-reporting-for-automated-driving-systems-and-level-2-adas).
- **Establish mandatory recall-effectiveness evaluation:** Following the NHTSA investigation into whether Tesla's 23V-838 remedy was adequate, regulators should adopt a standard protocol for evaluating OTA-based safety remedies, including post-remedy crash monitoring and independent testing [USA Today – Recall Probe](https://www.usatoday.com/story/money/cars/recalls/2024/04/26/nhtsa-tesla-self-driving-recall-investigation/73473322007).
- **Harmonize reporting regimes across jurisdictions:** The U.S., EU, and UK should coordinate crash reporting definitions and data standards (building on UN-R157 DSSAD and the EU EDR regulation) so that cross-jurisdictional data is comparable and manufacturers cannot exploit regulatory arbitrage.

### 6.6 Cross-Cutting Principles

Four principles should govern all regulatory action:

1. **Design-based controls over warning-based controls:** When a system can safely prevent a risk through design (geofencing to the ODD, camera-based DMS, disengagement from unauthorized use), design is always preferable to warnings. The Third Restatement's Comment l preference for safer design over warnings should be codified in ADAS regulation.
2. **Liability should track control:** The entity that controls the relevant function at the time of the crash should bear presumptive responsibility — the driver for supervision failures in Level 2, the manufacturer for system failures in Level 3+, with the presumption rebuttable by evidence of misuse or defect.
3. **Marketing claims define the standard of care:** Regulatory findings that conduct is deceptive (California DMV), marketing that creates confusion (UK AVA 2024), and judicial admission of marketing as evidence (Benavides) should be linked: a system whose marketing reasonably leads consumers to overestimate its capabilities should bear a corresponding share of liability for resulting crashes.
4. **Data transparency is a precondition for justice:** Victims, insurers, and regulators need access to the data that determines what the system did, what the driver did, and when control transferred. Without this, the boundary of responsibility will be litigated blindly, case by case, at enormous social cost.

## 7. Conclusion

The allocation of liability in ADAS-equipped vehicle accidents is transitioning from a driver-centric to a system-centric paradigm, but the transition is incomplete and uneven. Technically, the SAE taxonomy defines when the machine is "driving," but Level 2 systems — which dominate the market — blur that line by design: they control the vehicle while requiring the human to monitor, and they are frequently used (and marketed) in ways that erode the monitoring function. Legally, the frameworks exist — negligence and strict liability for drivers, product liability for manufacturers, insurance-first models in the UK, data-mandate models in Germany and the EU — but they have been applied inconsistently, with results ranging from complete defense verdicts for Tesla (*Hsu*, *Molander*) to a landmark 33/67 apportionment of fault (*Benavides*).

The emerging jurisprudence, particularly *Benavides* and the California DMV's false advertising ruling, points toward a principled synthesis: drivers remain responsible for their own inattention and misuse, but manufacturers are responsible for design choices that make safe human supervision impossible (inadequate DMS), that permit operation beyond validated capabilities (ODD violations), or that mislead consumers about what the system can do. NHTSA's recalls of Tesla's Autopilot and the regulatory frameworks in Germany, the UK, and the EU demonstrate that administrative action can shape the boundary of responsibility before — not merely after — crashes occur.

The most urgent regulatory priorities are clear: mandate direct driver monitoring, require design-based ODD enforcement, prohibit misleading marketing, guarantee independent access to crash data, and adopt an insurance-first compensation model for higher levels of automation. Each of these measures is technically feasible today; together, they would close the responsibility gap that currently leaves drivers and manufacturers litigating — one tragic crash at a time — over a boundary that regulators are best positioned to draw.
