# Reinterpreting the Past: The Politics of Historical Memory in the Contemporary World

## Introduction

The production of historical knowledge has never been a neutral enterprise. As Michel-Rolph Trouillot observed, "History is the fruit of power, but power itself is never so transparent that its analysis becomes superfluous. The ultimate mark of power may be its invisibility; the ultimate challenge, the exposition of its roots" [1]. Across the globe, historical narratives are being actively reinterpreted, contested, and weaponized through contemporary political and social lenses — from textbook wars in India and Japan to memory laws in Russia and Poland, from the toppling of colonial statues to the restitution of looted African art. This report examines four interrelated dimensions of this phenomenon: the **ideologisation of history** (how ideological frameworks shape or distort historical narratives); the **instrumentalisation of the past** (how history is deployed as a tool for current political, social, and economic agendas); efforts to **reclaim silenced narratives** (how marginalized and suppressed voices are being recovered); and the role of **commemorative practices and historical memory** in legitimating or contesting power. Drawing on cases spanning South Asia, East Asia, the Middle East, Europe, the Americas, and Africa, and on theoretical perspectives from historiography, memory studies, public history, cultural studies, and political theory, the report maps the recurring patterns through which the past is made to serve the present.

## 1. The Ideologisation of History: How Ideological Frameworks Shape Historical Narrative

### 1.1 Theoretical Foundations

**Hayden White and the narrativist turn.** Hayden White's *Metahistory* (1973) fundamentally challenged the positivist assumption that history can be a value-free reconstruction of facts. White argued that historical writing relies on rhetorical and literary strategies — employment into archetypal plot structures (Romance, Tragedy, Comedy, Satire), modes of argument, and ideological implication — and concluded that "a value-free history" cannot exist because history is recorded differently depending on the mode the historian chooses [2]. As Elizabeth Clark summarized, narrative "impose[s] coherence, continuity, and closure on the messiness of life and the historian's sources" [3]. White's insistence that "narrative is not neutral" and that it "entails ontological and epistemic choices with distinct ideological and even specifically political implications" provides the theoretical foundation for analyzing how the same events can be emplotted into radically different political narratives [2][3]. His critics, such as Geoffrey Elton, warned that if all reconstructions are of equal value, historians "cannot be refuted" — a charge that remains central to contemporary "history wars" [3].

**Marx and the Marxist tradition.** The Marxist tradition offers the most extensive theoretical framework for understanding ideology's role in history. Marx's materialist conception of history holds that "it is not the consciousness of men that determines their existence, but their social existence that determines their consciousness" [5]. Engels characterized ideology as "false consciousness," a concept later systematized by Georg Lukács, while Antonio Gramsci's theory of hegemony explained how ruling classes maintain dominance through cultural and ideological leadership rather than coercion alone [4]. Louis Althusser shifted the analysis from consciousness to "ideological state apparatuses" — institutions including education, religion, and media that "interpellate individuals as subjects" [4]. Marx's famous dictum that "the ideas of the ruling class are in every epoch the ruling ideas" remains a concise formulation of why state-controlled historical narratives tend to serve the interests of the powerful [5].

**Hobsbawm and Anderson: constructed nations and invented traditions.** Eric Hobsbawm and Terence Ranger's *The Invention of Tradition* (1983) demonstrated that "traditions which appear or claim to be old are often quite recent in origin and sometimes invented," serving three purposes: fostering social cohesion, legitimizing institutions and authority, and inculcating values and norms of behavior through repetition [6][7]. Hobsbawm noted the "curious but understandable paradox: modern nations... generally claim to be the opposite of novel, namely rooted in remotest antiquity" [6]. Benedict Anderson's *Imagined Communities* (1983) similarly argued that nations are not ancient communities but "imagined political communities" produced by nationalism itself, emerging from the interaction of capitalism and print technology [8]. The critical conclusion — the nation does not preexist nationalism — underpins analysis of how nationalist historiography fabricates ancient pedigrees for modern political projects [8].

**Trouillot: silencing and the production of history.** Trouillot's *Silencing the Past* (1995) provides perhaps the most useful analytical vocabulary for this study. Trouillot argued that power enters historical production at four crucial moments: fact creation (the making of sources), fact assembly (the making of archives), fact retrieval (the making of narratives), and retrospective significance (the making of history in the present) [1][9]. Silences are "inherent in the creation of sources," and archives are not neutral repositories but "an active act of production that prepares facts for historical intelligibility" [1]. His case studies — the Battle of the Alamo, the Haitian Revolution (which was "unthinkable" to Western contemporaries who could not conceive that enslaved people could organize and win their own revolution), and Columbus's "discovery" — demonstrate how commemorative naming and narration "sanitize further the messy history lived by the actors" [9].

### 1.2 Case Studies in Ideologised Historiography

**India: "Saffronisation" and the NCERT textbook wars.** India's National Council of Educational Research and Training (NCERT) has been a battleground for competing ideological visions of Indian history since the 1970s. Under the Janata government (1977–79), textbooks by historians Romila Thapar, Bipan Chandra, and R.S. Sharma were accused of being "anti-Indian and anti-national" [10]. Under the BJP-led NDA government (1998–2004), the 2002 "saffronisation" removed references to beef-eating, critiques of caste, and reduced coverage of Buddhism, Jainism, and Muslim rulers [10]. After the BJP returned to power in 2014, a 2022 "rationalisation" exercise deleted chapters on Mughal courts, the Cold War, social movements (Chipko, Dalit Panthers, Narmada Bachao Andolan), the 2002 Gujarat riots, and Gandhi's assassin Nathuram Godse [10]. Romila Thapar has condemned these deletions, arguing that "history is based on continuity of events" and that the NCERT is engaging in "rationing" rather than "rationalising": "When textbooks have been hacked, one knows that education is not the primary purpose of the books. The actual concern is to nurture citizens that are content with what they are taught by those in authority, without in any way questioning it" [11]. New textbooks describe Babur as a "brutal and ruthless conqueror" and Aurangzeb as a temple destroyer, echoing British colonial stereotypes — what historian Sekhar Bandyopadhyay calls an "erasure" of Muslim contributions that, if unchallenged, will let "the hegemony of ideologies... spread and erase what should not be forgotten" [12].

**Pakistan: Curriculum Islamisation under Zia-ul-Haq.** General Zia-ul-Haq's military regime (1977–1988) made Islamisation "the centerpiece" of state policy, including the "overhauling [of] school textbooks and libraries to remove un-Islamic material" [13]. Zia's policies, which also included the 1979 Hudud Ordinances and making Zakat a 2.5% annual tax, were justified as returning Pakistan to its Islamic foundations: "Pakistan which was created in the name of Islam will continue to survive only if it sticks to Islam" [13]. The curriculum reforms produced generations educated with religiously inflected historical narratives, contributing to what critics describe as an educational system producing "millions of religious students and sympathies toward extremism" [13].

**Japan: The textbook wars over World War II.** Japan's textbook authorization system has been a recurring site of ideological struggle since the 1950s. Historian Ienaga Saburo filed three lawsuits between 1965 and 1997 against the Ministry of Education for rejecting his textbook for containing "too many illustrations of the 'dark side' of the war" [14]. The 1982 diplomatic crisis erupted when the Ministry ordered changes substituting "advance into" for "aggression in" China, prompting protests from China and South Korea [14]. In 2001, the nationalist Japanese Society for History Textbook Reform (Tsukurukai) produced *The New History Textbook*, which an International Scholars' Appeal declared "unfit as a teaching tool" for negating "the truth about Japan's record in colonialism and war" [14][15]. Although the textbook was rejected by nearly all Japanese school districts, the controversies demonstrate how states attempt to whitewash wartime atrocities through curriculum control. As historians Laura Hein and Mark Selden note, "people fight over textbook content because education is so obviously about the future, reaches so deeply into society, and is directed by the state" [15].

**Turkey: Genocide denial as official historiography.** Armenian genocide denial has been "the official policy of every government of the Republic of Turkey" since the 1920s [16]. Historian Ronald Grigor Suny summarizes the denialist argument as "There was no genocide, and the Armenians were to blame for it" [16]. Denial is "taught in Turkish schools," archives have been destroyed or restricted, and citizens who recognize the genocide face prosecution for "insulting Turkishness" [16]. Taner Akçam, the first Turkish historian to acknowledge the genocide, argues that Turkey's founding cadres were members of the Committee of Union and Progress behind the 1915 genocide, creating five state-protected taboos, and that the denial mindset originates from the post-WWI period when "an open discussion of history" was reframed as a "security problem" [17]. Akçam's central conclusion — that "a failure to confront history honestly is one of the major reasons for insecurity and instability in the region" — illustrates how ideologised historiography becomes embedded in national security doctrine [17].

**Russia: Putin-era historical revisionism.** Russia's state historiography under Vladimir Putin has become a centrally directed project. A 2021 memory law bans "any public attempt to equate the aims and actions of the Soviet Union and Nazi Germany during World War II, as well as to deny the decisive role of the Soviet people in the victory over fascism" [18]. Putin subsequently established an inter-ministerial commission for the interpretation of history, headed by former culture minister Vladimir Medinsky, including officials from the FSB and the Military Historical Society, tasked with analyzing "foreign entities that may harm Russia's interests" and organizing "counter-propaganda events" — described by critics as "a level of systematic censorship not seen since Stalin's times" [19]. Russia rewrote its school history textbooks to include a chapter on the Ukraine war, describing how the West became "fixated on destabilizing Russia and dismembering it to grab its resources" — condemned by teachers as "a propaganda text" rather than a textbook [20].

**China: The "century of humiliation" and patriotic education.** China's official historiography is organized around the "century of humiliation" (1839–1949), a narrative cemented through the slogan "Never Forget National Humiliation" [21]. After the 1989 Tiananmen crackdown, the CCP launched the Patriotic Education Campaign to reestablish legitimacy and justify one-party rule; "historical memory provides essential raw material to construct China's national identity and shape the projection of Chinese power" [21]. By 2020, all provinces used uniform state textbooks; the Cultural Revolution was reduced from four pages to one, and Mao's mistake was removed [21]. Xi Jinping's "China Dream" narrative centers on national rejuvenation, adding new memorial days (December 13 Nanjing Massacre Memorial Day, September 3 Victory Day) and portraying the Boxer Rebellion as patriotic [22]. As Suisheng Zhao argues, Xi "has nurtured a new generation of nationalists who are intolerant of any criticism of the CCP regime and who are muscularly hostile to the Western powers and to Western values" [22]. China's territorial claims in the South China Sea and its stance on Taiwan are explicitly framed through this historical grievance narrative [23].

**Australia: The history wars.** The Australian "history wars" pit a "black armband" view of Australian history (emphasizing frontier violence and dispossession) against a "Three Cheers" view of "heroic achievement" [24]. Bill Stanner coined "the Great Australian Silence" in 1968 to describe history books' omission of "several hundred thousand Aboriginal people who lived and died between 1788 and 1938" [24]. Keith Windschuttle's *The Fabrication of Aboriginal History* (2002) claimed left-wing scholars exaggerated violence for political reasons, estimating only 118 Tasmanian Aboriginal people were directly killed by the British — a claim disputed in *Whitewash* (ed. Robert Manne) [24]. The debate over whether colonisation constituted genocide — with scholars like Tony Barta arguing that destroying a group constitutes genocide regardless of intent — remains unresolved [24]. The "Stolen Generations" debate similarly questioned the 1997 *Bringing Them Home* report's methodology, with Robert Manne calling such challenges "historical denialism" [24].

**United States: The 1619 Project and anti-CRT legislation.** In the United States, the 1619 Project (2019), developed by Nikole Hannah-Jones, "seeks to reframe American history by placing the consequences of slavery and the experiences of Black Americans at the core of the national story" [25]. The project became "a leading subject of the 'American history wars,'" criticized by historians across the political spectrum for inaccuracies (notably the claim that protecting slavery was a primary motivation of the American Revolution, which the Times later "clarified") [25]. In response, conservative legislatures passed laws banning "critical race theory" and the 1619 Project. Texas's House Bill 3979 (2021) prohibits requiring students to learn "that slavery was the true founding of the US" and bans requiring students to understand the 1619 Project [26]. Florida's "Stop WOKE Act" restricts how race and gender can be taught in schools and workplaces, though a federal appeals court blocked the law on First Amendment grounds [27]. As one teacher put it, the campaign has "disrupted all of my lesson plans" [27].

## 2. The Instrumentalisation of the Past: Memory Laws, State Narratives, and Political Agendas

### 2.1 Memory Laws as Instruments of Power

**Russia's weaponized memory laws.** Russia's Article 354.1 of the Criminal Code, introduced in 2014, criminalizes "rehabilitation of Nazism," including denial of facts recognized by the Nuremberg Tribunal and "spreading intentionally false information about the Soviet Union's activities during WWII" [28]. The 2021 law banning comparisons between the Soviet Union and Nazi Germany was extended two days before the February 2022 invasion of Ukraine, attaching fines and prison sentences [18]. As of July 2023, 620 people had been indicted on anti-war articles across 78 regions; cases included a Krasnoyarsk resident charged for posting that "Stalin was as much of an aggressor as Hitler" [20]. As Francine Hirsch argues, Putin's rewriting of WWII history "set the stage for the invasion of Ukraine": the memory law's co-author cited "the verdict of the Nuremberg Tribunal" as legal basis precisely because Nuremberg did not hold the Soviet Union accountable for the Molotov-Ribbentrop Pact, the invasion of Poland, the annexation of the Baltics, or the Katyn Forest Massacre [18].

**Ukraine's decommunization laws (2015).** In May 2015, Ukraine passed four laws banning Communist and Nazi symbols, opening KGB archives, and recognizing OUN/UPA fighters as independence heroes [29]. The campaign renamed 987 cities and villages and over 51,000 streets, and removed 2,389 communist monuments — "one of the most extensive symbolic overhauls ever undertaken by a European nation" [29]. Proponents like Volodymyr Vyatrovych argued the Communist regime "set out deliberately to destroy national identity"; critics like Vasyl Rasevych countered that "Ukrainian history is so antagonistic and confrontational, it can't perform a consolidating function" [30]. The Venice Commission, while recognizing Ukraine's right to ban totalitarian symbols, criticized the laws as "not precise enough" and called for a "multiperspective" approach to history [31]. The laws illustrate the double-edged nature of memory legislation: decommunization as liberation from Soviet narratives also imposed a single state-endorsed national narrative.

**Poland's 2018 IPN amendment.** Poland's Law and Justice government criminalized public speech attributing responsibility for the Holocaust to Poland or the Polish nation, punishable by up to three years in prison [32]. The law sparked "the biggest diplomatic crisis in Poland's recent history": Israel's Prime Minister accused Poland of Holocaust denial, the Simon Wiesenthal Center issued a travel advisory, and Yad Vashem condemned the bill [32]. After international pressure, the criminal provisions were removed within four months, but civil liability remained. The law had the perverse effect of boosting worldwide searches for "Polish death camps" ninefold [32]. In the 2021 case *Leszczyńska v. Engelking and Grabowski*, historians Jan Grabowski and Barbara Engelking were convicted under the amended law for a passage based on survivor testimony about a wartime mayor — a ruling later overturned on appeal [33]. Amnesty International called the law an infringement on freedom of expression with "a chilling effect" on historical research [34]. Yad Vashem's historians argued the revision "left the gist of the legislation unchanged" and that "any attempt to limit academic and public discourse by means of legislation and punishment is fundamentally improper" [35].

**European Holocaust denial laws.** Eighteen European countries criminalize Holocaust denial [36]. Germany's Section 130 (Volksverhetzung), first passed in 1985 and amended in 1994, prohibits denial or playing down of Nazi genocide, punishable by up to five years [36][37]. France's Gayssot Act (1990) criminalizes questioning the existence of "crimes against humanity" as defined in the Nuremberg Charter [36]. As Nikolay Koposov argues in *Memory Laws, Memory Wars*, Western European memory laws were "designed to create a common European memory centred on the memory of the Holocaust as a means of integrating Europe, combating racism, and averting national and ethnic conflicts," whereas in Russia and Eastern Europe "legislation on the issues of the past is often used to give the force of law to narratives which serve the narrower interests of nation states and protect the memory of perpetrators rather than victims" [38]. The IHRA notes a troubling trend: "more and more governments are rewriting these laws in order to protect national narratives about the Holocaust" [39].

### 2.2 State Memory Politics

**Russia's Great Patriotic War cult and the 2022 invasion.** The Great Patriotic War (1941–45) cost the USSR 24 million killed; Victory Day (May 9) remains Russia's most important national holiday [40]. But Victory Day is itself an "invented tradition": after the first parade in 1945, May 9 was a working day until 1965, and annual military parades only began after 1995 [41][43]. Under Putin, the holiday became "a cornerstone of official state ideology with an increasingly militarised character," incorporating civilian practices like the St George ribbon and the Immortal Regiment into the official canon [41]. Putin's Victory Day speeches evolved from celebrating victory to explicitly naming the US and NATO as enemies and framing the 2022 invasion as preemptive and defensive: "Today you are defending what your fathers, grandfathers and great-grandfathers fought for" [42]. The 2025 parade featured "veterans of the Special Military Operation" and equipment used in the invasion of Ukraine [41]. As one analysis puts it, Russia's memory politics transformed the war's meaning from "never again" to "we can repeat it" [41].

**Hungary: Trianon trauma and illiberal memory politics.** The Treaty of Trianon (1920) stripped Hungary of two-thirds of its territory and left one in three ethnic Hungarians outside its borders [44][45][46]. Since 2010, Viktor Orbán has weaponized this "national trauma": declaring June 4 the "Day of National Unity," granting citizenship to ~1.1 million transborder Hungarians who largely vote Fidesz, and building more than 125 Trianon monuments [44]. The 2020 Memorial of National Unity in Budapest — a descending ramp into a pit with black granite walls engraved with over 13,000 pre-Trianon place names — "inappropriately appropriates the international aesthetic of memorials like the Vietnam Veterans Memorial, Yad Vashem, and the 9/11 Memorial... to express an ethnocentric, revisionist narrative of territorial 'loss'" [44]. The House of Terror Museum, opened in 2002, "emphasizes Hungary's victimhood under communism within a broader narrative of twentieth-century oppression," framing Hungary solely as a victim of Nazi Germany and minimizing its responsibility for Jewish persecution [47]. As Réka Deim's research shows, memory politics has become "a central ideological instrument in Orbán's illiberal regime," promoting "a univocal, nationalist victimhood narrative" [48].

**Israel: Holocaust memory and political uses.** Yad Vashem, established by the Knesset in 1953, is Israel's official Holocaust memorial, receiving ~1 million visitors annually [49]. The institution maintains the Holocaust "cannot be compared to any other event," and a docent was fired in 2009 for comparing Holocaust trauma to Palestinian trauma [49]. Scholars have extensively documented the political instrumentalisation of Holocaust memory. Omer Bartov examines "the use and abuse of the Holocaust as a historical event, a traumatic memory, and a warning to future generations" [51]. Dani Dayan, Yad Vashem's current chair, critiques the use of the Shoah for "Hasbara" (public relations), warning that with the passing of survivors, "the happy hour of the denialists and distortionists is approaching" [50]. In Europe, Holocaust memory is weaponized to police criticism of Israel through the "memory-shell" concept — using Holocaust commemoration to claim moral authority and dismiss opposing viewpoints [51].

**Turkey: Gallipoli as counter-narrative.** The AKP government under Erdoğan reframed the 1915 Gallipoli campaign as a "struggle of Muslim martyrs against Christian invaders" [52]. The centenary commemorations were deliberately moved from April 25 to April 24, 2015 — coinciding with the Armenian genocide centenary — in what critics called "an indecent political manoeuvre" to divert international attention [52]. The state promoted inflated martyr figures ("253,000 martyrs") and organized Quran recitations across 81 cities [52]. This "Muslim martyrdom" politics, as Taner Akçam argues, strengthened Turkish martyrdom politics by placing "Muslim losses" in competition with Armenian genocide victims [52]. The Ottoman victory at Kut al-Amara was similarly "rediscovered" in 2016 as the "second most important Ottoman victory after Gallipoli," with lavish state ceremonies and a popular TV series [52].

### 2.3 Economic Instrumentalisation: Dark Tourism and Heritage Commodification

The past is also instrumentalised economically through "dark tourism" — travel to sites of death and tragedy, a term coined in 1996 by John Lennon and Malcolm Foley [54]. Battlefield tourism has transformed "from pilgrimage to a leisurely, commodified cultural consumption (souvenirs, sound and light shows, commemorative bike tours), raising ethical questions" [55]. During the WWI centenary, France generated an estimated 110 million euros from battlefield tourism; Australia spent 210 million euros on centenary commemoration (3,500 euros per dead soldier) while Germany spent only 6 million [55]. Gallipoli attracts 2–3 million visitors per year, making the Dardanelles the second most visited WWI front [55]. A systematic review of dark tourism research found that "interest in death was the least common reason for visiting Auschwitz," with learning, empathy, and heritage connection being primary motivations — though the commodification of suffering ("kitschification") remains a persistent critique [56].

## 3. Reclaiming Silenced Narratives: Subaltern Histories, Truth Commissions, and Decolonisation

### 3.1 Theoretical Foundations

**Subaltern Studies.** The Subaltern Studies collective, founded by Ranajit Guha in 1982, sought "to refocus history on the unique role of subaltern or marginalized people in bringing about large-scale transformations in society," critiquing both nationalist historians' elite focus and orthodox Marxists' Eurocentric restriction of the working class to industrial workers [57]. Guha's *Elementary Aspects of Peasant Insurgency in Colonial India* (1983) "challenges the idea that peasants were powerless agents who rebelled blindly," emphasizing their political awareness and will [57]. Gayatri Spivak's "Can the Subaltern Speak?" (1988) problematized the politics of representation: structural disadvantages make it "extremely difficult for subaltern women to insert themselves authoritatively into public conversation without mediation" [58]. Dipesh Chakrabarty's *Provincializing Europe* (2000) argued for "treating Europe and its history as one history amongst others rather than as the telos of all human history" [59]. Four challenges face subaltern studies: textual records contain few subaltern voices; those that exist were written by the powerful; subalterns spoke under "extremely imbalanced circumstances"; and the elite/subaltern division is itself a product of colonial oppression [57].

**Ann Laura Stoler and the colonial archive.** Stoler's *Along the Archival Grain* (2009) examines "the affective registers of imperial governance and the political content of archival forms," asking "not what colonial agents knew, but what happened when what they thought they knew they found they did not" [60]. Rather than treating archives as sources to be mined, Stoler approaches "archival production as a consequential act of governance, a field of force with violent effect" [60]. Reading "along the grain" reveals the "politics of disregard" — "the cultivation of deliberate ignorance and self-deception among colonizers as a shield to withstand the inequalities of colonial relations" [61].

**Linda Tuhiwai Smith and Decolonizing Methodologies.** Linda Tuhiwai Smith's *Decolonizing Methodologies* (1999) opens with the declaration that "'research' is probably one of the dirtiest words in the indigenous world's vocabulary," arguing that Western research has been "inextricably linked to European imperialism and colonialism" [62]. Smith's framework insists that decolonization "must be a long-term process involving the cultural, linguistic and psychological divesting of colonial parameters," and that Indigenous methodologies (like Kaupapa Māori) challenge the researcher as "a distant, objective, morally authoritative figure" [62].

**Oral tradition as history.** Jan Vansina "transformed African historical study by championing the integrity and rigor of oral traditions," demonstrating through the Kuba kingdom that "oral stories constitute a powerful archive of historical knowledge rather than mere folklore" [63]. His methods — triangulating oral testimony with archaeological, linguistic, and anthropological evidence — have influenced "cultural reclamation movements across Africa, indigenous rights groups worldwide... where oral histories are accepted as legitimate court evidence" [63]. In Australia, the 1992 *Mabo* decision recognized native title, rejecting terra nullius, based substantially on oral tradition and Indigenous testimony [72].

### 3.2 Truth and Reconciliation Processes

**South Africa.** The Truth and Reconciliation Commission (1996–1998), chaired by Desmond Tutu, was "a court-like restorative justice body" that heard over 22,000 victim statements and granted only 849 amnesties out of 7,111 applications [65]. Tutu's approach was "rooted in the African concept of ubuntu — focused on healing breaches, redressing imbalances, and restoring broken relationships rather than punishment" [65][66]. The TRC's public hearings exposed apartheid's atrocities but drew criticism: most victims felt reconciliation failed, reparations were "too little, too late," and the commission was called the "Kleenex Commission" for going easy on some perpetrators [65]. The case of Mrs. Konile — whose testimony about a dream of a goat was dismissed as incoherent due to translation issues, but which followed isiXhosa cultural conventions — demonstrates "what is lost when expressions of human suffering are pressed into the recognizable, standardized language of human rights claims" [67].

**Canada.** The Canadian TRC (2008–2015) heard testimony from more than 6,000 survivors of the Indian Residential School system, in which over 150,000 Indigenous children were forced to attend church-run, government-funded schools [68][69]. The final report described the policy as "cultural genocide" and issued 94 Calls to Action, including mandatory K-12 curriculum on residential schools, a National Day for Truth and Reconciliation, and measures to locate and commemorate burial sites (Calls 71–76) [68]. The 2021 discovery of 215 unmarked graves at Kamloops and 751 at Marieval brought the issue to global attention [70]. The NCTR has documented 4,117 child deaths, while Commissioner Murray Sinclair estimated 6,000 or more may have died [70].

**Australia.** The 1997 *Bringing Them Home* report found that "between one in three and one in ten Indigenous children were forcibly removed between 1910 and 1970," describing the removals as "an act of genocide, aimed at wiping out indigenous families, communities, and cultures" [71]. Prime Minister John Howard refused to apologize, arguing it would imply "intergenerational guilt"; Kevin Rudd's 2008 National Apology passed unanimously [71]. Yet only 6% of the report's recommendations have been fully implemented nearly 30 years later [74].

**Guatemala.** The Commission for Historical Clarification (CEH, 1997–1999) documented over 200,000 killed or disappeared during the 36-year civil war, attributing 93% of violations to state forces and concluding that "agents of the State of Guatemala... committed acts of genocide against groups of Mayan people" [75][76]. The 2013 genocide conviction of General Efraín Ríos Montt — later overturned on a technicality — was the first time a former head of state was prosecuted for genocide by his own country's courts [75].

**United States.** The Federal Indian Boarding School Initiative, announced by Interior Secretary Deb Haaland in June 2021, documented 417 federal Indian boarding schools across 37 states, at least 973 Indigenous children who died in attendance, and 74 marked and unmarked burial sites [77][78]. The investigation found the system "deployed militarized and identity-alteration methodologies to assimilate" children, including renaming, hair cutting, language prohibition, and corporal punishment [77]. In October 2024, President Biden issued a historic apology [78].

### 3.3 Recovery of Black Memory in the United States

**The 1619 Project.** Launched in August 2019 to mark the 400th anniversary of the arrival of the first enslaved Africans in Virginia, the 1619 Project "takes a critical view of traditionally revered figures and events in American history," arguing that "out of slavery — and the anti-black racism it required — grew nearly everything that has truly made America exceptional" [25]. Hannah-Jones won the 2020 Pulitzer Prize for her introductory essay [25].

**The National Memorial for Peace and Justice.** Opened April 26, 2018 in Montgomery, Alabama, the memorial "commemorates the black victims of lynching in the United States" — more than 800 hanging steel rectangles, each representing a county where a documented lynching occurred, with victims' names engraved [79][80]. EJI documented nearly 4,400 racial terror lynchings between 1877 and 1950. The accompanying Legacy Museum: From Enslavement to Mass Incarceration, built on a former slave warehouse site, connects slavery to mass incarceration [79]. Bryan Stevenson argues "Americans believe in memorials; we just seem not to believe in memorials that reflect our failings" [81].

**Whitney Plantation.** Opened in 2014, Whitney Plantation in Louisiana became "the first American museum focused on the history of slavery," created by attorney John Cummings at a cost of $8 million, with Senegalese scholar Ibrahima Seck as director of research [82]. The site features "moving memorials to the 100,000 women, men, and children who were enslaved in Louisiana" [82].

**The National Museum of African American History and Culture.** Opened September 24, 2016 on the National Mall, NMAAHC is "the nation's largest and most comprehensive cultural destination devoted exclusively to exploring, documenting and showcasing the African American story" [83]. Its founding director Lonnie Bunch described the museum's slave cabin from Edisto Island as ensuring "the rich, complex and difficult story of the enslaved will be made accessible" [84]. The museum's Slave Wrecks Project discovered the Clotilda, "America's last slave ship" [85].

### 3.4 Decolonisation of Museums and Restitution

**The Sarr-Savoy Report (2018).** Commissioned by President Macron, the Sarr-Savoy Report — "The Restitution of African Cultural Heritage. Toward a New Relational Ethics" — found that French public collections hold approximately 90,000 objects from sub-Saharan Africa, and estimated that "approximately 90% of all cultural heritage from sub-Saharan Africa is held in Western collections" [86][87]. The authors defined colonization as a "crime against humanity," characterized European museums as "unwittingly the public archives of the colonial system," and recommended "definitive restitution" without conditions [87]. The report led to France's return of 26 Abomey statues to Benin (2021) and the Djidji Ayokwe drum to Ivory Coast (2025–26), and triggered global momentum for restitution [86]. As the frieze analysis noted, the report reframed the issue around Africa's young population's need for cultural heritage [88]. Critics argued universal museums serve global audiences and questioned African conservation capacity, while others felt the report did not go far enough [86][87].

**The Benin Bronzes.** In July 2022, Germany "transferred ownership of all Benin Bronzes from public collections to Nigeria" — over 1,000 objects, the "single largest repatriation of artifacts anywhere in the world" [89][90]. The objects were looted by British forces during the 1897 Benin Expedition; approximately 3,000 were taken [91]. A third of the Berlin objects remain on loan for display at the Humboldt Forum [90]. The British Museum refuses to return its 900+ Bronzes, citing the 1963 British Museum Act [91]. In Nigeria, an internal dispute over whether the Oba of Benin or the National Commission for Museums should hold returned objects has paused further returns [91][92].

**Māori ancestral remains.** France enacted a 2010 law specifically enabling the return of Toi Moko (mummified tattooed Māori heads) to New Zealand; the first repatriation from Rouen occurred in 2011 [93]. Te Papa's Karanga Aotearoa Repatriation Programme has coordinated returns from institutions worldwide, including Berlin's Ethnologisches Museum (2020) and the Wellcome Trust, which considered "the likely cultural, religious and spiritual significance of the remains to Māori/Moriori people outweighed their potential scientific, historical and educational value" [93].

**The Parthenon Marbles.** Greece has sought return of the Parthenon sculptures since its independence in 1835; the first official request came in 1983 [94]. The British Museum's Trustees "refute claims that the sculptures were stolen," insisting Elgin "acted with the full knowledge and permission of the legal authorities of the day" [95]. Advocates for return point to the dubious firman, the Stonehenge analogy, and the Acropolis Museum's now-adequate housing; retentionists cite the marbles' global influence and the "slippery slope" argument [94]. The debate has become a touchstone for the broader restitution movement, with some arguing that a "compelling moral obligation" under charity law could justify return [96][97].

### 3.5 Curricular Decolonisation

**South Africa: Rhodes Must Fall and FeesMust Fall.** The Rhodes Must Fall movement began on March 9, 2015 at UCT when student Chumani Maxwele threw human faeces on the Cecil Rhodes statue; the movement expanded to demand "decolonisation of education" and "racial transformation" [98]. The statue was removed on April 9, 2015. The movement's three pillars were "Pan-Africanism, Black consciousness, and Black radical feminism" [99]. It spread to Oxford (where Oriel College initially voted to keep the statue after donors threatened to withdraw £100 million, then launched a commission in 2020), Harvard Law School (Royall Must Fall), and beyond [98][99]. As Sabelo Ndlovu-Gatsheni argues, the student uprisings demanded "cognitive justice, curriculum change, removal of colonial/apartheid symbols, free and quality education" [100]. FeesMustFall (2015–2016) extended the struggle to economic access.

**New Zealand: Aotearoa New Zealand's Histories.** New Zealand made teaching its own history compulsory from 2023, "foregrounding the centrality of Māori histories and the impacts of colonisation" [101]. The reform resulted from a 2015 petition by two Ōtorohanga College students who had learned about colonial atrocities at Rangiaowhia [101]. The curriculum has three elements: Understand (including "Māori history is the foundational and continuous history of Aotearoa New Zealand"), Know, and Do [101]. In 2025, the National-ACT government's draft social sciences curriculum removed references to this foundational principle; ACT leader David Seymour celebrated that the "Marxist 'big ideas'" are "GONE" [101].

**Brazil: Law 10.639/2003.** Brazil's Law 10.639/2003 made "the teaching of Afro-Brazilian and African history and culture compulsory in public and private schools," extended by Law 11.645/2008 to include Indigenous history [102]. The law emerged from "post-dictatorship re-democratization and pressure from Afro-Brazilian movements" [102]. Implementation "largely falls on individual black teachers rather than being institutionalized," with many teachers unaware of the law and neo-Pentecostal resistance to Afro-Brazilian content [103].

**United Kingdom: Empire and slavery in the curriculum.** UK debates center on whether Britain's colonial past and the transatlantic slave trade should be compulsory; a 2020 petition received 268,772 signatures [104]. Only 11% of GCSE students in 2019 studied modules referring to Black British history contributions, and in 2013 Education Secretary Michael Gove made Black history teaching optional by removing the compulsory section on colonialism and slavery [104].

## 4. Commemorative Practices and the Struggle over Public Memory

### 4.1 Memory Theory: From Collective Memory to Counter-Monuments

**Halbwachs and collective memory.** Maurice Halbwachs pioneered the study of collective memory, arguing that "individual memory is constructed through social frameworks provided by our membership in social groups like family, social class, religion, etc." [105]. Memory is "both stable (representing a whole group) and shifting (dependent on present political, social, and economic concerns)" [107]. Halbwachs distinguished autobiographical memory from historical memory, showing that "each generation reconstructs the past 'within its contemporary frame of reference'" [107].

**Nora and lieux de mémoire.** Pierre Nora argued that modern societies build "lieux de mémoire" (sites of memory) — monuments, archives, museums, rituals, anniversaries — because they no longer live in "environments of memory"; memory is authentic and lived, while history is a structured, deliberate representation [106][107]. Nora separated memory (authentic, experienced) from history (structured, deliberate representation), and identified both tangible sites (monuments, archives) and abstract ones (oaths, rituals, anniversaries) [106].

**The Assmanns: communicative and cultural memory.** Jan Assmann distinguished "communicative memory" (everyday, intergenerational exchange with a three-generation cycle) from "cultural memory" (institutionalized, long-term symbolic storage maintained through texts, rites, monuments) [108][107]. Aleida Assmann's *Cultural Memory and Western Civilization* examines how "cultures invest great efforts into creating a long-term memory on the basis of oral transmission, media technology, and institutional frameworks," distinguishing "functional memory" from "storage memory" [109]. Jan Assmann's work demonstrated "the connection between cultural memory, collective identity, and political legitimization" — directly relevant to understanding state memory politics [108].

**Rothberg and multidirectional memory.** Michael Rothberg's "multidirectional memory" challenges the "zero-sum logic of competition" in which memories of different histories crowd each other out, arguing instead that "memories actually feed off of each other in a productive dynamic" [110]. The Holocaust's emergence as a global memory was "from the start inflected by histories of slavery, colonialism, and decolonization" [110]. Rothberg's concept of "the implicated subject" addresses those who "enable, inherit, benefit from, and help perpetuate violence and inequality without being perpetrators" [110].

**James Young and counter-monuments.** James Young argued that "as soon as we assign monumental form to memory, we have to some degree divested ourselves of the obligation to remember" [111]. Counter-monuments "provoke their viewers, demand interaction, have the possibility to change over time and insist that memory work be the burden of the viewer, not of the monument itself" [112]. Key examples include Jochen and Esther Gerz's disappearing Monument Against Fascism in Harburg, Horst Hoheisel's inverted Aschrottbrunnen in Kassel, and Peter Eisenman's Memorial to the Murdered Jews of Europe in Berlin [111][112]. Young noted that "no country builds a national memorial legacy on the memory of national crimes" — they build on martyrdoms and triumphs — making memorials to national crimes historically unprecedented [111].

### 4.2 Contested Monuments and Statue Debates

**Edward Colston (Bristol, June 2020).** On June 7, 2020, during Black Lives Matter protests, an estimated 15,000 people gathered in Bristol; protesters toppled the statue of Edward Colston — a 17th-century slave trader whose Royal African Company transported an estimated 84,000 enslaved Africans — and pushed it into Bristol Harbour [113]. The "Colston 4" were acquitted by a jury in January 2022 [113]. The statue now lies horizontally in the M-Shed museum's Protest gallery, still covered in graffiti, after 8 out of 10 survey respondents wanted it in a museum [115]. As Karen Macdonald of Bristol City Council put it: "This isn't erasing history, this is recording history. History isn't something that can remain static and preserved - that moment was history in action" [114]. The toppling triggered a national debate and the removal of statues of Robert Milligan, John Cass, and others [113].

**Confederate monuments in the United States.** More than 160 Confederate monuments have been removed from public spaces since 2015 [116]. Historians agree the monuments "were not built for historical preservation but to intimidate African Americans and reaffirm white supremacy," with construction peaking during the Jim Crow era and again during the civil rights movement [116]. The Charlottesville "Unite the Right" rally (August 2017) — where a white nationalist killed a counterprotester — made the issue a national flashpoint [116]. The American Historical Association stated that removing a monument "is not to erase history, but rather to alter or call attention to a previous interpretation of history" [116]. In 2025, President Trump signed an executive order "Restoring Truth and Sanity to American History" instructing the reinstatement of removed monuments [117].

**Leopold II in Belgium.** Following George Floyd's murder, Leopold II statues across Belgium were defaced, burned, and removed; the Antwerp statue was removed on June 9, 2020 [118]. King Philippe expressed "profound regrets" for the "wounds of the past" — the first royal family member to do so — and a parliamentary "truth and reconciliation" commission was formed [118]. Activists argued "removing statues does not erase history, it rectifies history" [119]. Leopold II's rule of the Congo Free State caused an estimated 10 million Congolese deaths [118].

**Columbus statues.** In June 2020, Columbus statues were beheaded in Boston, toppled in St. Paul and Richmond, and removed overnight in Chicago [120][121]. Native American activists have "long objected to honouring Columbus, saying that his expeditions to the Americas led to the colonisation and genocide of their ancestors" [120]. In Mexico City, a Columbus statue was replaced by one of an Indigenous woman [121].

**Gandhi statues.** The University of Ghana removed a Gandhi statue in December 2018 after staff and students petitioned against his racist writings about Africans, including references to "raw Kaffir" [122][123]. Gandhi's grandson Rajmohan Gandhi admitted he was "at times ignorant and prejudiced about South Africa's Blacks" [124]. A Malawi court halted a Gandhi statue after "Gandhi Must Fall" activists cited the same writings [122]. These cases show that even figures celebrated as anti-colonial icons are subject to critical re-evaluation through contemporary lenses.

### 4.3 "Retain and Explain" versus Removal

The UK government's "Retain and Explain" policy holds that "commemorative heritage assets should remain in situ": "We should not hide from aspects of our history that we may deem unacceptable today" [125]. The policy, formalized in 2023 guidance, requires planning permission to remove any historic unlisted statue or memorial in place for 10 years or more, and the Police, Crime, Sentencing and Courts Act (2022) increased the maximum penalty for criminal damage to memorials to 10 years' imprisonment [125][126]. Historian David Olusoga argues the policy "regurgitates two falsehoods — that modern attitudes are the problem and that statues tell us our history" [126]. Cultural critic Nathaniel Adam Tobias Coleman: "plaques don't solve the problem. And our problem is, and always has been, the pedestal in public space. No amount of contextualisation will detract from glorification" [127]. The Runnymede Trust argues "defending" contested monuments may make people feel excluded from public space [126].

## Conclusion

The cases examined in this report reveal recurring patterns in the reinterpretation of historical narratives through contemporary political and social lenses. First, the state remains the primary producer of official historical narratives through ministries of education, textbook authorities, memory laws, and commemorative commissions — from India's NCERT to Russia's inter-ministerial history commission, from China's patriotic education campaign to Poland's Institute of National Remembrance. Second, ideological frameworks — whether Hindutva, Islamism, nationalism, Marxism-Leninism, or liberal universalism — shape which facts are selected, how they are emplotted, and what they are made to mean, precisely as Hayden White's narrativist theory predicted. Third, these narratives are always contested: by professional historians (Romila Thapar, Ienaga Saburo, Taner Akçam, Henry Reynolds), by transnational actors (the International Scholars' Appeal, the Venice Commission, Yad Vashem), and by social movements (Rhodes Must Fall, Black Lives Matter, #NoDAPL). Fourth, the recovery of silenced narratives — through subaltern historiography, truth commissions, museum restitution, and curricular reform — is never a purely academic exercise; it is always a struggle over the legitimacy of the present order. As Trouillot wrote, "the past — or, more accurately, pastness — is a position" [1]. The ongoing global contest over monuments, textbooks, memory laws, and museum collections is ultimately a contest over who has the power to occupy that position and speak from it.
