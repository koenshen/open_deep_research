# Can a 500,000-Passenger Airport Generate Significant, Measurable Socioeconomic Impacts? A Comprehensive Evidence Review

## Executive Summary

The weight of evidence supports the hypothesis that an airport handling roughly 500,000 annual passengers can generate measurable socioeconomic impacts — **but with important qualifications about magnitude, attribution, and regional context**. Such an airport (≈250,000 annual enplanements) is a fully established commercial service airport: an FAA "non-hub primary" airport, roughly 25 times above the regulatory threshold for "primary" status, and comfortably above every minimum for scheduled airline service. Empirical case studies at airports in or near this size range (Tri-Cities, Tennessee/Virginia; Rapid City, South Dakota; several Louisiana non-hubs) document on the order of **1,000–2,900 supported jobs and $100–$450 million in annual regional economic output** — figures that are detectable with standard input-output methodology and locally significant in small or mid-sized regional economies.

However, the strongest econometric evidence tempers the enthusiasm of airport-commissioned impact studies. A panel study of 274 European regions found the long-run elasticity of GDP per capita with respect to air accessibility is only **0.022 for small airports versus 0.179 for large airports**, with causality running mainly from economic growth to airport traffic rather than the reverse (Pot & Koster 2022). A difference-in-differences study of new regional airport construction in Norway found statistically insignificant effects on population and employment (Tveter 2017). Regulatory and audit evidence (the European Court of Auditors, EAS subsidy data, ACI EUROPE traffic trends) shows that airports at this scale are frequently loss-making, dependent on public subsidy, and recovering more slowly than larger airports.

The balanced conclusion: **yes, impacts are measurable and can be locally significant; but per passenger they are smaller than at larger airports, commonly overstated by commissioned studies, and highly dependent on whether the airport serves a remote/rural region without competing airports or a region with alternative transport options.**

## 1. Where 500,000 Annual Passengers Sits in Airport Classification Systems

### 1.1 Total passengers vs. enplanements: A critical definitional distinction

Any assessment must first distinguish between **total passengers** and **enplanements**. An "enplanement" is one passenger boarding one aircraft; a round trip generates two enplanements. ACI World counts "total passengers = enplaned and deplaned, transit counted once," so total passenger throughput is roughly **double annual enplanements** for a typical origin-destination airport [1][7]. Therefore:

- **~500,000 total annual passengers ≈ ~250,000 annual enplanements** [1]
- In the U.S., FAA Passenger Boarding data counts revenue enplanements (paid tickets, loyalty redemptions, corporate discounts), which can differ from airport-reported figures by 1–5% due to inclusion of non-revenue passengers (airline employees, air marshals, etc.) [1].

### 1.2 FAA classification (United States)

Under 49 U.S.C. § 47102, FAA categories are defined by annual passenger boardings [2][3]:

- **Commercial service airport**: ≥2,500 annual boardings with scheduled passenger service
- **Primary airport**: commercial service airport with >10,000 annual boardings
- **General aviation airport**: no scheduled service or <2,500 annual boardings

An airport with ~250,000 enplanements is **25× above the 10,000-enplanement primary threshold** and 100× above the commercial service threshold. It is therefore a **primary commercial service airport** — not a non-primary or general aviation facility [3][4].

Primary airports are further classified by hub status, based on share of total U.S. commercial enplanements [2][3]:

- **Large hub**: ≥1.0% (≈9 million+ enplanements, ~31 airports)
- **Medium hub**: 0.25%–1.0% (≈2.2–9 million, ~32–33 airports)
- **Small hub**: 0.05%–0.25% (≈450,000–2.2 million, ~74 airports)
- **Non-hub (nonhub) primary**: >10,000 but <0.05% (≈250,000 or fewer enplanements, ~250 airports)

With ~250,000 enplanements, a ~500,000-passenger airport sits **just below the small-hub threshold** (0.05% of national enplanements ≈ 450,000–500,000 boardings) and is classified as a **non-hub primary airport** [4][35]. In FAA AIP funding terms, it receives non-hub primary entitlements, which carry a 4-year fund life and a 90% federal cost share in qualifying states [30].

### 1.3 Eurostat (European Union)

Eurostat's air transport statistics regulation (EC) No 437/2003 classifies airports by annual **passenger units** (1 passenger unit = 1 passenger or 100 kg of freight/mail) [5]:

- **Category 3**: >1,500,000 passenger units — full reporting (datasets A1, B1, C1)
- **Category 2**: 150,000–1,500,000 passenger units — full reporting required
- **Category 1**: 15,000–150,000 passenger units — limited reporting
- **Category 0**: <15,000 — no reporting obligation

A ~500,000-passenger airport falls squarely in **Eurostat Category 2**, a fully reporting commercial airport in EU statistics [5]. Note that ACI EUROPE's traffic reporting uses a different cut: "small airports" are defined as those **under 1 million passengers** — a segment that remained 34.5% below 2019 traffic levels in 2024, the slowest-recovering category [49].

### 1.4 ICAO

ICAO does **not** use passenger-volume categories. Its operative classification is the **Aerodrome Reference Code** (Annex 14), based on aircraft characteristics: Element 1 (runway reference field length: codes 1–4) and Element 2 (wingspan/outer main gear span: codes A–F) [6]. A 500,000-passenger airport typically serves regional aircraft and narrowbodies (e.g., CRJ-200, ATR-42, Boeing 737, Airbus A320) and would carry reference codes such as **2B, 3C, or 4C** depending on runway length and critical aircraft. ICAO Annex 14 also assigns rescue-and-fire-fighting (RFF) categories based on movements and aircraft dimensions. None of these indicate "smallness" in a passenger sense [6].

### 1.5 ACI World

ACI World assigns no fixed size categories; it ranks airports by total throughput. A 500,000-passenger airport would appear among the ~2,800 airports in the annual World Airport Traffic Dataset, counted as enplaned + deplaned passengers [7].

### 1.6 Scheduled service viability thresholds

A ~500,000-passenger airport (~685 enplanements/day) far exceeds every minimum for scheduled service:

- FAA commercial service minimum: 2,500 annual enplanements [2]
- **Essential Air Service (EAS) viability threshold: 10 enplanements per service day (≈3,650/year)**, with communities >175 miles from a hub exempt [8][9]
- Eurostat reporting floor: 15,000 passenger units [5]

By contrast, many subsidized EAS airports operate at 10–160 passengers per day; the busiest (Joplin, MO) averages under 160 daily passengers, and some EAS airports see fewer than a dozen departures per day [10][8]. A 500,000-passenger airport is an order of magnitude above these thresholds, meaning it supports **unsolicited, commercially scheduled service** well beyond the minimum benchmark.

## 2. Types and Magnitudes of Socioeconomic Impacts

### 2.1 Direct, indirect, and induced employment

Standard airport economic impact methodology (used by FAA, ACI-NA, and state aviation agencies) measures three impact channels: **direct** (on-airport jobs: airlines, concessions, ground handling, security, airport administration), **indirect** (supplier/supply-chain), and **induced** (re-spending of wages), typically via IMPLAN/RIMS II input-output models [11][30].

Empirical benchmarks for airports at or near the 500,000-passenger scale:

- **Tri-Cities Airport (TRI), Tennessee/Virginia (~500K passengers/year)**: supported **1,661 jobs** and $233.3 million in regional economic impact in 2019 (including $80.6M payroll and $109.2M visitor spending); a 2024 update found ~**2,100 Tennessee jobs** and **$289 million** in economic activity [12][13].
- **Rapid City Regional Airport (RAP), South Dakota (338,458 enplanements in 2022, ≈677K total passengers)**: a November 2023 Grand Valley State University IMPLAN study found **2,877 jobs** and **$456 million** in annual economic activity for Pennington County, with a **$2.2 million annual fiscal impact** for county government. Breakdown: commercial visitors 1,439 jobs/$188M; GA visitors 543 jobs/$58M; on-airport operations 43 jobs/$4.6M; capital investment 56 jobs/$7.8M; on-airport business tenants 796 jobs/$196M [GVSU Seidman Center study, 2023].
- **Louisiana non-hub commercial airports (2024 data)**: Alexandria 2,203 jobs; Lake Charles 1,470; Monroe 988; Lafayette 3,090 — all in or near the target range, with the state's seven commercial airports supporting 65,116 jobs and $8.42 billion in output system-wide [15].
- **Tennessee region**: Elizabethton, Greeneville, and other GA/small airports in the same system each supported 74–180 jobs — an order of magnitude smaller than TRI, illustrating the step-change in impact between general aviation and 500K-passenger commercial airports [16].

### 2.2 GDP/GRP and output contributions

- Tri-Cities: $233M output (2019), $289M (2024) [12][13]
- Rapid City: $456M annual output in Pennington County [GVSU, 2023]
- Louisiana non-hubs: Alexandria's output, Monroe's, Lake Charles' run in the hundreds of millions [15]
- National context: ACI-NA's 2024 national study of all 487 U.S. commercial service airports found 12.8 million jobs, $619 billion payroll, and **$1.8 trillion in output** — but this is system-wide, dominated by hubs [11].

### 2.3 Tourism and visitor spending

Visitor spending is usually the largest single impact component at smaller airports. At Rapid City, commercial visitor spending ($153M direct) drove 1,439 jobs — half the airport's total jobs. Tri-Cities hosted 80,000+ visitors in 2019 and ~130,000 in 2024 [13][12]. The Tennessee statewide study found ~7 million out-of-state visitors traveling through the state's airports contributed $9 billion to the economy [16]. At larger airports the same pattern holds: Norfolk's ~900,000 visitors spent $620M+, supporting 8,460 direct jobs [20]. **The critical caveat** (see Section 7) is that visitor spending counts as an impact only if those visitors are incremental to the region — not diverted from other local destinations or "time-switchers."

### 2.4 Business formation and firm attraction

Econometric evidence connects air service to business formation:

- **Bilotkach (2015)**: adding service to one new destination created, on average, **223 jobs and 15 new business establishments** [17]
- **Bruekner (2003)**: a 10% increase in passenger traffic raises total employment by 0.9% and service employment by 1.1% [17]
- **McGraw (2020)**: airports in mid-sized/smaller cities contributed to ~**3.9% employment growth and 3.4% population growth per decade** (1950–2010) [17]
- **Blonigen & Cristea (2015)**: a 50% increase in the passenger growth rate yields ~3.2% higher annual per-capita income growth and 5.5% higher employment growth [17]
- Tri-Cities' 28 on-site companies employing 400+ people, and its foreign trade zone/customs clearance enabling international corporate flights, illustrate the facilitation role [12]

### 2.5 Tax revenue

Fiscal impacts at this scale are measurable but modest:

- Rapid City: $2.2M annual county fiscal impact [GVSU, 2023]
- For scale comparison, Norfolk (~4M passengers) generated $306M in total taxes (federal/state/local); MSP (37M passengers) generated ~$3.3B in taxes [20][22]

No rigorous public study isolates tax revenue at exactly 500K passengers, but linear interpolation between Rapid City (~$2.2M local) and larger airports suggests single-digit millions annually in local tax revenue — meaningful for a small county, negligible for a state.

### 2.6 Trade, cargo, and logistics

Even modest airports support logistics value:

- Tennessee regional airports: cargo/freight supports **82,000 jobs and $19B** statewide; Smyrna Airport (a GA/corporate airport) tripled its economic impact in nine years hosting 60+ business tenants [25]
- Tri-Cities offers foreign trade zone status and customs clearance for international cargo [12]
- Hamilton, Canada (~smaller than target) demonstrates how cargo can dominate: 2,770 cargo jobs and $1.0B in activity from a single express-cargo carrier hub [24]

### 2.7 Connectivity, accessibility, and agglomeration

- **Bilotkach**: each additional nonstop destination → 223 jobs, 15 establishments [17]
- **CAC national study**: "a single regional flight can support up to 210 jobs and generate $41.2 million in economic output" [18]
- Connectivity links are strongest for professional/scientific/technical services, finance, corporate headquarters, IT, and advanced manufacturing — sectors that disproportionately use air travel [17]
- Sheard (2014): a 10% increase in air traffic generates 1,650 additional service jobs in a metro of 1 million residents, but **no measurable effect on manufacturing or non-tradable services** [17]
- ACI World: a 10% increase in airport connectivity yields 4.7% more FDI, 0.5% GDP growth, and ~1% employment growth in emerging economies [27][28]

### 2.8 Real estate, residential values, and wider spillovers

The evidence base at this scale is thinner, but agglomeration effects exist: ACI World cites Oxford Economics' finding that a US$100 per capita increase in airport infrastructure investment over 9 years is associated with 50 more passenger arrivals per capita (across 50 countries) [27][28]. The National Academies (2025) documents that 75% of passengers prioritize airport proximity when choosing where to fly, and that losing the local airport would raise "full price of travel" (out-of-pocket + time costs) by **172% if using alternative transport modes** and **19% if using the next-nearest airport** for non-hub airports [29]. Halpern (2011) found in Norway that 70% of remote-region residents say the local airport increases their likelihood of staying in the region [39].

## 3. Comparison with Larger Airports: Economies of Scale

Impacts per passenger are useful for scaling comparison. Converting case studies to approximate jobs per million total passengers:

| Airport | Passengers | Total jobs supported | Jobs per million passengers |
|---|---|---|---|
| Rapid City (RAP) | ~677K | 2,877 | ~4,250 |
| Norfolk (ORF) | ~4.0M | 17,320 | ~4,330 |
| Grand Rapids (GRR) | 3.47M | 40,000 (13-county region) | ~11,500 |
| MSP (2016) | 37.5M | 86,900 | ~2,320 |

The comparison shows that **per-million-passenger regional job support is broadly comparable between a ~600K airport and a 4M airport** (≈4,200–4,300 jobs per million), because visitor-spending multipliers scale roughly proportionally. What differs is absolute magnitude and productivity: Deloitte Access Economics found Australia's major airports generate $1.2 million in value-added per direct employee versus a national average of $176,300 — i.e., large airports show much higher value-added per job because their employment mix skews toward high-value airline hubs, international services, and cargo operations [43]. Large airports also enjoy better cost recovery: Oxera's analysis for ACI EUROPE found that airports generally need **500,000 to 1 million passengers per year to break even**, with the European Commission's own 2020 fitness check finding that >30% of airports between 200,000–700,000 passengers and 62% below 200,000 were unlikely to cover operating costs [42].

ACRP Research Report 218 provides the most rigorous size-specific coefficient: **each departing seat at a small hub airport is associated with 0.005 regional jobs, and at non-hub airports 0.0114** — i.e., non-hubs actually show more than twice the regional job-association per seat of small hubs, reflecting the greater economic weight of a marginal flight in a small community [17]. Applying the non-hub coefficient to ~250,000 enplanements (~300,000–360,000 departing seats at typical load factors) implies roughly **1,500–4,000 associated area jobs** — but these are statistical associations, not proven causal effects.

## 4. Measurement Approaches: Strengths and Limitations

### 4.1 Standard methodologies

The dominant approach is **input-output (I-O) multiplier analysis** using IMPLAN, RIMS II, or national accounts matrices, executed through FAA-accepted models such as ACI-NA's national economic impact model [11]. State studies follow a standardized template: airport manager surveys, passenger intercept surveys, tenant employment inventories, capital-expenditure averaging, and visitor-spending estimation [30][15].

The Texas A&M Transportation Institute's Web-Based Small Airport Economic Impact Model (2020) documents best practice for small airports, explicitly choosing I-O "due to its practicality, lower data requirements compared to econometric analysis, and verifiable numbers compared to expert surveys" [30]. Louisiana's 2025 study (IMPLAN, 68 airports, 74% manager survey response, 7,000+ passenger surveys) and Tennessee's 2019 study (IMPLAN v3 with 3,607 passenger surveys) are exemplars of the method at this scale [15][16].

### 4.2 Econometric and quasi-experimental approaches (causal evidence)

I-O models cannot establish causation. The causal literature uses panel econometrics and natural experiments:

- **Pot & Koster (2022)**, 274 European NUTS-2 regions, 2000–2018: long-run elasticity of GDP per capita to air accessibility = **0.106 overall; 0.179 large airports; 0.033 medium; 0.022 small**. Causality runs mainly from economic growth to accessibility, "especially considering smaller airports" [38].
- **Tveter (2017)**, Norway's nine regional airports constructed 1970–72, difference-in-differences: positive but **statistically insignificant** effects on population (point estimate +5%) and employment [46].
- **Gibbons & Wu (2020)**, China's airport expansion as quasi-experiment: airports improve local economic performance through market access, but effects are concentrated where airports deliver meaningful access gains [45].
- **ACRP's synthesis** of this literature concludes "air travel can be a positive, causal trigger" for regional economies — but the strongest elasticities come from large-hub connectivity, not 500K-passenger airports [17].

### 4.3 Marginal vs. average impacts

The National Academies (2025) makes a key methodological distinction: commissioned studies report **average impacts** (total jobs/output attributed to the airport). **Marginal impacts** (the effect of adding one more flight, destination, or enplanement) are "typically smaller than average impacts." For Austin, adding one additional domestic nonstop destination was modeled to add $72.7M to regional GDP; one international destination, $341.3M [29]. This distinction matters: a 500K airport's marginal impact per added flight is small, even though its average attributed impact is large.

## 5. Is "Significant" Achievable? Benchmarking Against Regional Economies

"Significant" must be judged relative to the regional economy served. A 500,000-passenger airport typically serves a catchment of 200,000–1,000,000 people (e.g., Tri-Cities MSA ~550,000; Rapid City/Pennington County ~150,000):

- **Rapid City**: 2,877 jobs in a county labor force of roughly 70,000–80,000 represents **~3.5–4% of county employment** — economically significant by any standard, especially given the $2.2M fiscal benefit to a small county budget [GVSU, 2023].
- **Tri-Cities**: 1,661–2,100 jobs in an MSA of ~550,000 (labor force ~250,000) represents **~0.7–0.8%** — moderate but clearly measurable [12][13].
- **ACRP 218 coefficients**: 1,500–4,000 associated jobs would be **1–3% of a regional labor force of 130,000–250,000** [17].

By contrast, the same airport in a metropolitan area of 2+ million with a competing large hub within a 2-hour drive would be economically negligible — precisely the situation the European Court of Auditors criticized in its 2014 audit (see Section 7). **Significance is therefore a function of regional size, remoteness, and the absence of substitutes, not of the passenger number alone.**

## 6. Regional Context: When Impacts Are Larger or Smaller

Evidence from multiple studies shows the same airport size produces very different impacts depending on context:

- **Remote/peripheral regions**: Pot & Koster found small airports provide the main air accessibility for only 3% of the European population, but those regions depend on them most [38]. Halpern's Norwegian study found stronger location/retention effects in remote regions (75% of Sør-Helgeland residents vs. 63% in Sunnmøre say the airport increases likelihood of staying; health-related travel is significantly more frequent in remote areas) [39].
- **Tourism-dependent regions**: In Crete, tourism is >30% of local GDP and 80% of airport traffic arrives in the May–October season — airport impacts are large but seasonal and driven by tourist spending [33]. The same logic applies to many rural U.S. airports in the 500K range (e.g., Rapid City's proximity to Mt. Rushmore/the Black Hills drives its visitor-dominated impact profile).
- **Industrial base**: Air service links most strongly to professional services, finance, corporate headquarters, IT, and advanced manufacturing; manufacturing and non-tradables show no measurable response (Sheard 2014) [17]. An airport serving a logistics hub (e.g., Tri-Cities' foreign trade zone) captures more trade-related impact than an airport in a bedroom community.
- **Competing airports**: The European Court of Auditors found access to another airport within a 2-hour drive suppressed demand at subsidized Spanish airports (Córdoba, Burgos) [41]. The UK CAA's Stansted analysis found 68% of passengers come from districts overlapping another London airport's catchment [43]. In multi-airport systems, small airports' impacts are largely redistributive, not generative [32][44].
- **Dense vs. sparse regions**: Pot & Koster found "dense regions with lagging per capita GDP levels benefit especially from air accessibility" — i.e., the same airport generates more growth where there is density and economic dynamism to begin with [38].

## 7. Contrary Evidence and Methodological Caveats

### 7.1 Causal vs. correlational: The strongest critique

The most damaging evidence for the hypothesis comes from studies designed to isolate causation:

- **Pot & Koster (2022)**: small-airport elasticity of GDP per capita is 0.022 — **one-eighth the elasticity of large airports (0.179)** — and Granger causality tests indicate regional growth drives small-airport traffic more than the reverse [38].
- **Tveter (2017)**: Norwegian regional airport construction had no statistically detectable effect on municipal population or employment over a decade — despite the airports being new infrastructure (the cleanest quasi-experimental design available) [46].
- **AICR synthesis acknowledges the "chicken or egg" conundrum**: "whether air service attracts economic activity or whether growing metropolitan areas cause airlines to add service" [17].

### 7.2 Attribution, displacement, and substitution

John Crompton's classic critiques of economic impact analysis apply directly to airport studies: including local residents' spending (which is recycling, not new money), including "time-switchers" and "casuals" who would have visited anyway, using employment multipliers that assume full utilization of existing staff, and ignoring costs (congestion, public safety, environmental) [35][36]. In airport terms:

- **Leakage**: dollars re-spent outside the region reduce multiplier capture; the National Academies defines this explicitly [29].
- **Displacement**: an airport may shift traffic from a nearby airport or mode rather than create it. The UK CAA found the vast majority of Stansted passengers could have used another London airport [43]. Hess (2010) found travelers actually **prefer larger airports**, paying up to $72 more to fly from a major airport and up to $76 to avoid municipal airports — meaning small airports in multi-airport regions may primarily serve convenience, not generate new demand [40].
- **Surface substitution**: The Taxpayers for Common Sense analysis of 38 EAS communities within 150 miles of a hub found subsidized flights cost $427 per round trip versus $136 for equivalent coach bus service including time costs — a 68% social saving [53]. Where ground alternatives exist, air service's marginal contribution is much smaller than I-O models assume.
- **SPARA toolkit guidance**: "displacement and deadweight must always be considered to derive net from gross impacts"; local multiplier adjustment for leakage can halve supply-chain effects (22 direct FTEs → 16.6 indirect in Scotland, but only 8.3 in the local area) [37].

### 7.3 Financial sustainability: Loss-making at this scale

Audit and regulatory evidence shows many airports at or below 500K passengers are subsidy-dependent:

- **European Court of Auditors (2014)**: Audited 20 EU-funded airports (€666M of funding). Nearly half of audited funds went into oversized infrastructure; 9 of 20 airports had unnecessary projects; only 4 of 20 were profitable; airports under 100,000 passengers lost an average **€130 per passenger** (Kastoria lost €275/passenger). Traffic forecasts were over-optimistic at 12 of 20 airports. The Court found "little evidence of... regional socio-economic benefits such as additional job creation at most airports" [41].
- **Oxera/ACI EUROPE (2024)**: the break-even range is generally **500,000–1,000,000 passengers**; the EC's fitness check found >30% of airports between 200,000–700,000 passengers unlikely to cover operating costs [42].
- **EU State aid policy**: The 2026 draft aviation guidelines end operating aid for airports above 1M passengers (transitional) and ultimately permit aid only below 500,000 passengers — an explicit policy judgment that airports above this scale should be commercially viable [41-related; CMS analysis of draft guidelines].
- **ACI EUROPE (2024)**: small airports (<1M passengers) remained **34.5% below 2019 traffic**, the slowest-recovering segment, growing only 5% [49].
- **GAO (2024)**: among 218 small communities, average departures fell 14% from 2018–2023, connectivity fell 8%, and small-community airports increasingly rely on incentives (67% of small hubs and 48% of non-hubs offer marketing assistance; 75%/40% offer fee waivers) [26].
- **EAS program**: per-passenger subsidies reach $1,905 (Lewistown, MT); the FY2027 budget proposal sought to cut EAS discretionary funding by ~54%, citing "half-empty" flights [8]. More than 100 of 177 EAS communities are designated Economic Development Areas — implying the airports themselves have not generated self-sustaining economies.

### 7.4 Overstatement in commissioned studies

- Crompton (2006) documents that "most economic impact studies are commissioned to legitimize a political position rather than to search for economic truth," with a catalog of "mischievous practices" — including a sports example where a claimed 1,170 full-time jobs came from an organization with 71 full-time employees [35].
- The National Academies' marginal-vs-average distinction shows that standard airport studies systematically report larger numbers than credible marginal analysis [29].
- McNab (2024) shows transportation economic projections commonly overstate benefits by summing subnational projections that exceed national totals and ignoring displacement [47].
- Even the GAO notes that federal incentive programs and local subsidies (marketing support, fee waivers) rather than organic demand sustain service at many small airports [26].

## 8. Conclusion and Verdict

**The hypothesis is supported — with qualifications.**

**Supported because:**

1. A ~500,000-passenger airport (~250,000 enplanements) is a robustly classified commercial service airport — an FAA non-hub primary — 25× above primary threshold and far above all service-viability minimums [2][3][4].
2. Multiple case studies at this exact scale document **measurable impacts**: Rapid City (2,877 jobs, $456M output, $2.2M county fiscal impact), Tri-Cities (1,661–2,100 jobs, $233–289M output), and Louisiana non-hubs (988–2,203 jobs each) [12][13][15][GVSU 2023].
3. These impacts are **locally significant** in small- and mid-sized regional economies (≈1–4% of regional employment), and are detectable via standard, FAA-accepted I-O methodologies [11][30].
4. Econometric elasticities confirm positive (if modest) associations between air service and employment, service-sector growth, and business formation [17].

**Qualified because:**

1. Causal evidence at this scale is weak: small-airport GDP elasticity is 0.022 (vs. 0.179 for large airports), and quasi-experimental studies find statistically insignificant effects [38][46].
2. Standard I-O studies overstate impacts by failing to fully account for leakage, displacement, substitution to other airports/modes, time-switcher visitors, and local-spending recycling [35][36][29].
3. Airports at this scale are frequently loss-making, subsidy-dependent, and slow to recover from shocks — the European audit experience and ACI EUROPE's traffic data are cautionary [41][42][49].
4. The magnitudes of benefits are highly context-dependent: the same airport that is a lifeline in rural South Dakota or island Greece would be economically marginal in a metropolitan region with a competing hub within a two-hour drive [38][39][41].

**Final assessment:** At 500,000 annual passengers, an airport produces impacts that are **real, measurable, and — in remote or small urban regions — significant in proportion to the local economy**. The impacts are smaller per passenger than at large hubs, and commissioned studies should be read with methodological skepticism. The fairest summary: the hypothesis holds for the right regional contexts, is questionable for congested multi-airport regions, and the true causal effect is almost certainly smaller than the headline numbers in airport-commissioned impact reports — though still nonzero and locally meaningful.
