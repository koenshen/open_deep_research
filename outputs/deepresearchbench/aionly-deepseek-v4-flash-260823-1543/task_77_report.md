# The Role of Need for Closure in Misinformation Acceptance: A Comprehensive Review

## Introduction

The Need for Closure (NFC), also referred to as the Need for Cognitive Closure (NFCC or NCC), is a psychological construct introduced by Arie Kruglanski that describes a fundamental epistemic motivation: the desire for "an answer on a given topic, any answer… compared to confusion and ambiguity" [1]. This desire for certainty and aversion to ambiguity has profound implications for how individuals process information, evaluate evidence, and form beliefs—making it a critical variable in understanding susceptibility to misinformation.

This report synthesizes the existing empirical evidence and theoretical frameworks to answer the central question: How does an individual's need for closure influence their susceptibility to believing, accepting, or sharing misinformation? The review covers direct effects, mediating mechanisms, moderating factors, and the theoretical architectures that explain these relationships.

## Conceptual Foundation: The Need for Closure Construct

### Definition and Measurement

The Need for Closure Scale (NFCS) was developed by Webster and Kruglanski (1994) as a 42-item instrument measuring five facets: Preference for Order, Preference for Predictability, Decisiveness, Discomfort with Ambiguity, and Closed-mindedness [2]. The scale shows high internal consistency (Cronbach's α = .84) and test-retest reliability (r = .86) [2]. A revised 41-item version was provided by Roets and Van Hiel (2007) to address issues with the decisiveness items, and a validated 15-item brief version was subsequently developed [3][4].

### The Seizing and Freezing Framework

The foundational theoretical framework, articulated in Kruglanski and Webster's (1996) seminal paper "Motivated Closing of the Mind," identifies two core tendencies [5]:

- **Urgency tendency (Seizing):** The inclination to attain closure as soon as possible by latching onto the first available answer that resolves uncertainty
- **Permanence tendency (Freezing):** The inclination to maintain closure for as long as possible by resisting new information that might reopen the question

"People under a heightened need for closure may seize on information appearing early in a sequence and freeze on it, becoming impervious to subsequent data. Paradoxically, they may feel more assured of those judgments, even though they are less grounded in thorough exploration" [5].

### Situational and Dispositional Sources

NFC can arise from two sources: as a stable personality trait (measured by the NFCS) or as a situationally evoked state induced by time pressure, fatigue, ambient noise, alcoholic intoxication, or difficult processing conditions [6]. The construct shows low-to-moderate positive correlations with authoritarianism (r = .27), dogmatism (r = .29), and intolerance of ambiguity (r = .29), and negative correlations with need for cognition (r = -.28) and cognitive complexity (r = -.30) [2].

## Direct Effects of NFC on Misinformation Acceptance

### Evidence from Correlational and Longitudinal Studies

**Jedinger and Masch (2025)** — This study, published in *Frontiers in Social Psychology*, used data from the representative GESIS Panel in Germany (N = 2,883) with NFC measured pre-pandemic (2018-2019) and COVID-19 conspiracy beliefs measured during the second wave of the pandemic (August-October 2020). The key finding was that "individuals with a high need for cognitive closure are slightly more likely to endorse COVID-19 conspiracy theories, but the effect size is small (β = 0.08, p < 0.001 in regression models)" [7]. Political trust showed much stronger negative associations (β = -0.33 to -0.51, p < 0.001). The authors conclude that "individual differences in epistemic needs play a relatively marginal role compared to political trust" [7].

**Marchlewska, Cichocka, and Kossowska (2017)** — Two experiments (N = 245; N = 455) demonstrated that the NFC-conspiracy belief relationship is highly context-dependent. In Experiment 1, when conspiratorial explanations for the EU refugee relocation program were made salient, NFC positively predicted conspiracy beliefs (B = 0.97, p < .001 in the conspiracy condition vs. B = 0.29, p = .20 in the control). In Experiment 2, a three-way interaction emerged (B = -0.22, p = .03): NFC was positively (marginally) related to conspiracy beliefs for an uncertain event (B = 0.39, p = .08) but negatively related for a certain event (B = -0.44, p = .04). The authors conclude that "people high (vs. low) in NFCC seize on conspiratorial explanations for uncertain events when such explanations are situationally accessible," but freeze on official explanations when those are available [8].

**Hejniak et al. (2019)** — Two experiments using an eyewitness paradigm found that "the misinformation effect was replicated and a positive correlation between the need for cognitive closure and susceptibility to misinformation was established" [9]. The second experiment demonstrated that high NFC is associated with a greater tendency to make false confessions [9].

**Leman and Cinnirella (2013)** — Two experimental studies (N = 30; N = 86) found no direct relationship between NFC and belief in conspiracy theories in Study 1. However, in Study 2, when NFC was experimentally lowered via accountability instructions, "the effects of prior beliefs in conspiracy theories diminished" [10].

**Cosgrove (2026)** — Across two studies (N = 354; N = 306), this research found that "narcissism, and less consistently need for cognitive closure, offset the negative effects of education on generic conspiracy beliefs and susceptibility to misinformation" [11]. The benefits of education disappeared at just one standard deviation above the mean on these traits, supporting a framework of "epistemic-social motivated reasoning" [11].

### Summary of Direct Effect Sizes

The direct effect of NFC on misinformation acceptance is generally small to moderate, context-dependent, and often weaker than other factors. The effect is strongest when:
- Conspiratorial explanations are salient and situationally accessible
- Events are uncertain or ambiguous
- Official explanations are absent or untrusted

## Mediating Mechanisms: How NFC Leads to Misinformation Acceptance

### 1. Reduced Depth of Information Processing

The most well-established mechanism linking NFC to misinformation acceptance is the reduction of systematic, analytic processing. Kruglanski's framework predicts that high NFC leads to "fewer hypothesis generations, increased subjective confidence, greater reliance on early cues (primacy effects, anchoring), and stronger use of stereotypes" [5].

**Swami et al. (2014)** — Across four studies (N = 990; N = 112; N = 189; N = 140), this research found that "stronger belief in conspiracy theories was significantly associated with lower analytic thinking and open-mindedness, and greater intuitive thinking" [12]. A verbal fluency task that elicited analytic thinking reduced belief in conspiracy theories (d = 0.46), and cognitive disfluency as an alternative method yielded similar effects (d = 0.49) [12].

**Edelson et al. (2024)** — This *Annual Review of Developmental Psychology* article reports that "cognitive ability, analytical thinking style, numeracy, and metacognitive scrutiny are protective against misinformation" [13]. The review notes that "Actively open-minded thinking (AOT) and Cognitive Reflection Test (CRT) performance predict better truth discernment" [13].

**Modirrousta-Galian and Higham (2024)** — This systematic individual participant data meta-analysis (N = 11,561; 256,337 choices) found that "higher analytical thinking skills are linked to higher discrimination ability and a false-news bias (caution)" [14].

**Pennycook and Rand (2018)** — Across three studies (N = 3,446), this research found that "CRT performance was negatively correlated with perceived accuracy of fake news and positively correlated with the ability to discern fake from real news, regardless of political ideology alignment" [15]. The authors conclude that susceptibility to fake news is driven more by "lazy thinking" (failure to engage analytic reasoning) than by partisan bias per se [15].

**Mechanism summary:** NFC reduces the depth of information processing by promoting shallow, rapid processing and reducing systematic/analytic thinking. The "seizing" mechanism causes individuals to grab onto the first available closure-providing information, bypassing critical evaluation. The protective effect of analytic thinking against misinformation is well-established, and NFC directly undermines this protective mechanism.

### 2. Increased Reliance on Cognitive Heuristics

**Kossowska and Bar-Tal (2012)** — This study, published in the *British Journal of Psychology*, found that "there were positive relationships between NFC and heuristic information processing when participants assessed themselves as being able to use cognitive strategies consistent with their level of NFC (high AAC [Ability to Achieve Closure])" [16].

**The Illusory Truth Effect** — Multiple sources establish that repetition increases belief in information, an effect driven by processing fluency. A review in *Current Opinion in Psychology* notes that "repetition increases belief in information" and "repetition even increases belief in claims that are implausible or that contradict prior knowledge" [17]. A meta-analysis found the illusory truth effect has a medium effect size (d = 0.53) [18]. The relationship between repetition frequency and perceived truth is logarithmic, "meaning that each additional repetition has a smaller effect on perceived truth than the one before it" [17].

Critically, research suggests that "the illusory truth effect is such a robust phenomenon that it operates independently of cognitive ability. Neither differences in cognitive ability, in need for closure, nor in cognitive thinking style influence the power of repeated statements to appear true" [19]. However, this conclusion may be nuanced by the finding that "even experts in an area of knowledge are vulnerable to the illusory effect" due to "knowledge neglect" [19].

**Mechanism summary:** NFC increases reliance on cognitive heuristics because high-NFC individuals are motivated to achieve closure quickly, and heuristic processing provides a faster route to closure than systematic processing. The illusory truth effect operates independently of individual differences in NFC, meaning all individuals are susceptible, but NFC may amplify the tendency to rely on these heuristic cues rather than engaging in deeper verification.

### 3. Epistemic Trust and Mistrust

The relationship between NFC and epistemic trust in misinformation contexts is complex and somewhat paradoxical.

**Jedinger and Masch (2025)** — This study found that "pre-pandemic trust and concurrent trust in political and medical institutions are strongly negatively related to conspiracy beliefs" (β = -0.33 and β = -0.51 respectively, p < 0.001), while NFC showed only a small positive effect (β = 0.08) [7]. Importantly, "no support was found for the hypothesized moderating effect of political trust on the NCC-conspiracy relationship," suggesting NFC and trust operate as independent pathways [7].

**Epistemic Mistrust and Credulity** — Research published in *Research in Psychotherapy* (December 2023, N = 417) found that "epistemic mistrust was significantly associated with conspiracy mentality, even when controlling for other factors" [20]. A second study in *PLOS Global Public Health* (December 2024, N = 705; N = 502) found that "credulity was associated with reduced ability to discriminate between fake and real news" and "both Mistrust and Credulity were associated with general and COVID-19 related conspiracy beliefs and vaccine hesitancy" [21].

**Schmidt-Boddy et al. (2026)** — This article in *Human Studies* examines how "structural and phenomenological features of online environments can create epistemic scenarios that increase susceptibility to conspiratory mentalities and corresponding epistemic trust dynamics" [22].

**Mechanism summary:** NFC does not appear to directly increase trust in low-credibility sources. Rather, epistemic mistrust (rejection of authorized sources) is a stronger predictor of conspiracy beliefs and misinformation acceptance than NFC. However, NFC may interact with epistemic trust: high-NFC individuals who are epistemically mistrustful of mainstream sources may be particularly likely to seize on alternative explanations that provide closure. The mediating role of epistemic credulity—accepting information without sufficient discrimination—is also important, as it reduces the ability to distinguish fake from real news.

### 4. Motivated Reasoning and Identity-Protective Cognition

**Kahan (2013)** — Using a nationally representative sample of 1,750 U.S. adults, this study found that "subjects who scored highest in cognitive reflection were the most likely to display ideologically motivated cognition" [23]. The findings support the view that ideologically motivated cognition is "a form of information processing that promotes individuals' interests in forming and maintaining beliefs that signify their loyalty to important affinity groups" [23].

**Van Bavel and Pereira (2018)** — This article in *Trends in Cognitive Sciences* proposes an "identity-based model of political belief" arguing that "when these identity goals are stronger than our accuracy goals, they lead us to believe in fake news, propaganda, and other misinformation" [24].

**Cosgrove (2026)** — The proposed framework of "epistemic-social motivated reasoning" describes how "education-related abilities can be redirected towards underlying psychological and social needs (certainty, distinctiveness, and superiority) rather than accuracy goals" [11]. NFC (certainty) is one of the three needs that can redirect reasoning away from accuracy.

**Szebeni, Lönnqvist, and Jasinskaja-Lahti (2021)** — This study in Hungary (N = 295) found that "the only psychological predisposition that consistently explained belief in all types of fake news was a conspiracy mentality," supporting "the notion of ideological symmetry in fake news belief" [25].

**Mechanism summary:** NFC can drive motivated reasoning in misinformation processing by creating a preference for closure that aligns with identity-protective goals. When individuals are motivated to reach conclusions that affirm their group identity, high NFC may amplify the tendency to seize on identity-congruent information and freeze on those beliefs, rejecting contradictory evidence. However, the relationship is not straightforward—higher cognitive reflection can actually increase motivated reasoning, and NFC effects are context-dependent.

### 5. The Seizing and Freezing Mechanisms in Action

The seizing and freezing mechanisms are central to understanding how NFC leads to misinformation acceptance. The urgency tendency causes high-NFC individuals to quickly grab onto the first available explanation that provides closure for an uncertain event. The permanence tendency then causes these individuals to resist updating their beliefs even when contradictory evidence emerges.

**Marchlewska et al. (2017)** provides the most direct evidence for these mechanisms in the misinformation context: "people high (vs. low) in NFCC seize on conspiratorial explanations for uncertain events when such explanations are situationally accessible" [8]. Conversely, when official explanations exist, those high in NFCC "reject salient conspiracy theories, freezing on the available non-conspiratorial information" (B = -0.44, p = .04) [8].

**Growiec and Szumowska (2026)** — Published in *Scientific Reports*, this research found that higher NFC is consistently associated with lower heterophily (fewer interactions with dissimilar others). A meta-analysis confirmed a significant negative association (β = -0.20, p < .001). In the experimental study (N = 238), "under uncertainty, participants showed a stronger preference for demographically similar others. This effect was moderated by NFC: the uncertainty manipulation did not affect low-NFC individuals, but among high-NFC individuals, uncertainty significantly increased preference for similar others" [26]. This creates echo chambers that reinforce "frozen" beliefs.

## Moderating Factors

### 1. Cognitive Ability and Working Memory Capacity

**Working Memory Capacity (WMC)** — Czernatowicz-Kukuczka, Jaśko, and Kossowska (2014, *Personality and Individual Differences*, N = 124) found that WMC moderates the relationship between the Behavioral Inhibition System (BIS) and NFC in decision-making. The mediating effect of NFC only occurs for individuals with high WMC, meaning that "cognitive resources are necessary to successfully fulfill the epistemic goal of achieving closure" [27].

**NFC and Cognitive Effort** — A PLOS ONE study (2015, N = 60) found that "task ambiguity increased cognitive effort only in low and medium NFC participants, but not in high NFC participants" [28]. Conversely, high outcome relevance increased cognitive effort in medium and high NFC participants, but not in low NFC participants. This suggests high-NFC individuals are more extrinsically motivated (responsive to monetary incentives) while low-NFC individuals are more intrinsically motivated (responsive to task difficulty) [28].

**Education** — Cosgrove (2026) found that the protective effect of education against conspiracy beliefs and misinformation susceptibility is offset by high NFC, narcissism, and need for uniqueness. The benefits of education "disappeared at just one standard deviation above the mean on these traits" [11].

**Ability to Achieve Closure (AAC)** — Bar-Tal and Kossowska (2010) introduced the concept of "Efficacy at Fulfilling the Need for Closure (EFNC)," defined as the extent to which individuals perceive themselves capable of using information processing methods consistent with their level of NFC. EFNC moderates the effect of NFC on epistemic freezing behavior [29].

### 2. Political Ideology and Political Trust

**Political Trust** — Jedinger and Masch (2025) found that political trust does NOT moderate the NFC-conspiracy belief relationship. The interaction was not significant, suggesting NFC and trust operate as independent pathways [7].

**Ideological Motivated Reasoning** — Kahan (2013) found that conservatives did not score lower than liberals on the Cognitive Reflection Test, and that subjects who scored highest on CRT were the most likely to display ideologically motivated cognition, contradicting the view that political polarization stems from a deficit in reflective reasoning [23].

**Ideological Symmetry** — Szebeni et al. (2021) found that conspiracy mentality explains belief in all types of fake news across the political spectrum, supporting ideological symmetry in misinformation susceptibility [25].

### 3. Source Credibility

**Source Credibility Effects** — A systematic review of 91 studies (64,162 participants, 162 effects) found that source credibility effects in misinformation contexts are "highly inconsistent (47.53% significant, 40.12% non-significant, 12.35% mixed)" [30]. Effects are more consistent when credibility is measured versus manipulated, and more consistently linked to cognitive outcomes (e.g., perceived accuracy) than behavioral outcomes (e.g., sharing intentions) [30].

**Source Discreditation** — Research published in *Cognitive Research: Principles and Implications* (2024) found that both correction and source discreditation individually reduce misinformation reliance, but the combined intervention was the most effective, reducing misinformation reliance to control levels [31].

**The "Break in the Message" Effect** — Dolinski, Dolinska, and Bar-Tal (2016, *Frontiers in Psychology*, N = 103) found that the media practice of splitting news into a headline followed by a delayed elaboration interacts with NFC. For high-NFC individuals, the break significantly increased negative moral judgments (b = 1.39, p < 0.01), while for low-NFC individuals the effect was non-significant (b = 0.50, p = ns). The interaction effect was significant (ΔR² = 0.08, p < 0.01) [32].

### 4. Emotional Arousal

**Fear and Anxiety** — A Polish study (N = 380) found that belief in COVID-19 conspiracy theories fully mediated the relationship between coronavirus fear and two dimensions of NFC: avoidance of ambiguity and closed-mindedness. A partial mediation was found for the need for predictability. The model explained 22% of the variance in coronavirus fear. The authors suggest that "conspiracy theories, by providing a stable and understandable framework, can offer a false sense of security and familiarity with an uncertain situation" [33].

**BIS and NFC** — Czernatowicz-Kukuczka et al. (2014) found that NFC works as a "motivational mechanism that enables individuals high in BIS to deal with uncertainty; therefore, NFC mediates the effect of BIS on behavior in a decision-making situation" [27]. High NFC may be adaptive for high BIS individuals because it leads to behavioral strategies that decrease the anxiety experienced in the face of uncertainty.

**Emotion and Misinformation (General)** — Edelson et al. (2024) report that "heightened emotional reactions are associated with worse truth discernment" [13].

### 5. Time Pressure and Situational Factors

**Time Pressure and NFC** — Zhang, Chen, and Wang (2025, *Acta Psychologica*, N = 91) found a significant interaction between NFC and time pressure on risky decision-making. For low-NFC individuals, risky behavior was significantly higher in the absence of time pressure than in its presence. For high-NFC individuals, time pressure did not significantly affect behavior [34].

**Time Pressure and Misinformation** — Batailler et al. (2022, *Scientific Reports*, N = 760) found that time pressure reduced discrimination ability—overall accuracy dropped from 72.7% (control) to 69.6% (time pressure), with a credible negative effect (β = -0.26). Time pressure did not alter response bias. The effect of motivated reflection (CRT × ideological congruency) was credible only in the control treatment, not under time pressure [35].

### 6. Cultural Factors

**Cross-Cultural Differences** — A study comparing the U.S. and China (N = 745 college students) found that "trust in government and attitudes toward preventive measures mediated the relationship between NFC and pandemic compliance behaviors among Chinese participants, but not U.S. participants" [36]. NFC predicted better mental health outcomes among participants in China compared to U.S. participants [36].

**Cultural Differences in Misinformation Correction** — Al-Saggaf and O'Donnell (2024, *Behaviour & Information Technology*, N = 462) found that Arab participants demonstrated significantly higher willingness to challenge misinformation (M = 4.70) compared to UK participants (M = 3.30). Different factors predicted willingness in each cultural context: injunctive norms were the sole significant predictor in both samples, but relationship costs emerged as a significant predictor only for Arab participants [37].

## Theoretical Frameworks

### 1. Lay Epistemic Theory (Kruglanski)

Lay epistemic theory is the foundational framework describing the knowledge formation process, with the need for cognitive closure as the central variable determining the epistemic process [38]. The theory inspired three interrelated research programs: (1) the need for cognitive closure, (2) the unimodel of social judgment, and (3) epistemic authority [38][39].

The theory posits that the epistemic process involves hypothesis generation and validation, with NFC affecting both phases. The urgency tendency accelerates hypothesis generation (seizing), while the permanence tendency inhibits validation of alternative hypotheses (freezing) [5].

### 2. Dual-Process Models: ELM and HSM

**Elaboration Likelihood Model (ELM)** — Proposes two primary routes to persuasion: the central route (high elaboration, systematic processing of argument quality) and the peripheral route (low elaboration, reliance on simple cues). High NFC is typically associated with peripheral route processing [40].

**Heuristic-Systematic Model (HSM)** — Proposes two processing modes: heuristic (fast, cue-driven) and systematic (deliberate, content-based). The sufficiency principle explains that people seek the minimum cognitive effort needed to reach acceptable confidence levels. High NFC reduces the threshold for sufficient confidence, favoring heuristic processing [41].

A study applying the ELM to false news (N = 931 Taiwanese participants) found that "argument quality (central cue) negatively influenced belief in false news in both groups, enabling users to identify false messages," while "topical relevance positively contributed to belief in false news in the social media group" and "source credibility was associated with belief in false news in the SM group" [42].

### 3. The Unimodel of Persuasion

Proposed by Kruglanski and Thompson (1999) as an alternative to dual-process models, the unimodel argues that central/systematic and peripheral/heuristic routes are not qualitatively different processes but rather represent functionally equivalent types of evidence from which persuasive conclusions are drawn via the same underlying syllogistic reasoning process [43]. The unimodel reconceptualizes cues and message arguments as different contents of evidence relevant to a conclusion, rather than representing different processes.

### 4. Epistemic Motivation Theory

Epistemic motivation is the desire to develop and maintain a rich, thorough understanding of a situation. The need for cognitive closure is an epistemic motivation that advances knowledge formation and has widely ramifying consequences for individual, interpersonal, and group phenomena [38]. The HSM has been expanded to include defense-motivation and impression-motivation as additional processing motives beyond accuracy-seeking [41].

### 5. Motivated Reasoning Framework

Motivated reasoning describes how individuals access, construct, and evaluate their beliefs in response to new information where their biases cause them to arrive at desired conclusions. Two categories exist: accuracy-oriented (non-directional, seeking accurate conclusions) and goal-oriented (directional, seeking particular conclusions favored based on identity protection) [44].

Kahan (2013) identified ideologically motivated cognition as "a form of information processing that promotes individuals' interests in forming and maintaining beliefs that signify their loyalty to important affinity groups" [23]. Kruglanski, Jasko, and Friston (2020) argue in *Trends in Cognitive Science* that "all thinking is wishful thinking" and that the dichotomy between motivation and cognition is artificial [45].

## Empirical Methodologies and Evidence Types

### Experimental Paradigms

Researchers have manipulated NFC through several methods:
- **Time pressure:** Inducing urgency to reach closure [34][35]
- **Accountability instructions:** Lowering NFC by increasing the motivation to scrutinize evidence [10]
- **Uncertainty induction:** Creating ambiguous situations that trigger closure-seeking [26]
- **Informational helplessness training:** Manipulating the ability to achieve closure [27]

### Correlational and Longitudinal Designs

The GESIS Panel study (Jedinger & Masch, 2025) provides the strongest longitudinal evidence, with NFC measured pre-pandemic and conspiracy beliefs measured during the pandemic, demonstrating temporal precedence of NFC [7].

### Meta-Analytic Evidence

A meta-analytic systematic review of 279 independent studies (N = 137,406) found significant relationships between conspiracy beliefs and epistemic needs (r = .14, k = 114), existential needs (r = .16, k = 121), and social needs (r = .16, k = 100). Moderation analyses indicated that relationships were weaker in experimental vs. correlational designs [46].

## Integration and Conclusion

The relationship between need for closure and misinformation acceptance is best understood as a context-dependent, multiply-mediated, and multiply-moderated phenomenon. The direct effect is generally small to moderate, with NFC explaining approximately 0.6% of variance in conspiracy beliefs (based on β = 0.08) [7]. However, this relatively small direct effect belies the importance of NFC as a factor that operates through multiple mechanisms and interacts with numerous contextual variables.

### Key Pathways

The central pathway from NFC to misinformation acceptance involves:
1. **Seizing and freezing:** High-NFC individuals seize on the first available closure-providing explanation and freeze on it, resisting contradictory evidence
2. **Reduced systematic processing:** NFC reduces analytic thinking, making individuals more vulnerable to accepting misinformation at face value
3. **Heuristic reliance:** NFC increases reliance on cognitive heuristics, including the illusory truth effect
4. **Motivated reasoning:** When combined with identity motivations, NFC amplifies the tendency to seek and maintain identity-congruent beliefs

### When NFC Matters Most

NFC is most consequential for misinformation acceptance when:
- Events are uncertain or ambiguous (no clear official explanation exists)
- Conspiratorial or closure-providing explanations are salient and situationally accessible
- Trust in official sources is low (epistemic mistrust)
- Time pressure or other situational constraints limit processing capacity
- Identity motivations are strong and aligned with available misinformation

### When NFC Matters Less

NFC is less consequential when:
- Official explanations are available and trusted
- Individuals have sufficient working memory capacity and cognitive resources
- The illusory truth effect operates independently of NFC
- Political trust (a much stronger predictor) is high

### Practical Implications

The research suggests that interventions to combat misinformation should:
1. Address epistemic trust in institutions, as this is a stronger predictor than individual differences in NFC
2. Promote analytic thinking, which protects against misinformation regardless of NFC level
3. Recognize that high-NFC individuals may be particularly resistant to corrections after freezing on initial beliefs
4. Provide closure through accurate, trusted sources before misinformation can fill the gap
5. Be aware that the benefits of education can be offset by high NFC combined with other psychological needs

### Limitations and Future Directions

The existing research has several limitations: most studies are correlational or cross-sectional, effect sizes are generally small, and the NFC-misinformation relationship is highly context-dependent. Future research should examine the interaction between NFC and other psychological needs (distinctiveness, superiority) as proposed by the epistemic-social motivated reasoning framework, and investigate how online environments affect the seizing and freezing mechanisms in real-world misinformation contexts.
