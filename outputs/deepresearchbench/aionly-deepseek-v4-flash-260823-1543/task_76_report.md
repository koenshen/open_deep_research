# The Gut Microbiota in Intestinal Health and Colorectal Cancer: A Comprehensive Evidence-Based Overview

## Introduction

The human gastrointestinal tract harbors a complex community of approximately 40 to 100 trillion microorganisms collectively termed the gut microbiota, which plays fundamental roles in maintaining intestinal homeostasis, modulating immune function, and mediating metabolic processes [1]. Disruption of microbial homeostasis—termed dysbiosis—has been implicated in the pathogenesis of intestinal inflammation and colorectal cancer (CRC), the third most common cancer and second leading cause of cancer-related deaths worldwide [1,2]. Conversely, probiotics and prebiotics have demonstrated the capacity to mitigate inflammation and retard the progression of colorectal carcinogenesis. This report provides a comprehensive, evidence-based overview addressing the predominant types of gut probiotics, the mechanistic roles of prebiotics, the pathogenic bacteria of greatest concern in colorectal inflammation and cancer, and how these findings can inform daily dietary choices for maintaining gut health and reducing CRC risk.

## 1. Predominant Types of Gut Probiotics and Evidence for Their Beneficial Roles

Probiotics are defined by the International Scientific Association for Probiotics and Prebiotics (ISAPP) as "live microorganisms that, when administered in adequate amounts, confer a health benefit on the host" [3]. The most extensively studied probiotic genera include *Lactobacillus*, *Bifidobacterium*, *Saccharomyces*, and *Streptococcus*, with strain-specific effects being a critical determinant of clinical outcomes.

### 1.1 *Lactobacillus rhamnosus* GG (LGG)

**Strain Background and Colonization:** *Lactobacillus rhamnosus* GG (LGG) is a Gram-positive bacterium originally isolated from healthy human intestines. Genomic analysis identified LGG-specific islands containing genes for pili (spaCBA) that mediate adhesion to intestinal mucus, and exopolysaccharide biosynthesis genes [4]. LGG has superior adherence to intestinal mucus due to unique pili encoded by the *spaCBA* gene cluster, surviving 1-2 weeks in the GI tract [4].

**Intestinal Barrier Function:** LGG produces several secreted proteins that protect the intestinal barrier. A landmark study identified HM0539, a novel secreted protein from LGG culture supernatant, which dose-dependently induced mucin production and MUC2 expression, protected Caco-2 monolayers from LPS- or TNF-α-induced barrier injury by preserving ZO-1, occludin, and MUC2 expression, and in DSS-induced colitis, prevented body weight loss, colon shortening, crypt loss, and leukocyte infiltration [5]. Two other LGG-derived proteins, p40 and p75, have been shown to prevent cytokine-induced intestinal epithelial damage and apoptosis through activation of the epidermal growth factor (EGF) receptor and its downstream target Akt [4].

**Anti-Inflammatory and Immune Modulation:** A comprehensive review identified four main mechanisms by which *L. rhamnosus* alleviates inflammatory bowel disease: (1) gut microbiota regulation—restoring microbiota structure by increasing beneficial bacteria (e.g., *Bifidobacterium*, *Akkermansia*) and reducing harmful bacteria; (2) intestinal barrier restoration—strengthening epithelial tight junctions and increasing expression of ZO-1, occludin, and E-cadherin; (3) immune function modulation—regulating Th17/Treg cell homeostasis, inhibiting pro-inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6), promoting anti-inflammatory IL-10, and blocking NF-κB activation; and (4) competitive exclusion of pathogenic bacteria through the pilus structure (SpaC, SpaFED) [6].

**Clinical Evidence:** A systematic review of LGG clinical use across 22 disease areas demonstrated that LGG monotherapy (6×10⁹–2×10¹⁰ CFU) reduced severe diarrhea during chemotherapy and improved *H. pylori* treatment tolerability; LGG was as effective as a low-FODMAP diet for diarrhea-predominant IBS; LGG (≥2×10⁹ CFU) reduced antibiotic-associated diarrhea risk (RR=0.49, NNT=9); high-dose LGG (10¹⁰ CFU/day) reduced rotavirus diarrhea duration by approximately 31 hours; and LGG improved liver enzymes in children with NAFLD and helped maintain glycemic control in healthy adults [4]. Notably, evidence does not support LGG for Crohn's disease, and it may increase relapse rates in this population [4].

### 1.2 *Lactobacillus acidophilus*

**Intestinal Barrier Function:** The LA1 strain of *L. acidophilus* has been shown to rapidly increase intestinal barrier function through attachment to the intestinal epithelial membrane via the toll-like receptor-2 (TLR-2) complex, activating pathways that tighten the intestinal barrier and trigger an immune response [7]. In vivo studies in mice confirmed that LA1 enhanced small intestine and colonic epithelial barriers, prevented colonic inflammation formation, and promoted colitis healing [7]. The LA1 effect on tight junction barrier function was shown to be mediated by the TLR-2/TLR-1 and TLR-2/TLR-6 heterodimeric complex, with protective effects against DSS-induced colitis abolished in TLR-2 knockout mice [8].

**Anti-Inflammatory Effects:** A 2019 study demonstrated that *L. acidophilus* significantly reduced IL-8 secretion and suppressed phosphorylated p65 NF-κB, phosphorylated p38 MAPK, VCAM-1, and COX-2 in HT-29 intestinal epithelial cells stimulated with LPS and TNF-α, while increasing TLR2 expression [9]. The combination of *L. acidophilus* and *Bifidobacterium animalis* subsp. *lactis* showed the strongest anti-inflammatory activity across all markers, suggesting synergistic effects [9].

### 1.3 *Lactobacillus casei* (particularly Shirota strain)

**Cancer Prevention and Colorectal Cancer Evidence:** *Lactobacillus casei* Shirota (LcS) has been shown to have potent antitumor and antimetastatic effects on transplantable tumor cells and to suppress chemically induced carcinogenesis in rodents [10]. A 4-year study of 398 subjects found LcS decreased recurrence of colonic polyps, and an intervention trial showed lower recurrence rates for superficial bladder cancer in subjects receiving freeze-dried LcS [10].

A systematic review of 21 studies identified four key mechanisms through which *L. casei* exerts anticancer effects against CRC: (1) apoptosis induction and antiproliferation—*L. casei* ATCC 393 reduced tumor volume by 63.1% in BALB/c mice, and *L. casei* BL23 completely prevented tumor development in 100% of treated mice versus 67% tumor incidence in controls; (2) immunomodulation—increased IFN-γ, IL-12, and Granzyme B production, enhanced CD8+ cytotoxic T cell infiltration into tumors (nearly 4-fold increase); (3) gut microbiome and intestinal barrier modulation—enriched beneficial genera (*Lactobacillus*, *Bifidobacterium*, *Akkermansia*, *Roseburia*) while reducing harmful taxa (*Fusobacterium nucleatum*, *Bacteroides*, *Clostridium*); and (4) detoxification of carcinogens—the DN-114001 strain demonstrated ability to adsorb colorectal carcinogens including IQ, MeIQx, and PhIP [11].

### 1.4 *Lactobacillus plantarum*

**Intestinal Barrier Function—Human RCT Evidence:** A randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled crossover trial in healthy volunteers investigated the effects of three *L. plantarum* strains on small intestinal barrier function. While none of the bacterial strains significantly affected intestinal permeability measurements, gene transcription analysis of duodenal biopsies revealed strain-specific effects: *L. plantarum* TIFN101 modulated pathways related to cell-cell adhesion, tight/adhesion junction protein turnover, integrin signaling, and mucin-2 gene upregulation, suggesting enhanced mucosal repair processes [12]. This study demonstrated that probiotic effects are highly strain-dependent even within the same species [12].

**Tight Junction Regulation:** *L. plantarum* induces apical relocalization of ZO-1 and occludin via TLR2 stimulation, and some strains increase transepithelial resistance by 60-235% [8]. *L. plantarum* MB452 has been shown to enhance the function of the intestinal barrier by improving the expression of tight junction proteins and reducing intestinal permeability [13].

### 1.5 *Bifidobacterium* Species

**Genus Overview:** The genus *Bifidobacterium* belongs to the Actinobacteria phylum and contains over 50 species. Bifidobacteria are among the first microbial colonizers of the intestines of newborns and play key roles in the development of physiology, including maturation of the immune system [14]. Common human-associated species include *B. adolescentis*, *B. breve*, *B. longum*, *B. bifidum*, *B. catenulatum*, and *B. pseudocatenulatum* [14].

***Bifidobacterium longum*:** This species is the most abundant in the infant gut (representing 56.2% of species in Bifidobacteriales) and produces beneficial bioactive molecules including short-chain fatty acids (acetate, lactate), exopolysaccharides, and serine protease inhibitors (serpins) [15]. Clinical findings include: a formulation including *B. longum* subsp. *infantis* BB02 was associated with a 54% reduction in necrotizing enterocolitis in very preterm infants; *B. longum* BB536 significantly elevated IFN-γ secretion cells in healthy newborns; and a VSL#3 blend (containing *B. longum*) showed significant efficacy for inducing and maintaining remission in children with newly diagnosed ulcerative colitis [15].

***Bifidobacterium breve*:** *B. breve* has been commonly isolated from the intestines of healthy breastfed infants and has demonstrated health benefits including allergy management, neuroprotection, and cardiovascular and metabolic health effects [16]. A large study (1755 newborns) showed *B. breve* M-16V significantly reduced NEC [16]. Some *B. breve* strains have shown potential in preventing cognitive impairment in Alzheimer's disease models and improving cognitive function in elderly humans with mild cognitive impairment [16].

### 1.6 *Saccharomyces boulardii*

**Unique Advantages:** *Saccharomyces boulardii* is currently the only probiotic yeast with established clinical validation for use in humans. It offers distinct advantages: higher resistance to gastric acid and bile salts; intrinsic tolerance to multiple antibiotics; and secretion of unique antimicrobial metabolites such as phenyllactic acid and 2-hydroxyisocaproic acid [17].

**Mechanisms of Action:** *S. boulardii* secretes a 54-kDa serine protease that degrades *Clostridium difficile* toxins A and B, and a 120-kDa phosphatase that dephosphorylates LPS endotoxins [17]. It upregulates MUC2, ZO-1, occludin, and claudin-1, strengthening intestinal tight junctions, and modulates inflammatory responses by decreasing pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-8, IL-1β, NF-κB) and increasing anti-inflammatory cytokines (IL-4, IL-10) [17].

**Clinical Evidence:** Meta-analysis of 10 RCTs found that *S. boulardii* was significantly protective for antibiotic-associated diarrhea with a pooled relative risk of 0.47 [18]. For *C. difficile* infection, meta-analysis showed RR=0.59 for preventing recurrences. In acute infectious diarrhea, meta-analysis of 7 RCTs showed approximately 1 day reduction in duration of childhood diarrhea [18]. *S. boulardii* also improved *H. pylori* eradication rates from 71% to 80% with supplementation and reduced side effects of triple therapy [18].

### 1.7 *Streptococcus thermophilus*

**Health Benefits:** *Streptococcus thermophilus* is a Gram-positive, homofermentative lactic acid bacterium with an estimated global consumption of 10²¹ CFU annually and a market value of approximately $40 billion [19]. It is classified as GRAS (Generally Recognized as Safe) by the FDA and holds QPS (Qualified Presumption of Safety) status from the European Food Safety Authority [19].

Key benefits include: lactose intolerance relief—the beta-galactosidase produced by *S. thermophilus* serves as an "autodigestive source of lactose" pre-digesting lactose and reducing breath hydrogen levels in lactase-deficient adults [19]; gut barrier integrity—its exopolysaccharides support tight junction proteins and mucus production; and it is included in VSL#3, which showed efficacy in inducing remission in mild-to-moderate ulcerative colitis [19]. *S. thermophilus* also inhibits the activity of endocannabinoid-degrading enzymes FAAH and MAGL, effectively elevating endocannabinoid tone and reducing pain and inflammatory signaling [19].

### 1.8 Evidence Summary for Probiotic Efficacy by Condition

A systematic review and meta-analysis of 26 randomized controlled trials (1,891 participants) found that probiotics significantly improved gut barrier function as measured by transepithelial resistance (MD 5.27), serum zonulin (SMD -1.58), and endotoxin (SMD -3.20) [20]. Probiotics reduced inflammatory markers including CRP (SMD -1.76), TNF-α (SMD -0.68), and IL-6 (SMD -0.80), and significantly boosted the enrichment of *Bifidobacterium* (SMD 1.85) and *Lactobacillus* (SMD 2.22) [20].

High-quality evidence (Grade A) supports probiotics for: acute infectious diarrhea (reduces duration by ~25 hours), antibiotic-associated diarrhea (NNT=10-13), ulcerative colitis remission (NNT=8), and necrotizing enterocolitis (reduces risk by 57% and mortality) [21]. Moderate evidence (Grade B) supports probiotics for: *C. difficile*-associated diarrhea (NNT=29-30), hepatic encephalopathy (NNT=7), IBS (NNT=7), and functional abdominal pain. Probiotics are ineffective for acute pancreatitis and Crohn disease. Dosing of ≥5 billion CFUs/day is significantly more effective than lower doses [21].

## 2. Prebiotics: Definition, Types, and Mechanistic Roles

### 2.1 Definition and Criteria

The ISAPP consensus statement (2017) defines a prebiotic as "a substrate that is selectively utilized by host microorganisms conferring a health benefit" [22]. This definition expands the concept to possibly include non-carbohydrate substances and applications to body sites other than the gastrointestinal tract. Key criteria for establishing prebiotic status include: the substance must be a substrate utilized by host microorganisms; must be sufficiently characterized; must be selectively utilized by the host microbiota (demonstrated through reproducible and specific changes in composition or function); must show a demonstrated health benefit via well-controlled studies in the target population; and must have a hypothesis for how microbiome modulation leads to the health benefit [23].

### 2.2 Main Types of Prebiotic Compounds

**Inulin and Fructooligosaccharides (FOS):** Inulin is a fructan-type prebiotic composed of fructose units with a terminal glucose, with chicory root being the richest natural source. Jerusalem artichoke contains the highest natural concentration of inulin at 10-22% [24]. FOS are oligosaccharide prebiotics composed of short chains of fructose units. Both consistently increase beneficial bacteria (*Bifidobacterium* and *Lactobacillus*) and short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) like acetate, propionate, and butyrate [25].

**Galactooligosaccharides (GOS):** GOS are prebiotic compounds similar to breast milk oligosaccharides that encourage a gut bacteria population promoting health and reducing the incidence of intestinal infections [26]. In a clinical trial, Bimuno GOS alone over 12 weeks produced significant reductions in total cholesterol, triglycerides, and TC:HDL ratio, plus improvements in insulin, CRP, and fecal sIgA [27].

**Human Milk Oligosaccharides (HMOs):** HMOs are structurally complex carbohydrates naturally present in human milk with four key advantages over other prebiotics: (1) more complex structure stimulating a greater diversity of beneficial bacterial species; (2) unique to humans and specifically tailored to infant needs; (3) additional health benefits beyond prebiotic effects, including anti-infective properties and immune support; and (4) extensive study in human clinical trials demonstrating safety and efficacy [28].

**Resistant Starch:** A 68-person crossover trial found that Resistant Starch Type 2 (RS2, native starch) increased *Ruminococcus bromii* and *Blautia glucerasea*, while Resistant Starch Type 4 (RS4, chemically modified starch) influenced *Parabacteroides distasonis* [27]. Both activated more genes for complex carbohydrate breakdown, with shifts reversing within 5 days of cessation [27].

**Pectin:** Pectin is a dietary fiber that is not degraded by human saliva or gastric acid and is resistant to digestive enzymes. Pectic substrates are slowly but completely fermented, with greater production of acetate compared with other fibers. Pectin fermentation increases abundances of *Ruminococcaceae*, *Bacteroides*, *Lachnospira*, and *Faecalibacterium prausnitzii* [29]. Pectin reduces blood LDL cholesterol by 3-7% (at 15 g/day over 4 weeks), delays glucose absorption, maintains intestinal barrier by increasing mucin 2 expression, and has anti-cancer effects [30].

### 2.3 Mechanistic Roles of Prebiotics

**Selective Stimulation of Beneficial Bacteria:** Prebiotics resist digestion in the upper GI tract, reach the colon intact, and are fermented by beneficial bacteria (primarily *Bifidobacterium* and *Lactobacillus*), producing SCFAs [25]. Cross-feeding between bacterial species is essential—*Bifidobacterium* species produce acetate, which butyrate-producing bacteria (like *Faecalibacterium prausnitzii* and *Roseburia* species) convert to butyrate. Multi-strain probiotic preparations increase SCFA production more effectively than single strains because they support multiple stages of the fermentation cascade [31].

**Production of Short-Chain Fatty Acids (SCFAs):** SCFAs—primarily acetate (60%), propionate (20%), and butyrate (20%)—are the end products of fermentation of non-digestible carbohydrates in the large intestine by the anaerobic intestinal microbiota [32]. SCFAs can provide up to 10% of the host's daily caloric needs and regulate gut homeostasis; their deficiency is involved in the pathogenesis of inflammatory bowel diseases, colorectal cancer, and cardiometabolic disorders [33].

**Effects on Gut Barrier Function:** SCFAs strengthen intestinal barrier function, lower colonic pH, enhance mineral absorption, and influence metabolic health [25]. Butyrate is the primary energy source for colonocytes, providing up to 70% of their energy needs [31]. Butyrate strengthens gut barrier integrity, has anti-inflammatory effects via HDAC inhibition, and supports metabolic regulation [31].

**Immune Modulation and Anti-Inflammatory Properties:** SCFAs possess extensive inhibitory activities on class I and II histone deacetylases (HDACs), targeting the epigenome by inducing chromatin remodeling that influences cellular processes and functions [34]. The anti-inflammatory effects of SCFAs encompass epithelial cells and a diverse range of immune cells, including macrophages (promoting M2 anti-inflammatory polarization), dendritic cells (promoting Treg differentiation), neutrophils, and mast cells [34]. Butyrate exerts the strongest anti-inflammatory effect of all SCFAs [32].

**Butyrate and Colorectal Cancer Protection:** Butyrate suppresses the proliferation of colorectal cancer cells via targeting pyruvate kinase M2 (PKM2) and metabolic reprogramming. Butyrate is present at high concentrations (>10 mM) in the colon and is known to promote normal colonocyte growth while paradoxically inhibiting cancerous colonocytes—known as "the butyrate paradox" [35]. Butyrate activates PKM2 by promoting its dephosphorylation and tetramerization, which reprograms colorectal cancer cell metabolism, inhibiting the Warburg effect while favoring energetic metabolism [35]. Butyrate also decreases its own oxidation in colorectal cancer cells through inhibition of histone deacetylases, selectively targeting cancer cells to reduce its own metabolism [36].

### 2.4 Clinical Trial Evidence for Prebiotics

A 2026 review of 22 randomized controlled trials confirms that prebiotic supplementation consistently increases beneficial bacteria and SCFA production, with clinical improvements in stool frequency/consistency, intestinal barrier function, and immune modulation across diverse populations [25]. Prebiotic response varies significantly based on baseline gut microbiota composition, host health status, genetics, and dietary patterns—factors like inflammation (e.g., in ulcerative colitis) can preclude a prebiotic response [25].

A meta-analysis of 19 clinical trials (897 people) found that gut microbiome-modulating therapies (probiotics, prebiotics, synbiotics) reduced total cholesterol by approximately 9 mg/dL and triglycerides by approximately 11 mg/dL in adults with metabolic syndrome [27]. A systematic review and meta-analysis of 28 RCTs involving 2002 patients demonstrated significant reductions in CRP (SMD -0.67) and TNF-α (SMD -1.81) with microbiome-modulating therapies in autoimmune diseases [37].

## 3. Pathogenic Bacteria of Greatest Concern in Colorectal Inflammation and Cancer

The gut microbiota drives colorectal cancer through four core pathways: (1) chronic inflammation from pathogenic bacteria; (2) DNA damage from bacterial genotoxins; (3) oxidative stress and chromosomal instability; and (4) microbial metabolites promoting malignant transformation [38]. CRC-associated dysbiosis is characterized by enrichment of pathobionts and loss of protective, SCFA-producing commensals [39].

### 3.1 *Fusobacterium nucleatum*

**Overview and Colonization:** *Fusobacterium nucleatum* (*Fn*) is an oral anaerobic commensal that has emerged as a crucial oncogenic contributor to CRC through its ectopic colonization in the gastrointestinal tract [40]. *Fn* is enriched in adenomas and CRC tissues compared to normal tissue, with abundance increasing with cancer progression. It is more prevalent in right-sided CRC and is associated with MSI-H, CIMP-H, BRAF mutation, and CMS1 subtype [40].

**FadA Adhesin and E-Cadherin/β-Catenin Signaling:** *Fn* promotes colorectal carcinogenesis through its FadA adhesin by binding to E-cadherin and activating β-catenin signaling. FadA binding leads to E-cadherin phosphorylation, β-catenin accumulation in the cytoplasm and translocation to the nucleus, activating β-catenin-regulated transcription including LEF/TCF, NF-κB, Myc, and Cyclin D1 [41]. In human colon tissue specimens, FadA gene levels were >10-100 times higher in patients with adenomas and adenocarcinomas compared to normal individuals, with a stepwise increase from normal to precancerous to cancerous tissues [41].

**Fap2 and Immune Evasion:** *Fn* binds to the inhibitory receptors TIGIT and CEACAM1 on T cells and NK cells through Fap2 and CbpF, respectively, to mediate immunosuppression and apoptosis [40]. The cryo-EM structure of Fap2 reveals an elongated approximately 50 nm long β-helix with a matchstick-like tip that binds to TIGIT on its membrane-distal tip, with a dissociation constant of approximately 0.6 μM [42]. Fap2 is a bifunctional adhesin through which *Fn* binds to tumors via Gal-GalNAc (a glycan abundant on and specific for colon cancer cells) and to immune cells via TIGIT [42].

**Clinical Significance:** High abundance of *Fn* is strongly associated with poorer patient prognosis and increased cancer recurrence—the hazard ratio for CRC-specific mortality was 1.25 in *Fn*-low cases and 1.58 in *Fn*-high cases compared to *Fn*-negative cases [40]. *Fn* is associated with resistance to chemotherapy, radiotherapy, and immunotherapy [40].

**Fna C2: The Dominant CRC Clade:** A 2024 *Nature* study generated closed genomes for 135 *Fn* strains and identified that the subspecies *F. nucleatum animalis* (Fna) is composed of two distinct clades (Fna C1 and Fna C2), with only 91.61-93.11% average nucleotide identity between them [43]. Only Fna C2 is significantly enriched in the CRC tumor niche (p<0.00001). Key virulence factors Fap2, CmpA, and fusolisin are absent in Fna C1 but significantly associated with Fna C2. Fna C2 showed significantly higher cancer epithelial cell invasion compared to Fna C1, and Fna C2-treated mice had increased intestinal adenomas [43].

**ADP-Heptose Secretion:** *Fusobacterium* species secrete ADP-heptose, which activates ALPK1 signaling and promotes tumor immune evasion by upregulating PD-L1, suggesting a mechanism for colorectal cancer development [44].

### 3.2 Enterotoxigenic *Bacteroides fragilis* (ETBF) and the *B. fragilis* Toxin (BFT)

**Overview:** *Bacteroides fragilis* exists in two variants: non-toxigenic (NTBF), which can be beneficial by promoting gut homeostasis through regulatory T cells, and enterotoxigenic (ETBF), which produces BFT and is pathogenic [45]. ETBF has emerged as a gut microbiome pathogen that can promote intestinal inflammation and contribute to CRC [45].

**BFT Structure and Function:** BFT is a zinc-dependent metalloprotease (44.5 kDa, 397 amino acids) encoded by the *bft* gene located on a pathogenicity island. Three isoforms exist (BFT-1, BFT-2, BFT-3), with BFT-2 having the highest pathogenic potential [45]. Structural studies revealed that a single residue (position 357) within the catalytic domain determines subtype-specific activity and explains the heightened potency of BFT-2 [46].

**Pathogenic Mechanisms:** BFT cleaves E-cadherin, disrupting epithelial barrier integrity, activates MAPK and NF-κB pathways, induces COX-2 and prostaglandin E2, promotes proinflammatory cytokines (IL-8, TNF-α), activates STAT3 and Th17 responses, and drives β-catenin-mediated oncogenic signaling [45]. BFT also induces spermine oxidase leading to ROS production and DNA damage [47].

**Clinical Evidence:** A Johns Hopkins study found that the mucosa of colorectal neoplasia cases was significantly more often *bft*-positive (85.7% left, 91.7% right) compared to controls (53.1% left, 55.5% right), with a trend toward increased *bft* positivity in late-stage versus early-stage CRC patients (100% vs 72.7%) [48]. The first connection to CRC was demonstrated in 2006—38% of isolates from CRC patients carried the *bft* gene versus 12% in controls [45].

**Immunomodulation:** ETBF colonization leads to Treg activation, which consumes IL-2 and reduces its levels, allowing Th17 cell expansion and increased IL-17 production. IL-17 promotes cancer cell survival and proliferation and triggers IL-6 production, further activating STAT3 [47]. In APCMin/+ mouse models, ETBF colonization induces STAT3 activation and a robust Th17 cytokine profile, generating a pro-carcinogenic inflammatory milieu [45].

### 3.3 *Escherichia coli* Strains Carrying the pks Pathogenicity Island (Colibactin)

**The pks Island and Colibactin Biosynthesis:** *E. coli* strains of phylogenetic group B2 harbor a 54 kb genomic island called pks that codes for the production of a polyketide-peptide genotoxin, colibactin [49]. Approximately 20% of healthy individuals harbor pks+ *E. coli*, with prevalence increasing to approximately 60% in patients with CRC and inflammatory bowel disease [50].

**DNA Damage and Genomic Instability:** pks+ *E. coli* induces DNA double-strand breaks in vivo in mouse enterocytes and triggers genomic instability in cultured mammalian cells even at low infectious doses [49]. Low-dose infection caused a transient DNA damage response followed by cell division with incomplete repair, leading to anaphase bridges, chromosome aberrations, micronuclei, and aneuploidy that persisted for up to 21 days after infection [49].

**The Colibactin Mutational Signature:** Colibactin generates a distinct mutational signature found in approximately one in eight colon tumors, linking bacterial genotoxicity to cancer development [51]. A study using human intestinal organoids demonstrated that pks+ *E. coli* induce a mutational signature associated with CRC, with these mutagenic signatures present in approximately 5% of metastatic tumors primarily from CRC-derived primary sites, with APC showing the highest number of matching mutations [52].

**Transformation of Primary Colon Epithelial Cells:** Short-term infection (3 hours) with pks+ *E. coli* caused DNA double-strand breaks, megalocytosis, and multinucleated cells in primary colon epithelial cells [53]. Infected organoids developed Wnt-independent growth with enhanced proliferation, impaired differentiation, and indefinite growth capacity (>1 year in culture). Whole-genome sequencing revealed major chromosomal aberrations including copy number variations, aneuploidy, and chromosomal instability [53].

**Cancer Stemness, Therapy Resistance, and Immune Suppression:** Beyond DNA damage, pks+ *E. coli* promotes CRC progression through cancer stemness, therapy resistance, microbiota shifts, and immune suppression [51]. Chronic exposure selects for clones with restored homologous recombination proficiency, conferring resistance to DNA-damaging agents such as irinotecan [39]. pks+ *E. coli* also depletes CD3+ and CD8+ T cells, potentially enhancing immunotherapy resistance [51].

### 3.4 *Helicobacter pylori* (CagA Toxin)

**Background:** *Helicobacter pylori* infects approximately 50% of the global population and is classified as a Group 1 carcinogen by the IARC (1994). It is the leading infectious cause of gastric cancer, with *cagA*-positive strains increasing gastric cancer risk by at least one order of magnitude over *cagA*-negative strains [54].

**CagA Structure and Delivery:** CagA (128-145 kDa) is delivered into gastric epithelial cells via the type IV secretion system (T4SS). It has a structured N-terminal region and an intrinsically disordered C-terminal tail containing EPIYA (Glu-Pro-Ile-Tyr-Ala) motifs. CagA undergoes tyrosine phosphorylation at EPIYA motifs by host kinases, and the CagA-multimerization (CM) motif is required for CagA multimerization at the plasma membrane [54].

**Pro-Oncogenic Actions:** Tyrosine-phosphorylated CagA acts as an oncogenic scaffold protein, binding multiple host signaling proteins. It aberrantly activates SHP2 phosphatase, constitutively driving MEK-ERK signaling and disrupting FAK-mediated focal adhesion dynamics, causing the "hummingbird phenotype" (elongated cells with increased motility) [54]. Transgenic CagA expression in mice induces oncogenic action without inflammation, potentiated further in the presence of chronic inflammation [54].

**Evidence for Colorectal Cancer Association:** A large retrospective cohort study of 812,736 U.S. military veterans found that *H. pylori* infection was associated with an 18% increased risk of developing CRC and a 12% increased risk of dying from CRC [55]. Treatment of *H. pylori* infection reduced CRC risk and mortality by a magnitude similar to colonoscopy, with absolute risk reductions of 0.23% to 0.35%. Untreated patients had a 23% higher risk of developing CRC and a 40% higher risk of CRC death [55]. Among patients infected with *H. pylori*, CagA+ seropositivity is associated with increased risk for both gastric and colonic cancer [56].

### 3.5 *Enterococcus faecalis*

**The Controversial Role:** *Enterococcus faecalis* is a Gram-positive, facultative anaerobic commensal bacterium that colonizes the human GI tract early in life. Its role in CRC is controversial, with evidence for both harmful and protective effects [57].

**Harmful Effects:** The harmful role is mainly associated with its ability to generate extracellular superoxide and hydrogen peroxide that can cause genomic instability, damaging colonic DNA and predisposing the host to mutations and cancer [57]. *E. faecalis* is unique in generating extracellular superoxide, promoting oxidative stress, and damaging DNA. Additional harmful mechanisms include production of a metalloprotease that compromises the intestinal epithelial barrier and activation of macrophage MMP-9 [57].

**Protective Effects:** Some strains (e.g., EC-12, CECT7121) show antitumor properties by suppressing β-catenin signaling, inducing apoptosis in cancer cells, and enhancing immune responses [57]. The conflicting roles may be attributed to different strains, as horizontal gene transfer can lead to mutations that alter virulence. It is proposed that *E. faecalis* likely acts as a "passenger" bacterium in CRC rather than a "driver"—it may become pathogenic only when the intestinal environment undergoes alterations such as those caused by tumorigenesis [57].

### 3.6 *Clostridium difficile*

**Role in Colorectal Carcinogenesis:** *C. difficile* synthesizes three distinct toxins: toxin A (TcdA), toxin B (TcdB), and a binary toxin known as *C. difficile* transferase (CDT) [58]. TcdA disrupts epithelial barrier integrity by targeting tight junction proteins, increasing intestinal permeability. TcdB induces DNA damage through reactive oxygen species generation, causes double-stranded DNA breaks, and triggers senescence in enteric glial cells. Both toxins activate inflammatory signaling pathways (NF-κB and STAT3), leading to production of pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-6, IL-8, TNF-α) that create a tumorigenic environment [58].

**Epidemiological Evidence:** CRC incidence is significantly higher in patients with *C. difficile* infection, with a relative risk of approximately 2.7 times higher [58]. Mouse APC min+ models demonstrate accelerated tumor growth with chronic CDI, and tumor biopsies from CRC patients frequently contain *C. difficile* DNA or toxins. Higher *C. difficile* colonization rates are associated with lymph node metastasis [58].

### 3.7 Other Pathogenic Bacteria

**Peptostreptococcus anaerobius:** Promotes tumorigenesis via TLR2/TLR4, ROS accumulation, and PI3K/AKT/NF-κB signaling [59]. **Parvimonas micra** is a potential non-invasive biomarker for early CRC detection [59]. **Streptococcus gallolyticus** (formerly *S. bovis*): Patients with SBSEC bacteremia were 3.73-fold more likely to harbor underlying CRC than those without bacteremia (RR 3.73, 95% CI 2.79–5.01) [38].

## 4. Informing and Optimizing Daily Dietary Choices for Gut Health and CRC Risk Reduction

The findings regarding probiotics, prebiotics, and pathogenic bacteria offer a robust evidence base for practical dietary strategies to maintain gut health and reduce colorectal cancer risk. The overarching principle is to promote a gut microbiota composition characterized by high diversity, abundance of beneficial SCFA-producing bacteria, and low levels of pathogenic bacteria and their genotoxic metabolites.

### 4.1 Dietary Fiber and Prebiotic Intake

**Recommendation:** Consume 25-38 grams of dietary fiber per day from diverse sources, including prebiotic-rich foods. This is the single most impactful dietary intervention for gut health.

**Evidence Base:** SCFAs—primarily butyrate, propionate, and acetate—are produced by gut bacteria through fermentation of dietary fiber. Butyrate serves as the primary energy source for colonocytes, strengthens epithelial integrity, modulates immune functions, suppresses pro-inflammatory cytokines, and enhances mucosal defense mechanisms [32]. Low levels of butyrate- and propionate-producing bacteria have been linked to inflammatory disorders such as ulcerative colitis and asthma [33]. Clinical trials have demonstrated that high-fiber diets increase microbial diversity and promote health-associated genera including *Faecalibacterium* and *Akkermansia* [60].

**Practical Sources:**

- **Inulin and FOS:** Chicory root, Jerusalem artichoke (10-22% inulin), garlic, onion, leek, asparagus, banana (especially slightly green), wheat bran
- **GOS:** Legumes (chickpeas, lentils, beans), certain root vegetables
- **Resistant Starch:** Cooked and cooled potatoes, pasta, and rice; green bananas; legumes; raw oats; whole grains
- **Pectin:** Apples (especially with skin), citrus fruits (peel and pith), plums, pears, carrots, green beans
- **Whole Grains:** Oats, barley, rye, whole wheat, brown rice, quinoa
- **Other Sources:** Flaxseeds, chia seeds, almonds, pistachios, leafy greens

**Specific Guidance:** Incorporate a variety of these foods daily. For example, starting the day with oatmeal (beta-glucan) topped with sliced banana (resistant starch) and a tablespoon of flaxseeds; including legumes in soups, salads, or as a side dish; snacking on an apple or a handful of almonds; and including cooked and cooled potatoes or pasta in salads.

### 4.2 Probiotic-Rich Foods and Supplementation

**Recommendation:** Regular consumption of fermented foods containing live probiotics, and consideration of targeted probiotic supplementation for specific conditions.

**Evidence Base:** A systematic review and meta-analysis of 26 RCTs demonstrated that probiotics significantly improve gut barrier function, reduce inflammatory markers (CRP, TNF-α, IL-6), and boost beneficial bacteria [20]. High-quality evidence supports probiotics for acute infectious diarrhea, antibiotic-associated diarrhea, ulcerative colitis remission, and necrotizing enterocolitis [21].

**Strain-Specific Food Sources:**

- ***Lactobacillus* species:** Yogurt (look for "live and active cultures"), kefir, sauerkraut (unpasteurized), kimchi, miso, tempeh, pickles (naturally fermented), sourdough bread
- ***Bifidobacterium* species:** Yogurt, kefir, some fermented dairy products, many probiotic supplements
- ***Saccharomyces boulardii:*** Florastor® (commercial probiotic), not naturally found in foods; available as a supplement
- ***Streptococcus thermophilus:*** Yogurt, certain cheeses

**Supplementation Guidance:** When using probiotic supplements, key considerations include: (1) strain specificity matters—effects are not generalizable across species; (2) dosing of ≥5 billion CFUs/day is significantly more effective than lower doses [21]; (3) multi-strain formulations may show synergistic effects; (4) start probiotics within 2 days of first antibiotic dose for prevention of antibiotic-associated diarrhea [3]; and (5) consult a healthcare provider for immunocompromised individuals or those with central venous catheters.

### 4.3 Synbiotic Combinations

**Recommendation:** Combine prebiotic and probiotic foods for synergistic effects (synbiotics).

**Evidence Base:** A 2025 systematic review and meta-analysis of 90 RCTs with 5,207 participants found that both synbiotics and prebiotics significantly improved inflammatory biomarkers in adults, including CRP, hs-CRP, TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6. Synbiotics showed stronger effects than prebiotics for several biomarkers (e.g., IL-6: SMD = -2.02 vs. -0.41; IL-1β: SMD = -1.35 vs. -0.44) [61].

**Practical Examples:** Yogurt (probiotic) with banana or berries (prebiotic fiber); kefir blended with oats or flaxseeds; sauerkraut or kimchi served with a meal containing legumes or whole grains; miso soup with added seaweed or vegetables.

### 4.4 Dietary Patterns to Limit Pathogenic Bacteria

**Recommendation:** Adopt a dietary pattern that limits the growth and activity of pathogenic bacteria while promoting beneficial species.

**Evidence Base:** Western diets high in simple sugars, saturated fats, and processed foods contribute to chronic low-grade inflammation and dysbiosis characterized by enrichment of pathobionts such as *F. nucleatum*, pks+ *E. coli*, and ETBF [62]. The "hygiene hypothesis" suggests reduced early-life microbial contact increases risk of inflammatory diseases later in life [62].

**Strategies to Limit Pathogenic Bacteria:**

- **Limit Red and Processed Meat:** High heme iron and protein content promote the growth of sulfur-reducing bacteria and production of genotoxic hydrogen sulfide and N-nitroso compounds. Heterocyclic amines and polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons from high-temperature cooking are carcinogenic. The WHO classifies processed meat as Group 1 carcinogen and red meat as Group 2A.
- **Limit Alcohol:** Alcohol metabolism produces acetaldehyde, a carcinogenic metabolite that damages DNA and disrupts gut barrier function, increasing endotoxin translocation.
- **Limit High-Sugar and Ultra-Processed Foods:** Simple sugars promote the growth of potentially pathogenic bacteria and reduce microbial diversity.
- **Increase Intake of Polyphenol-Rich Foods:** Polyphenols from fruits, vegetables, tea, coffee, and dark chocolate have antimicrobial effects against pathogenic bacteria and promote beneficial bacteria. Examples include berries, green tea, red wine (in moderation), turmeric, and extra virgin olive oil.
- **Include Anti-Inflammatory Spices:** Turmeric (curcumin), ginger, and garlic have demonstrated anti-inflammatory and antimicrobial properties.

### 4.5 Specific Dietary Interventions for CRC Risk Reduction

**Evidence-Based Dietary Recommendations:**

1. **High-Fiber Diet:** A systematic review of 19 clinical trials found that gut microbiome-modulating therapies reduced total cholesterol by approximately 9 mg/dL and triglycerides by approximately 11 mg/dL in adults with metabolic syndrome [27]. High-fiber diets increase microbial diversity and promote health-associated genera including *Faecalibacterium* and *Akkermansia* [60].

2. **Mediterranean Diet Pattern:** Rich in fiber, polyphenols, unsaturated fats, and fermented foods. Associated with higher microbial diversity, increased SCFA production, and lower levels of inflammatory markers. A systematic review of 28 RCTs demonstrated significant reductions in CRP and TNF-α with microbiome-modulating therapies in autoimmune diseases [37].

3. **Adequate Vitamin D and Calcium:** Vitamin D has immunomodulatory effects and may reduce CRC risk. Calcium binds secondary bile acids in the colon, reducing their carcinogenic potential.

4. **Omega-3 Fatty Acids:** Found in fatty fish, flaxseeds, and walnuts. Omega-3s have anti-inflammatory properties and may promote beneficial gut bacteria.

5. **Butyrate-Boosting Foods:** To maximize butyrate production, focus on foods that support cross-feeding: prebiotic fibers (inulin, FOS, GOS, resistant starch) that are fermented by *Bifidobacterium* and *Lactobacillus* to produce acetate and lactate, which are then converted to butyrate by *Faecalibacterium prausnitzii* and *Roseburia* species [31]. Specific foods include cooked and cooled potatoes, green bananas, legumes, whole grains, and chicory root.

### 4.6 Practical Daily Dietary Guidelines

**Sample Daily Pattern for Gut Health:**

- **Breakfast:** Oatmeal (beta-glucan fiber) topped with sliced banana (resistant starch), a handful of berries (polyphenols), and a tablespoon of ground flaxseeds (fiber, omega-3s). Serve with plain yogurt (probiotics).
- **Lunch:** Large salad with mixed greens, chickpeas or lentils (prebiotic fiber, resistant starch), chopped apple (pectin), carrots, and a fermented vegetable like sauerkraut or kimchi (probiotics). Dressing with extra virgin olive oil and lemon juice.
- **Snack:** An apple (pectin) or a handful of almonds (fiber, vitamin E). A cup of green tea (polyphenols).
- **Dinner:** Fatty fish (e.g., salmon, omega-3s) or plant-based protein, with steamed broccoli (fiber, sulforaphane), quinoa (fiber, resistant starch), and a side of cooked and cooled sweet potato (resistant starch).
- **Evening:** A small serving of kefir (probiotics) or a cup of herbal tea.

**Key Principles:**

1. **Diversity is crucial:** Consume at least 30 different plant foods per week to maximize microbial diversity.
2. **Eat the rainbow:** Different colored fruits and vegetables provide different polyphenols and fibers.
3. **Fermented foods daily:** Include at least one serving of fermented food with live cultures.
4. **Whole grains over refined:** Replace refined grains with whole grains.
5. **Limit harmful factors:** Minimize red and processed meat, alcohol, sugar, and ultra-processed foods.
6. **Consider supplementation when needed:** Probiotic supplements for specific conditions (antibiotic use, diarrhea, IBS), and consider prebiotic supplements if dietary intake is insufficient.

### 4.7 Special Populations and Considerations

**For Individuals with IBD:** Probiotics may be beneficial for ulcerative colitis (Grade A evidence) but are ineffective for Crohn disease [21]. Synbiotics may be more effective than prebiotics alone. The strong anti-inflammatory effects of butyrate-producing bacteria support a high-fiber diet, but individual tolerance varies.

**For Individuals at High Risk of CRC:** Those with family history, personal history of polyps, or inflammatory bowel disease should prioritize: high-fiber diet (30-38 g/day), avoidance of processed meat, moderate alcohol consumption (or none), regular physical activity, and maintenance of healthy body weight. Consider probiotic supplementation with strains shown to have anti-carcinogenic properties (e.g., *L. casei* Shirota, *L. rhamnosus* GG, *B. longum*).

**For Individuals Taking Antibiotics:** Take probiotics (especially *S. boulardii* and LGG) within 2 days of starting antibiotics to reduce risk of antibiotic-associated diarrhea and *C. difficile* infection [3]. Continue for at least 1-2 weeks after completing antibiotics.

**For Immunocompromised Individuals:** Exercise caution with probiotics due to risk of infection. *S. boulardii* fungemia has been reported in immunocompromised patients and those with central venous catheters [18]. Probiotics are generally safe for healthy populations but should be used under medical supervision in vulnerable individuals.

## 5. Conclusion

The gut microbiota plays a pivotal role in maintaining intestinal health and influencing colorectal cancer pathogenesis. Specific probiotic strains—including *Lactobacillus rhamnosus* GG, *Lactobacillus acidophilus* LA1, *Lactobacillus casei* Shirota, *Lactobacillus plantarum* TIFN101, *Bifidobacterium longum*, *Bifidobacterium breve*, *Saccharomyces boulardii*, and *Streptococcus thermophilus*—have demonstrated beneficial effects on intestinal barrier function, immune modulation, and inflammation reduction through well-characterized molecular mechanisms. Prebiotics such as inulin, FOS, GOS, HMOs, resistant starch, and pectin selectively stimulate beneficial bacteria and promote SCFA production, particularly butyrate, which exerts anti-inflammatory and anti-carcinogenic effects through HDAC inhibition and metabolic reprogramming.

Conversely, pathogenic bacteria including *Fusobacterium nucleatum*, enterotoxigenic *Bacteroides fragilis*, pks+ *Escherichia coli*, *Helicobacter pylori* (CagA+), *Enterococcus faecalis*, and *Clostridium difficile* produce toxic metabolites that promote chronic inflammation, DNA damage, immune evasion, and oncogenic signaling, contributing to colorectal carcinogenesis. The enrichment of these pathobionts and depletion of protective SCFA-producing bacteria characterizes CRC-associated dysbiosis.

These findings translate into actionable dietary strategies: consuming a diverse, high-fiber diet rich in prebiotic foods; incorporating fermented foods with live probiotics; adopting synbiotic combinations for synergistic benefits; limiting red and processed meat, alcohol, and ultra-processed foods; and considering targeted probiotic supplementation for specific conditions. The Mediterranean dietary pattern, with its emphasis on plant foods, healthy fats, and fermented products, aligns well with these evidence-based recommendations. Continued research into strain-specific effects, individualized interventions, and the therapeutic potential of postbiotics, bacteriophages, and engineered biotherapeutics promises to further refine our ability to harness the gut microbiota for intestinal health and CRC prevention.
