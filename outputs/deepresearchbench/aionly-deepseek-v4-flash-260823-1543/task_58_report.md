# Horizontal Gene Transfer in Plants and Animals: Frequency, Mechanisms, and Evolutionary Significance

## Introduction

Horizontal gene transfer (HGT)—the movement of genetic material between species by means other than parent-to-offspring reproduction—is a foundational evolutionary force in prokaryotes, where it drives antibiotic resistance, virulence, and metabolic innovation. For most of the history of molecular biology, HGT in multicellular eukaryotes was considered negligible or an artifact. Over the past two decades, however, whole-genome sequencing and phylogenomic methods have overturned that assumption, documenting hundreds of well-supported cases of gene transfer into plant and animal genomes, including several with demonstrated adaptive functions. At the same time, the data make clear that HGT in plants and animals is orders of magnitude rarer than in microbes—a difference explained by the biology of germline sequestration, sexual reproduction, multicellular development, and genome defense systems.

This report synthesizes the primary literature on HGT in plants and animals, covering (1) documented frequency and occurrence, (2) proposed mechanisms, (3) adaptive versus neutral significance, (4) comparative framing against microbial HGT, (5) the history and methodological evolution of the field, and (6) an evaluation of the open debates.

## 1. Frequency and Occurrence of HGT in Plants

Plant HGT is now documented across mitochondria, plastids, and nuclear genomes, from pairwise gene transfers to the wholesale capture of entire organelle genomes.

### 1.1 Amborella trichopoda: the most extreme HGT genome ever examined

The basal angiosperm *Amborella trichopoda*, a shrub endemic to New Caledonia, holds the record for foreign DNA content. Bergthorsson et al. (2004) showed that of 31 mitochondrial protein genes recovered from *Amborella*, 20 showed evidence of one or more HGT events—26 foreign genes in total—with donors including other angiosperms and at least three different mosses. The authors concluded that "*Amborella* mtDNA has sustained proportionately more HGT than any other eukaryotic, or perhaps even prokaryotic, genome yet examined" [1].

The complete mitochondrial genome, published by Rice et al. (2013) in *Science*, is an extraordinary 3.9 Mb assembled as five circular chromosomes. It contains roughly **six genome equivalents of foreign mitochondrial DNA**: four complete mitochondrial genomes from three green algae and one moss, plus a partial genome from a fourth green alga, and ~753 kb from other angiosperms (including Santalales, a parasitic-plant order, as the major donor). Only 50 of 197 foreign mitochondrial protein genes retain intact open reading frames; the rest are pseudogenes, making *Amborella*'s mitogenome "a striking example of neutral evolution and 'junk DNA'" [2]. The authors proposed a **fusion-compatibility model**: *Amborella* captures whole mitochondria from attached epiphytes (mosses, lichens, algae), and green-plant mitochondria—unlike those of animals and fungi—fuse and recombine, permitting massive integration [2].

### 1.2 Parasitic plants: HGT scales with parasitic dependence

Parasitic plants, which form haustorial connections to host vasculature, are the richest documented system of plant-to-plant nuclear HGT.

- **Striga hermonthica (witchweed):** Yoshida et al. (2010) provided the first empirical evidence of a nuclear monocot gene transferred into a eudicot parasite genome; the gene clusters phylogenetically with sorghum, the parasite's host [4]. The *Striga asiatica* genome later confirmed host-derived genes and retrotransposons, alongside a striking expansion of strigolactone receptors that enable host detection [5].
- **Orobanchaceae phylogenomic survey:** Yang et al. (2016) analyzed transcriptomes of four Orobanchaceae species with increasing parasitic dependence—free-living *Lindenbergia*, facultative hemiparasite *Triphysaria*, obligate hemiparasite *Striga*, and holoparasite *Phelipanche*—confirming a minimum of **52 high-confidence HGT events**. Frequency scaled with heterotrophy: only one candidate in the nonparasite, versus dozens in the obligate parasites. Transfers mirrored host range (grass donors only in *Striga*; dicot donors in *Phelipanche*), and acquired genes were strongly biased toward expression in the haustorium and enriched for defense, transport, and transposable-element functions [3].
- **Rafflesiaceae:** In *Rafflesia cantleyi*, ~2% of the transcriptome consisted of host (*Tetrastigma*) genes, most integrated into the parasite nucleus [7]. Mitochondrial HGT in Rafflesiaceae (*Rafflesia*, *Rhizanthes*, *Sapria*) is an order of magnitude higher: 24–41% of sampled mitochondrial gene sequences show HGT, with intact reading frames and expression levels comparable to native genes [8]. The *Sapria himalayana* genome revealed ~44% loss of typical flowering-plant genes but dozens of host-derived HGTs, with ~90% of the genome composed of DNA transposons [10, 11]. *Rafflesia* seed transcriptomes also show host-family genes (e.g., SERK, RHM1) potentially involved in evading host defenses [9].
- **Cuscuta (dodder):** Kim et al. (2014) demonstrated bidirectional, genome-scale mRNA movement between *Cuscuta* and its hosts—nearly half the expressed *Arabidopsis* transcriptome was detected in the parasite [12]. Yang et al. (2019) identified **108 transcribed, probably functional HGTs** in *Cuscuta campestris*, of which **18 were independently acquired from the same gene families as Orobanchaceae parasites**—convergent HGT—and 16–20 predate the radiation of the genus. Many acquired genes are sources of 24-nt small RNAs targeting host genes, suggesting stolen genes are used as "silencing weapons" [13]. The *Cuscuta campestris* genome (Vogel et al. 2018) identified 64 novel high-confidence HGT candidates, including a block of adjacent genes matching neighboring carrot genes with conserved introns [14]. Curiously, the *Cuscuta* mitochondrial genomes show little or no HGT, a notable contrast with its nuclear genome [14].

### 1.3 Grasses: HGT is widespread even among non-parasitic plants

Grasses—the most economically important plant family—show repeated, demonstrable lateral transfers.

- The chromosome-level genome of *Alloteropsis semialata* (a C4 grass) revealed **59 lateral transfers from at least nine donor species**, clustered in 23 genomic fragments up to 170 kb, with most genes expressed [15].
- A systematic screen of 17 grass genomes spanning >50 million years of divergence found LGT in **13 of 17 species** (mean 8.4; range 0–30), including 11 in maize and 10 in wheat. Rhizomatous species acquired significantly more genes, likely because rhizomes create root-to-root contact and a route into the germline. The authors concluded that LGT "appears to represent a potent evolutionary force capable of spreading functional genes among distantly related grass species" [16, 54].
- Ribosomal DNA transfers between grass subfamilies separated by ~60 million years were documented in 16 diploid *Hordeum* species, involving a ~70-kb *Panicum*-derived region; the transferred rDNA was silenced and retained as selectively neutral DNA [18].
- Transposon HGT in grasses: the first well-documented nuclear HGT between higher plants was the transfer of a *Mu*-like transposable element (MULE) between rice and *Setaria* millets, with >88% identity over 3.8 kb despite ~50 million years of host divergence [17].

### 1.4 Ferns: adaptive photoreceptor acquisition from bryophytes

The most celebrated adaptive plant HGT is the **neochrome gene**, a chimeric blue/red-light photoreceptor acquired by ferns from hornworts ~179 million years ago (Li et al. 2014). Neochrome appears to have enabled ferns to thrive in the low-light, red-enriched understory of angiosperm forests, and its acquisition "was perhaps one of the most important events in fern evolution" [19].

An earlier landmark was the transfer of a mitochondrial *nad1* gene region from a parasitic flowering plant (a member of Santalales, likely Loranthaceae) to the rattlesnake fern *Botrychium virginianum*, an obligately mycotrophic fern, with the transgene present in all 34 individuals sampled worldwide. The authors proposed either direct parasitism or a shared mycorrhizal fungus as the vector—the "wood-wide web" hypothesis [20]. Fern genomes more broadly carry large quantities of transposon-derived DNA, and *Ceratopteris richardii* acquired bacterial-like defense genes multiple times, described as a "lottery win" following wounding [55].

### 1.5 Microbe-to-plant and Agrobacterium-mediated transfers

- **Land-plant origin:** Yue et al. (2012) identified **57 families of nuclear genes (128 genes) in the moss *Physcomitrella patens*** acquired from prokaryotes, fungi, or viruses, involved in xylem formation, defense, nitrogen recycling, starch, polyamine, auxin, and glutathione biosynthesis—concluding HGT was "a dynamic process occurring frequently in the early evolution of land plants" [21]. Ma et al. (2022) extended this: analyzing 31 charophyte and land-plant genomes, they found **593 transferred gene families**, concentrated in two major episodes—early streptophyte evolution and the origin of land plants—with 58–71% retained in descendants, affecting stress responses, ion transport, hormone signaling, and specialized metabolism [22].
- **Wheat blight resistance:** The *Fhb7* gene, which confers broad Fusarium head blight and crown rot resistance, was shown to have been acquired by the wheat-grass relative *Thinopyrum elongatum* via HGT from an endophytic *Epichloë* fungus, and introgressed into wheat without yield penalty—a rare, fully functional fungus-to-plant transfer [23].
- **Natural Agrobacterium transformation:** Ancient *Agrobacterium rhizogenes* T-DNA insertions ("cellular T-DNAs") occur naturally in at least 15 *Nicotiana* species [25, 26], in *Linaria vulgaris* [27], and—most strikingly—in cultivated sweet potato, where all 291 tested accessions carry an expressed *Agrobacterium* T-DNA that was apparently selected for during domestication [24].
- **Recent microbiome-scale evidence:** Haimlich et al. (2024) found 180 cross-kingdom HGT events between *Arabidopsis* and its commensal bacteria (84 plant-to-bacteria; 96 bacteria-to-plant), and functionally rescued an *Arabidopsis det2* dwarf mutant with the bacterial homolog—demonstrating that plant-microbiota HGT is more frequent than previously appreciated [50].
- **Recurrent metallophore acquisition:** The nicotianamine synthase (*NAS*) gene—central to metal chelation—was independently acquired by at least five plant lineages from distinct microbial donors over hundreds of millions of years (fungi ~275 and ~210 Mya, bacteria, plus a moss-to-moss transfer), with knockout experiments in *Physcomitrium patens* showing severe growth and reproduction defects. The gene even transferred to a whitefly, the only known metazoan carrying it [51].

## 2. Frequency and Occurrence of HGT in Animals

Animal HGT is rarer and often associated with endosymbiosis, parasitism, or extreme life-history traits such as anhydrobiosis. The best-documented cases follow.

### 2.1 Bdelloid rotifers: ~10% of the genome from non-metazoan sources

Bdelloid rotifers—ancient, exclusively asexual animals famous for surviving desiccation—contain the highest proportion of foreign genes of any animal.

- Gladyshev, Meselson & Arkhipova (2008) discovered "many genes" in bdelloids that originated from bacteria, fungi, and plants, concentrated in telomeric regions; some were defective, but others were intact, transcribed, contained spliceosomal introns, and one bacterial-origin gene produced an active enzyme when expressed in *E. coli* [30].
- The *Adineta vaga* genome (Flot et al. 2013) confirmed that **>8% of genes are of non-metazoan origin**—"at levels comparable to some bacteria"—and linked HGT and gene conversion to the maintenance of an asexual lifestyle [32].
- Eyres et al. (2015) showed HGT is not confined to desiccation-tolerant lineages: two aquatic *Rotaria* species had 9.5–10.4% foreign genes, while desiccating-habitat species had 12.8–14.1%. They estimated ~12.8 gains per million years in the desiccating clade and found differential loss of desiccation-protection genes (glutathione/trypanothione metabolism) in aquatic species [31].
- Nowell et al. (2024) showed that horizontally acquired genes are **2–3× more likely to be upregulated during fungal infection**, including NRP/PKS biosynthetic gene clusters resembling bacterial antibiotic pathways; the pathogen-resistant rotifer species upregulated 11 such clusters with ~10× stronger fold-changes than the susceptible species. This suggests bdelloids have "domesticated" bacterial secondary-metabolite pathways into antimicrobial defense—a potential explanation for their long-term persistence without sex [33].

### 2.2 Pea aphids: fungal carotenoid biosynthesis genes

Moran & Jarvik (2010) demonstrated that the pea aphid *Acyrthosiphon pisum* carries multiple fungal-derived carotenoid biosynthesis genes (phytoene synthase/desaturase and carotenoid desaturase), the first such genes found in any animal. Red and green color morphs—which differ in susceptibility to predators and parasitoids—are determined by a 30-kb region encoding a carotenoid desaturase: a point mutation abolishes torulene production and red body color. This is a clear case of HGT providing a fundamentally new metabolic capability [34]. Follow-up work (2025) showed that these horizontally acquired carotenoid genes enable light-driven ATP synthesis promoting cold adaptation: at 12°C, high light increased aphid ATP content by 240%, shortened pre-reproductive time by 46%, and increased fecundity 2.4-fold—evidence that the transferred pathway has been co-opted into thermal physiology [35].

### 2.3 Wolbachia-to-host transfers: symbiont genomes in animal chromosomes

*Wolbachia*, an endosymbiont infecting an estimated 40–52% of insect species, is the most prolific source of animal HGT.

- Kondo et al. (2002) provided the first prokaryote-to-eukaryote transfer case: a >11-kb *Wolbachia* fragment (including *wsp* and *ftsZ*) on the X chromosome of the adzuki bean beetle, present in >90% of Japanese populations and largely pseudogenized [36].
- Dunning Hotopp et al. (2007) generalized this: transfers into 4 insect and 4 nematode species range from short insertions to **nearly the entire ~1.4-Mb *Wolbachia* genome in *Drosophila ananassae***, which is paternally inherited in a Mendelian pattern, present in geographically dispersed lines, and partially transcribed (28 of 1,206 genes) [37].
- Leclercq et al. (2016) showed *Wolbachia* genes created a **new W sex chromosome in the pillbug *Armadillidium vulgare***—i.e., HGT itself triggered the evolution of a sex-determination system [38].
- Bioinformatic surveys estimate ~33% of sequenced arthropod genomes contain *Wolbachia*-host LGT, and ~70% of infected hosts may carry at least one transfer [37, 38].

### 2.4 Plant-parasitic nematodes: bacterial cell wall-degrading enzymes

The root-knot nematode *Meloidogyne incognita* genome contains **>60 genes across six protein families for plant cell-wall degradation**—cellulases, xylanases, polygalacturonases, pectate lyases, arabinanases, and expansin-like proteins—whose closest relatives are soil/plant-associated bacteria (e.g., *Ralstonia*, *Clavibacter*, *Clostridium*). Massive gene duplication followed acquisition, and proteins are secreted during migration and, surprisingly, at late sedentary stages (including egg laying), suggesting additional roles in parasitism [39]. Earlier work in *Meloidogyne* independently recovered rhizobial-type genes (e.g., *nodL*, glutamine synthetase) with codon-usage amelioration matching nematode genomes [40]. In the free-living nematode *Pristionchus pacificus*, cellulases acquired from an amoebozoan donor were functionally integrated: transcribed, translated, enzymatically active, with 13–16 introns gained after transfer and signatures of positive selection [41]. HGT of cell wall-degrading enzymes from bacteria and fungi is now considered "a prerequisite for successful plant parasitism" in at least three independent nematode lineages [39, 41].

### 2.5 Tardigrades: a cautionary tale of contamination

Boothby et al. (2015) claimed that ~17.5% of the tardigrade *Hypsibius dujardini* genome was acquired via HGT, including stress-tolerance genes, and proposed that desiccation-induced DNA breakage and membrane leakiness facilitate DNA uptake [42]. Re-analysis by two independent groups showed the original assembly was heavily contaminated—~27% of the sequence was bacterial (including near-complete bacterial genomes) [43, 44]. After rigorous filtering, the real HGT proportion is **at most 1–2%**, comparable to other animals. The episode remains the field's most instructive lesson about contamination control in eukaryotic HGT claims [42, 43, 44].

### 2.6 Other notable animal cases

- **Bracovirus-mediated transfers:** Polydnavirus (bracovirus) DNA—injected by thousands of parasitoid wasp species into caterpillar hosts—has inserted repeatedly into lepidopteran genomes, including the monarch and silkworm. Transferred C-type lectin genes are under purifying selection; one gene impairs baculovirus actin-based motility, reducing viral production by >100-fold, suggesting adaptive antiviral function [44].
- **Whitefly plant-gene theft:** *Bemisia tabaci* acquired a plant phenolic glucoside malonyltransferase gene (BtPMaT1) that neutralizes plant toxins—an adaptive plant-to-insect transfer enabling a major agricultural pest to feed on poisonous hosts [45].
- **Trypanosome transfer to animals (other metazoa):** several additional fungus-to-insect transfers (e.g., coffee berry borer mannanase) demonstrate that insect-plant and insect-fungus HGT, though rare, repeatedly provides metabolic novelties [44, 45].
- **Humans and vertebrates:** claims of hundreds of bacterial genes in the human genome (2001; re-asserted by Crisp et al. 2015) were systematically debunked: after reanalysis, virtually all candidates fail BLAST-based, phylogenetic, or contamination screens. The best-known candidate—hyaluronan synthase—is best explained by gene loss and rate variation. Steven Salzberg concludes that "no genes have been horizontally transferred into the human genome" beyond mitochondria and retroviruses [49]. No credible bacterial HGT has been found in any vertebrate genome, underscoring how exceptional animal HGT is [47, 49].

## 3. Mechanisms of HGT in Plants and Animals

Given that bacteria-typical conjugation, transduction, and natural transformation do not operate in multicellular eukaryotes, how does foreign DNA enter genomes? The primary literature supports a set of pathway-specific mechanisms, often converging on "weak links" in the life cycle where germ cells or early developmental stages are exposed.

**3.1 Haustorial connections (parasitic plants).** The haustorium is a multicellular invasive organ that penetrates host stems or roots and connects to vascular tissue, exchanging water, nutrients, proteins, mRNAs, small RNAs, pathogens, and retrotransposons [5, 53]. Host-parasite intimacy is the single strongest predictor of plant HGT; holoparasites show far more transfers than hemiparasites, and nonparasites show almost none [3, 53]. The obligate parasite *Cuscuta* even forms symplastic connections allowing thousands of host mRNAs to move bidirectionally [12].

**3.2 mRNA-mediated transfer.** The ShContig9484 gene in *Striga* carries traces of an mRNA intermediate (poly-A tail) [53]. At the other extreme, Mower et al. (2010) proved that three *Cuscuta* mitochondrial genes (*atp1*, *atp6*, *matR*) moved to *Plantago* as a single **DNA-level** transfer event—retention of ancestral RNA-editing cytidines rules out any RNA intermediate [29]. Most functional nuclear transfers appear DNA-mediated because introns are present [3, 13].

**3.3 Mitochondrial fusion and whole-genome capture.** Green-plant mitochondria fuse via a mechanism independent of the dynamin-related GTPases used by animals and fungi; this permits *Amborella* to capture entire mitochondrial genomes from epiphytes and recombine them into its own [2]. Mitochondria are the most frequent recipients of alien DNA generally, attributed to their frequent fusion/fission and high noncoding content [53].

**3.4 Natural Agrobacterium transformation.** The T4SS-mediated T-DNA transfer that genetic engineers exploit also happens in nature: *A. rhizogenes* transformed ancestral *Nicotiana*, *Linaria*, and sweet potato, with the resulting cT-DNAs inherited, expressed, and sometimes selected during domestication [24, 25, 26, 27].

**3.5 Transposable elements as carriers.** TEs exist in extrachromosomal forms during transposition, making them unusually mobile between genomes. El Baidouri et al. (2014) found horizontal TE transfer (HTT) in 65% of 40 plant genomes, extrapolating to >2 million HTTs among flowering-plant genera in the last ~3 million years, and argued HTT is an "escape route" from host silencing that explains TE long-term persistence [28]. Host retrotransposons are also among the genes transferred into *Striga* [5], and transposon-rich regions of the *Sapria* genome are hypothesized to be "engines driving HGT" [10].

**3.6 Desiccation, DNA repair, and asexual life cycles.** In bdelloid rotifers, desiccation causes chromosome fragmentation; rehydration triggers DNA repair that may inadvertently incorporate foreign DNA. The aquatic-rotifer comparison supports this: species from desiccating habitats have threefold higher HGT gain rates [31], and the *A. vaga* genome shows expansion of RNA-silencing machinery and evidence of gene conversion substituting for meiosis [32]. Huang's "weak-link model" generalizes this: HGT is most likely at unicellular or early developmental stages (spores, zygotes, embryos, gametophytes), and asexual organisms, in which every cell is potentially germline, should show elevated HGT [47, 48]. Fern gametophytes—small, cuticle-free, with exposed sex organs—are a textbook example, plausibly explaining neochrome acquisition [19]. The same logic applies to moss protonemata and weakly protected zygotes at the origin of land plants [21].

**3.7 Vectors: grafting, pollen, fungi, insects.** Grasses may acquire genes via wind-borne "illegitimate pollination" [54]; rhizomatous growth creates root inosculation and graft-like fusion, statistically associated with elevated LGT [16]. Grafting between *Nicotiana* species can exchange complete chloroplasts and generate allopolyploids de novo [53]. Mycorrhizal and endophytic fungi are proposed as "bridges" connecting unrelated plants (the wood-wide web) [20], aphids as vectors of grass rDNA [18], and *Epichloë* endophytes donated *Fhb7* to wheatgrass [23]. In animals, parasitoid wasps vector *Wolbachia* between species, and bracoviruses physically deliver wasp DNA into caterpillar germlines [37, 44].

**3.8 Endosymbiosis as the master HGT.** Mitochondria and plastids themselves are products of endosymbiotic gene transfer (EGT), and heritable germline endosymbionts such as *Wolbachia* give lysed bacterial DNA direct access to gamete nuclei—the likely reason insect/nematode genomes are littered with *Wolbachia* fragments [37, 48].

**3.9 Barriers.** Against these entry routes, multicellular eukaryotes deploy germline sequestration, sexual reproduction (which dilutes and purges foreign DNA), RNA-directed DNA methylation, small-RNA silencing, and—in fungi—RIP/MIP/ MSUD systems. Transferred genes are frequently silenced via pseudogenization and methylation (e.g., *Hordeum* rDNA [18]; *Cuscuta* HGT-derived 24-nt siRNAs [13]; degenerate grass LGT genes with elevated gene-body methylation [52]).

## 4. Evolutionary Significance and Adaptive Value

### 4.1 Demonstrably adaptive transfers

Several HGT events confer benefits that could not plausibly have arisen by vertical inheritance alone, satisfying the "novel capability" criterion:

- **Strigolactone perception in parasitic plants.** The KAI2d clade of strigolactone receptors expanded dramatically in Orobanchaceae and enables germination in response to host root exudates; introducing a parasite KAI2 into *Arabidopsis* transfers the trait. This is convergent evolution of a host-detection mechanism—and the strigolactone receptor itself traces to a proteobacterial RsbQ gene via ancient HGT [6].
- **Fern neochrome.** A chimeric photoreceptor from hornworts gave ferns a private light-sensing adaptation in shaded environments, associated with their diversification in the angiosperm-dominated Cretaceous [19].
- ***Fhb7* in wheat.** Fungus-derived glutathione S-transferase detoxifies trichothecene mycotoxins and confers both Fusarium head blight and crown rot resistance with no yield penalty—protecting a crop against a pathogen causing billions of dollars in annual losses [23].
- **Aphid carotenogenesis.** Fungal genes created a de novo animal pathway for carotenoid biosynthesis, controlling predator-relevant coloration [34] and, more recently, temperature-gated light-dependent ATP production during cold stress [35].
- **Nematode cell wall-degrading enzymes.** Bacterial cellulases, pectate lyases, and expansins enabled the evolution of plant parasitism in *Meloidogyne* and other nematodes [39, 40, 41].
- **Microbial genes at land-plant origin.** Hundreds of bacterium- and fungus-derived genes retained over ~450 million years—including auxin biosynthesis, aluminum resistance (ALS3/NAP3), desiccation-related LEA2 proteins, and anti-herbivore lectins with experimentally demonstrated insect mortality—indicate that HGT was integral to terrestrialization [21, 22].
- **Rotifer antimicrobial biosynthetic clusters.** Bacterial-type NRP/PKS gene clusters are upregulated during fungal infection, with copy-number differences correlating with pathogen resistance across species—"domesticated" microbial chemical defense in an animal [33].
- **Whitefly detoxification.** The plant-derived BtPMaT1 gene neutralizes plant toxins and underpins host-plant adaptation in a global pest [45].
- **Microbiota-derived brassinosteroid gene.** The *Leifsonia* DET2 homolog rescues the *Arabidopsis det2* dwarf phenotype, proving functional cross-kingdom transfer with a measurable phenotype [50].
- **NAS metallophore synthesis.** Repeated acquisitions of nicotianamine synthase from microbes are functionally essential in mosses (knockouts fail to grow, photosynthesize, or reproduce sexually) and may compensate for the absence of mycorrhizal associations [51].

Selection analyses consistently find **purifying selection predominates** on functional transfers in Orobanchaceae [3], Rafflesiaceae [8], *Sapria* [11], *Cuscuta* [13], bracovirus-derived lepidopteran genes [44], and fungal cellulases in nematodes [41], with smaller subsets under positive selection. The convergent independent acquisition of the same gene families in *Cuscuta* and Orobanchaceae—both expressed at the host-parasite interface—is arguably the strongest comparative evidence for adaptive retention [13].

### 4.2 Neutral and deleterious fates

Most transferred DNA is not adaptive. In *Amborella*, three-quarters of foreign mitochondrial genes are pseudogenes [2]; in *Plantago*, *Cuscuta*-derived mitochondrial genes decayed immediately after transfer and are untranscribed [29]; in *Hordeum*, transferred panicoid rDNA is silenced and retained as selectively neutral DNA [18]; in grasses generally, 68–73% of laterally acquired genes show reduced expression, higher truncation rates, elevated gene-body methylation, and are lost at rates ~500× higher than vertically inherited genes [52]. Transferred transposable elements are frequently epigenetically inactivated [17]. The fact that HGT "is very common in microbes" but mostly neutral in plants fits the view that eukaryotic genomes are generally refractory to foreign DNA, with selection occasionally "rescuing" a rare beneficial integration [48, 52].

### 4.3 Can HGT replace sex?

Bdelloid rotifers—asexual for tens of millions of years—carry ~10% foreign genes. Flot et al. (2013) proposed that "gene conversion and horizontal gene transfer may have replaced the homogenizing and diversifying roles of sex," with HGT providing the genetic novelty that obligate asexuality would otherwise deny [32]. This hypothesis remains debated, but the enriched functional categories (hydrolases, oxidoreductases, stress-response and antimicrobial genes) support a role for HGT in ecological adaptability [31, 32, 33].

## 5. Comparative Framing: Why HGT Is Far Rarer in Plants and Animals

### 5.1 The prokaryotic baseline

In bacteria and archaea, HGT is universal and continuous: transformation (a regulated, ~40-gene competence program in *Bacillus subtilis*), transduction (phage-mediated), conjugation (T4SS plasmids), and gene transfer agents all operate routinely. In a 200-generation *Bacillus* evolution experiment, gene transfer swapped ~12% of the core genome between subspecies near the species boundary—at ~11 genes/hour, vastly exceeding mutation rates—with ~40% of transfers inferred adaptive and a repeatable ~5% fitness gain in hybrids [57]. HGT is the primary mechanism of antibiotic resistance spread and metabolic innovation [47, 57].

### 5.2 The eukaryotic deficit, quantified

By contrast, eukaryotic genomes contain orders of magnitude fewer foreign genes:

- Unicellular eukaryotes (best-case): *Galdieria sulphuraria* ~5% [48]; rumen ciliates ~4% [47].
- Fungi: 0.21% of genes across 750 genomes [47, 48].
- Plants: >600 plant-to-plant cases reported, with >95% of non-parasitic cases in Poaceae; >800 microbe-derived genes, mostly ancient [53].
- Animals: bdelloid rotifers ~8–10% [30, 31, 32], tardigrades ≤1–2% [43], *C. elegans* ~0.8%, *Drosophila* ~0.3%, primates ~0.5% [43], humans ~0 [49].

Keeling & Palmer (2008) noted "extant eukaryotes arose by HGT on the grandest scale possible"—the endosymbioses that created mitochondria and plastids—yet ongoing HGT in eukaryotes "varies enormously, from zero to hundreds of bacterial genes per genome," with no credible cases in vertebrates [47]. Keeling's more recent synthesis frames the paradox: phagocytosis and endosymbiosis should *increase* eukaryote HGT, but "any increased chance of acquiring new genes through phagocytosis and endosymbiosis is offset by a reduced need for these genes in eukaryotes, because selection in most eukaryotes operates on variation not readily generated by HGT" [47].

### 5.3 Explanations for the difference

1. **Germline sequestration.** In animals, only germ cells transmit DNA; somatic HGT is an evolutionary dead end unless gametes or their progenitors are exposed. *Wolbachia*'s success as a vector stems precisely from its presence in germline tissues [37, 48]. In plants, germ cells arise late from meristems, and vegetative reproduction (rhizomes, tubers) creates alternative routes [16, 53].
2. **Sexual reproduction and reproductive isolation.** Meiosis and syngamy create genetic bottlenecks that purge foreign sequences; HGT bypasses these barriers only when it occurs in cells that will form gametes [47, 48].
3. **Genome defenses.** RNA-directed DNA methylation, small-RNA silencing, and heterochromatization silence foreign DNA (documented for cT-DNAs, TE transfers, and grass LGT) [17, 18, 19, 28, 52]. Fungi add RIP/MIP/MSUD [47, 48].
4. **The complexity hypothesis.** Jain, Rivera & Lake (1999) showed that informational genes (transcription, translation—members of large, complex systems) transfer far less than operational genes (metabolism) in prokaryotes, because a transferred component must fit into a pre-existing complex; by extension, the highly integrated regulatory and developmental networks of multicellular eukaryotes make most foreign gene products nonfunctional or deleterious [46]. The "simplicity hypothesis" formalizes this: transferability decreases with protein connectivity [46].
5. **Donor availability and opportunity.** Bacterial population sizes and gene pools are vastly larger, and conjugation/transformation directly deliver DNA. Plants and animals require extraordinary routes—haustoria, grafting, endosymbiont lysis in germ cells, or desiccation-repair mishaps—each of which is rare and stochastic [47, 48].
6. **The "weak-link" life-cycle filter.** HGT is concentrated at exposed unicellular stages (spores, gametes, embryos, gametophytes), which are brief and protected in most multicellular organisms—explaining why frequency is elevated in mosses, ferns, and asexual animals [21, 47, 48].

## 6. Timescales and Historical Context

### 6.1 History of discovery

- **Pre-genomics era (1990s–2000):** Early reviews (Kidwell 1993; Rosewich & Kistler 2000) cataloged anecdotal cases—fungal linear plasmids transferred by co-culture, group I introns moving between fungi and plant mitochondria—and defined criteria (phylogenetic incongruence, patchy distribution, compositional anomalies) long before genome-scale data [47].
- **2001: the human genome claim and its chilling effect.** The first draft human genome suggested ~100 genes had been transferred from bacteria. This was soon debunked, and the episode "chilled the field," reinforcing skepticism that made subsequent discoveries harder to publish [47, 49].
- **2003–2013: plant mitochondrial HGT comes of age.** Bergthorsson et al. (2003, *Nature*) found widespread mitochondrial HGT among flowering plants; Bergthorsson et al. (2004, *PNAS*) documented massive HGT into *Amborella* [1]; Hao et al. (2010) revealed "gorgeous mosaics" of mitochondrial genes created by transfer plus gene conversion; Rice et al. (2013, *Science*) capped the era with the 3.9-Mb *Amborella* mitogenome containing whole foreign genomes [2].
- **2008–2013: the animal revolution.** Gladyshev et al. (2008, *Science*) reported massive HGT in bdelloid rotifers [30]; Moran & Jarvik (2010, *Science*) showed fungal carotenoid genes in aphids [34]; the *A. vaga* genome (Flot et al. 2013, *Nature*) tied HGT to asexuality [32].
- **2010–2021: parasitic plants and crop genomics.** Yoshida et al. (2010) established host-to-parasite nuclear transfer [4]; Danchin et al. (2010) revealed bacterial cell wall-degrading enzymes in nematodes [39]; Kim et al. (2014) found transcriptome-scale mRNA exchange in *Cuscuta* [12]; Yang et al. (2016) quantified HGT scaling with parasitism [3]; Dunning et al. (2019) and Hibdige et al. (2021) showed LGT is common across grasses [15, 16]; Wang et al. (2020) cloned the fungus-derived *Fhb7* resistance gene [23]; the *Sapria* genome (Cai et al. 2021) revealed extreme gene loss plus host-gene graveyards [10].
- **2015–2016: the tardigrade controversy and methodological hardening.** The claim of 17.5% HGT in *H. dujardini* [42] was overturned by contamination-aware re-sequencing [43, 44], establishing new community standards: assembly-quality metrics, k-mer spectra, blobplots, metagenomic filtering, and physical linkage evidence (long reads, PCR across junctions) before any HGT claim is accepted.
- **2022–2025: macroevolutionary synthesis.** Ma et al. (2022) identified major HGT episodes at land-plant origins [22]; the NAS study (2025) revealed recurrent microbial acquisitions of a single gene family across plants [51]; plant-microbiota screens found pervasive low-level transfers [50].

### 6.2 Methodological advances

Modern HGT detection rests on **(i) phylogenetic incongruence**—the gold standard, requiring dense taxon sampling to avoid long-branch attraction; **(ii) compositional and synteny evidence** (GC content, codon usage, intron positions, conserved genomic neighborhoods); **(iii) HGT-index/alien-index sequence screens**; and **(iv) contamination controls** (independent DNA preps, PCR across gene-genome junctions, long-read co-localization, metagenomic binning, expression evidence). The field's history shows that "surrogate methods should never be used in isolation"—the 2001 human claim and the tardigrade case both failed when phylogenomic and contamination-aware scrutiny was applied [47, 49]. Conversely, insufficient taxon sampling generated many false positives in early parasitic-plant screens, resolved by validation across additional species [3].

## 7. Evaluation: Overall Significance, Debates, and Open Questions

### 7.1 Overall significance

The accumulated evidence justifies a revised view: HGT is a **minor but real and occasionally decisive** force in plant and animal evolution. It cannot rival gene duplication and mutation as a source of variation in most lineages, but it can deliver **functionally complete, pre-tested genetic inventions that would be essentially impossible to assemble vertically**—a chimeric photoreceptor (neochrome), a complete carotenoid pathway in an animal, a mycotoxin-detoxifying enzyme in wheat, cell wall-degrading arsenals in nematodes, strigolactone receptors in parasites, and hundreds of microbial metabolic genes at the origin of land plants [19, 21, 22, 23, 34, 39, 51]. The convergent, interface-localized retention of independently acquired genes in distantly related parasites is compelling evidence that natural selection filters and keeps functional HGTs [13].

At the same time, the majority of transferred DNA is neutral junk or becomes silenced—*Amborella*'s mitochondrial "graveyard" and the rapid decay of most grass LGTs show that eukaryotic genomes are not passive receptacles [2, 52]. The evolutionary significance of plant/animal HGT is therefore best summarized as **occasionally transformative, usually inconsequential, and always conditional on the same selective filter that governs all mutations**.

### 7.2 Major debates and open questions

1. **How much HGT is "real"?** The tardigrade episode and the debunked human claims demonstrate that frequency estimates are only as good as contamination controls. Current consensus bounds functional HGT at ~1% of genes in most animals/plants, with rotifers and some parasites as outliers [30, 43, 47, 49].
2. **What are the actual vectors in non-parasitic systems?** The mechanism of grass LGT remains "the million-dollar question"—wind-borne illegitimate pollination, root grafting, and shared fungi are all hypotheses without direct observation [54]. No one has yet witnessed a plant-to-plant nuclear transfer occurring.
3. **Why do mitochondria accept so much foreign DNA while plastids and animal mitochondria accept almost none?** The fusion-compatibility model explains *Amborella*, but the striking contrast between plant mitochondrial permissiveness and plastid/animal-mitochondrial resistance remains mechanistically incomplete [2, 53].
4. **What determines retention versus loss?** New work shows co-transfer of cis-regulatory elements predicts functional retention of laterally acquired genes, implying that "promoter capture" may be the key filter [52]. Whether this generalizes awaits more genomes.
5. **Does HGT substitute for sex?** The rotifer hypothesis—that HGT provides novelty in obligate asexuals—is plausible but "several key steps remain untested" [32, 33].
6. **How prevalent is microbe-to-animal HGT?** The *Wolbachia* phenomenon is well understood, but the broader contribution of gut/skin/parasite microbiomes to animal germlines—hypothesized to be significant—remains largely unquantified [48].
7. **Detection windows and underestimation.** HGT is easiest to detect soon after transfer; ancient transfers become compositionally ameliorated and obscured by differential loss, so published frequencies are lower bounds [47, 48, 53].
8. **Practical applications.** If the mechanisms of natural plant HGT were understood, "we may be able to naturally modify crops and make them more resistant to climate change" [54]; the sweet potato T-DNA and *Fhb7* introgressions already show that naturally transferred genes can be harnessed in breeding [23, 24].

### 7.3 Concluding assessment

Horizontal gene transfer in plants and animals is rare—orders of magnitude rarer than in microbes—because germline barriers, sexuality, genome defenses, and the complexity of eukaryotic regulatory networks conspire against it. But rarity is not triviality. The cases reviewed here—*Amborella*'s stolen mitogenomes, ferns' hornwort photoreceptor, parasites' host-detection and virulence genes, aphids' fungal carotenoid pathway, nematodes' bacterial cell wall-degrading arsenals, rotifers' domesticated antibiotic clusters, and wheat's fungal resistance gene—demonstrate that on evolutionary timescales, even vanishingly rare events can deliver innovations that shape the diversification of entire clades. The field's history also carries a methodological lesson: extraordinary claims require extraordinary, contamination-proof evidence, and the maturing standards of phylogenomics are what separate the genuine cases from the artifacts. The next decade of long-read sequencing, experimental evolution, and synthetic-biology validation promises to resolve how—and how often—the tree of life's branches are grafted together.
