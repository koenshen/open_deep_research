# Avian Migratory Navigation: Sensory Mechanisms, Map-Based Position Determination, Cue Integration, and Disrupting Factors

## 1. Introduction

Bird migration requires solving two distinct navigational problems: knowing **which direction** to fly (compass/heading information) and knowing **where you are** relative to the destination (map/positional information). This two-step "map-and-compass" framework, first formalized by Gustav Kramer in the 1950s, remains the conceptual foundation of the field [1][3]. The evidence accumulated over six decades of behavioral experiments, radar studies, and more recently GPS tracking and virtual-reality magnetic manipulations shows that birds possess multiple, partially redundant compass systems — geomagnetic, solar, stellar, and polarized-light based — as well as several candidate "map" systems based on magnetic field parameters, atmospheric odors, and possibly infrasound [1][2][3].

The picture that emerges is one of hierarchical, experience-dependent integration: innate magnetic compass information is calibrated early in life by celestial rotation, then recalibrated on a daily basis from twilight cues during migration; experienced adults add learned map-based navigation that allows true goal-directed correction, while first-time migrants rely on inherited "clock-and-compass" programs [2][30][46]. The relative weighting of cues shifts with ontogeny, time of day, geography, and weather, and this finely balanced system is vulnerable to both natural perturbations (geomagnetic storms, weather) and anthropogenic disturbances (artificial light at night, electromagnetic noise, habitat change) [52][54][57].

## 2. Compass Mechanisms: How Birds Obtain Directional (Heading) Information

### 2.1 The Geomagnetic Compass

**Discovery and basic properties.** The avian magnetic compass was discovered in the 1960s by Wolfgang Wiltschko, who showed that European Robins (*Erithacus rubecula*) orient their migratory restlessness (*Zugunruhe*) according to the direction of the ambient magnetic field; the landmark 1972 *Science* paper by Wiltschko & Wiltschko formalized the "inclination compass" concept [4][5]. Unlike a human-built polarity compass, the avian magnetic compass is **axial**: birds do not distinguish magnetic "north" from magnetic "south." Instead, they sense the **inclination** of the field lines — the angle at which they dip relative to gravity — and use the distinction between "poleward" (field lines pointing downward in the Northern Hemisphere) and "equatorward" (pointing upward) as the directional reference [4][6]. This was demonstrated by inverting the vertical component of the field, which reverses the birds' preferred heading, whereas inverting the horizontal component does not [5][6].

The compass operates only within a "functional window" of field intensities near the local value: European Robins kept at Frankfurt's ~46 µT field oriented at 43 and 54 µT but became disoriented at 34 and 60 µT, although they could adapt to a wider range with prolonged exposure [4]. The compass is also strictly **light-dependent**: birds orient under UV, blue, turquoise, and green light but are disoriented under yellow and red light — the classic 1993 *Nature* experiment showed robins orienting normally under 571 nm green light but randomly under 633 nm red light [7][8]. Covering the right eye abolishes magnetic compass orientation, indicating strong lateralization to the right eye/left hemisphere [4][6].

**The radical-pair mechanism.** The leading mechanistic explanation for the light-dependent compass is the **radical-pair hypothesis**, originally proposed by Schulten et al. (1978) and developed by Ritz, Adem & Schulten (2000), which proposes that cryptochrome proteins in the retina form spin-correlated radical pairs whose singlet/triplet reaction ratio is modulated by the geomagnetic field [4][9]. Key requirements and predictions of the model — anisotropic hyperfine coupling, spin coherence lasting >1 µs, and aligned receptor molecules — are laid out in Rodgers & Hore's 2009 *PNAS* review [9]. Behavioral support comes from resonance experiments: Ritz, Wiltschko, Hore and colleagues (2009, *Biophysical Journal*) showed that European Robins tested in Frankfurt (local field ~46–47 µT, Larmor frequency 1.315 MHz) were disoriented by oscillating fields at the Larmor frequency at intensities as low as 15 nT, and that this sensitivity scaled with field intensity (in a 92 µT field, birds became most sensitive at 2.63 MHz) — implying a magnetically isolated electron spin, consistent with a flavin–superoxide radical pair in cryptochrome [10]. The earlier 2004 *Nature* paper by Ritz et al. demonstrated that a 7 MHz oscillating field of only 0.47 µT disoriented robins when applied at 24° or 48° to the geomagnetic field, but not when parallel — a diagnostic resonance effect that magnetite-based receptors could not produce [11].

**Cryptochrome and the visual pathway.** Five cryptochrome types (Cry1a, Cry1b, Cry2, Cry4a, Cry4b) have been identified in bird retinas. Cry1a is located in the outer segments of UV/V-cones and is considered the most likely magnetoreceptor molecule; Cry4 is expressed more strongly in the migratory season [4][12]. Garden warbler cryptochromes, recombinantly expressed, form radical pairs lasting ~10 milliseconds — long enough to detect Earth-strength fields — and absorb in the blue spectral range matching the wavelengths that support behavioral orientation [13]. Mouritsen et al. (2004, *Current Biology*) showed that caged migratory garden warblers perform characteristic **head-scanning** movements before choosing a direction, and that in a zero-magnetic field they nearly tripled scanning frequency and then oriented randomly — demonstrating that the sensor is in the head and that scanning is used to locate the magnetic reference direction [14]. Molecular mapping identified **Cluster N**, a visual forebrain area (part of the Wulst) that is active at night during magnetic orientation in night-migratory birds but inactive in non-migratory species [13][15]. Surgically deactivating Cluster N in European Robins abolished magnetic compass orientation while leaving sun and star compasses intact — and, critically, cutting the ophthalmic branch of the trigeminal nerve had no effect on the compass, disproving the hypothesis that iron-mineral receptors in the beak mediate the compass [15][16]. The current consensus is that birds effectively **"see" the magnetic field** as a visual modulation pattern superimposed on the retina [4][15].

**Magnetite-based receptors and the "map" component.** A second magnetoreception system, based on iron-mineral (magnetite/maghemite) particles, is located in the upper beak and innervated by the ophthalmic branch of the trigeminal nerve. Fleissner and colleagues described elaborate iron-containing dendrites in the beak skin of pigeons, robins, garden warblers, and chickens [60], and Heyers et al. (2010, *PNAS*) showed that magnetic field changes activate the trigeminal brainstem complex (PrV/SpV) in robins [18]. However, the interpretation of these structures as magnetoreceptors has been challenged: a 2012 *Nature* paper by Treiber et al. argued the iron-rich beak cells are macrophages, not neurons [5][17]. Despite this controversy, the behavioral evidence that the trigeminal system carries **positional** (map) rather than compass information is strong. Magnetic pulse experiments — brief 0.5 T pulses that remagnetize magnetite but leave radical-pair chemistry unaffected — cause experienced (but not naive) migratory birds such as Australian Silvereyes to shift direction by ~90°, an effect lasting days and interpreted as false positional information [17][19]. Similarly, in homing pigeons, local anesthesia of the beak suppressed the disorienting effect of a magnetic anomaly, implicating beak receptors in magnetic map perception [17]. A series of "virtual displacement" experiments in Eurasian Reed Warblers (section 3.2) confirmed that an intact trigeminal nerve is necessary for magnetic-map-based reorientation [20]. The current consensus, summarized by Wiltschko & Wiltschko (2019), is that birds have two magnetic senses: a light-dependent radical-pair compass in the eye (optic nerve → Cluster N) and a magnetite-based intensity/map sensor in the beak region (trigeminal nerve → brainstem), although the exact receptor structures remain debated [6][17].

### 2.2 The Sun Compass

Gustav Kramer's orientation-cage experiments with starlings in the 1950s established that birds can use the **sun's azimuth** as a compass, compensating for the sun's ~15°/hour apparent movement using an internal circadian clock [21][22]. The time-compensated nature of the sun compass was proven by **clock-shift experiments**: shifting a bird's circadian clock by 6 hours causes a ~90° error in heading, and a 12-hour shift causes a 180° reversal [22][23]. Schmidt-Koenig's systematic releases of clock-shifted homing pigeons at 15–200 km confirmed that 6-hour shifts produce ~90° deviations while 2-hour shifts (predicting ~30° error) produce none — exactly as the compass hypothesis predicts, and ruling out a navigational (map) role for the sun [23]. The sun compass is **learned**, not innate: young birds must observe the sun's arc, and its use is tied to the hippocampus and circadian pacemaker systems (pineal, SCN, retina) [1][21]. Guilford & Taylor have argued that the traditional "time-compensated compass" paradigm may obscure other ways solar information is used — e.g., as a simple heading indicator (gyroscope) or via constant-offset corrections that need not be fully time-compensated [24]. The sun compass is primarily a tool of **diurnal** migrants (starlings, pigeons, raptors, storks); it is useless to nocturnal migrants flying after sunset [22].

### 2.3 The Star Compass

Franz Sauer's planetarium experiments in the 1950s first demonstrated stellar orientation in nocturnal migrants, but the definitive work came from Stephen Emlen, who raised Indigo Buntings (*Passerina cyanea*) in a planetarium and showed that they learn a **star compass** by watching the rotation of the night sky during their juvenile period [22][25]. Emlen's classic experiments (1967, *Auk*; 1970, *Science*) revealed that buntings do not innately know which star is "north": young birds exposed to a false sky rotating around Betelgeuse treated Betelgeuse as the celestial pole and learned their migratory direction relative to it [25][26]. The star compass is therefore a **learned sky map** based on the geometry of constellations around the axis of celestial rotation, not on individual stars, and it is not time-compensated (the same star pattern indicates the same direction all night) [1][25]. Emlen also showed that celestial rotation is only effective if the stars move in the natural direction — reversing the rotation prevented learning [26]. The star compass is of primary importance to nocturnal passerine migrants, though its relative weight versus the magnetic compass varies (section 4).

### 2.4 Polarized Light and Twilight Cues

At sunrise and sunset, the pattern of skylight polarization near the horizon provides a reliable, geography-independent reference to the solar meridian, and several lines of evidence indicate that birds use these patterns to **calibrate their other compasses**. Phillips & Moore (1992) showed that migratory warblers exposed to a rotated polarized-light pattern at sunset shifted their orientation accordingly, and that polarized light, not the sun's disc position per se, was the effective calibration stimulus [28]. Muheim, Phillips & Åkesson (2006, *Science*) demonstrated that Savannah Sparrows use polarized light from the sky region near the horizon to recalibrate their magnetic compass at both sunrise and sunset, proposing that skylight polarization provides the primary absolute directional reference for all compass systems of migratory songbirds [27]. Muheim's 2011 review details the physical basis — the e-vector of skylight is always perpendicular to the scattering plane, with maximum polarization at 90° from the sun — and notes that birds need a view of the lowest 5–10° of sky near the horizon for calibration, which is why Emlen funnels that restrict the sky view can produce misleading results [29]. The exact receptor mechanism for polarized light (likely double cones) remains less well characterized than magnetoreception [29].

### 2.5 Olfactory Cues

Floriano Papi's 1971–72 experiments in Tuscany showed that homing pigeons with sectioned olfactory nerves failed to return from unfamiliar release sites, launching the olfactory navigation hypothesis — the most controversial topic in bird navigation [31][32]. Two versions exist: Papi's **mosaic model** (birds learn a map of local wind-borne odors around the loft) and Wallraff's **gradient model** (birds compare concentrations of volatile compounds that vary along stable spatial gradients, allowing bicoordinate position fixing) [31][43]. Wallraff's experiments with pigeons raised in screened aviaries demonstrated that an "atmospheric factor" (wind-borne odors) is necessary for developing the navigational map [32].

The modern evidence, from Anna Gagliardo's group using GPS tracking, strongly supports a true olfactory map role rather than a mere motivational "activation" effect. In 2011, pigeons transported in air-tight containers and exposed to artificial fragrances (eucalyptus, rose, lavender) showed severely impaired homing — tortuous tracks, poor homeward orientation, and low homing efficiency — whereas control birds exposed to natural air oriented correctly [34]. In 2016, pigeons allowed to smell environmental air before being made anosmic (via zinc sulfate) still oriented homeward, while birds that never smelled natural air did not — showing that local odors at the release site are critical for determining the direction of displacement [35]. In 2018, Gagliardo, Pollonara & Wikelski confirmed that only natural local odors, not artificial ones, allow homeward orientation [33]. In free-ranging seabirds, anosmic Scopoli's Shearwaters showed impaired homeward orientation during pelagic homing (>40 km from coast) but normal coastal navigation, indicating an olfactory map used specifically over the open sea [37].

The chemical identity of the navigational odors remained unknown for 40 years until Zannoni et al. (2020, *Scientific Reports*) measured volatile organic compounds (VOCs) around a pigeon loft in Pisa, identifying three regional gradients — dimethyl sulfide (marine), aromatic compounds (anthropogenic), and monoterpenes (biogenic) — sloping in different directions, theoretically sufficient for triangulating the home direction [36]. This is the first direct measurement of candidate olfactory map cues, and the authors note that climate change may be altering VOC emission patterns, potentially degrading olfactory maps [36].

### 2.6 Infrasound

Kreithen & Quine (1979) demonstrated that homing pigeons can hear infrasound down to 0.05 Hz — frequencies far below human hearing — using classically conditioned heart-rate responses [38]. Jonathan Hagstrum (a geophysicist at the USGS) built on this to propose that birds navigate using **infrasonic "acoustic maps"**: microseisms generated by interfering ocean waves are reradiated as infrasound from topographic features, and birds listen to these signals to determine their position relative to home [39]. Hagstrum's 2000 *Journal of Experimental Biology* paper supported this with correlational evidence, including the remarkable observation that four major pigeon races in Europe and the northeastern US were disrupted in 1997–98, each coinciding with Concorde supersonic shock waves [39]. In 2013, he used atmospheric propagation modeling of 14 years of Cornell pigeon-release data to show that on days when pigeons were disoriented at particular release sites, infrasonic signals from the loft area were being bent away by temperature inversions and wind structure; when the sound reached the release site, pigeons oriented normally [40][41]. The infrasound hypothesis has been extended to seabirds, where Patrick et al. (2021) proposed three testable mechanisms (beacon, landmark, and gradient models) and noted that infrasonic cues propagate thousands of kilometers in the marine environment [42]. However, the hypothesis remains controversial: pigeons with cochleae removed have homed normally from 150–160 km, and no experiment has directly demonstrated infrasonic map use in wild migrants [42][43].

### 2.7 Landscape and Topographic Landmarks

Visual landmarks are undoubtedly important, especially for experienced birds in familiar areas. GPS tracking of homing pigeons has revealed that individuals develop idiosyncratic, stereotyped "route maps," following narrow flight corridors (often along linear features such as roads and railways) and deflecting toward their established routes from up to 1.5 km away when released off-route (Biro, Meade & Guilford 2004, *PNAS*) [1]. A 2026 study by Wikelski et al. released trained pigeons from a Cessna under clear skies, fog with distant Alpine landmarks visible, and fog with no landmarks: birds with distant mountain landmarks visible flew homeward (covering 96% of the distance in 20 minutes), while birds with all landmarks hidden showed poor orientation and descended through the fog — demonstrating that distant landscape features can be essential for homing when other cues are unavailable [1]. Even in long-distance migration, topographic "leading lines" (coastlines, mountain ridges, river valleys) serve as waypoints and guides [22]. However, passive exposure to a novel site's visual/olfactory cues is insufficient for pigeons to learn its navigational information (Morford et al. 2024, *Animal Behaviour*) — active exploration appears necessary [59].

## 3. Positional ("Map") Information: How Birds Know Where They Are

### 3.1 The Map-and-Compass Framework and True Navigation

Kramer's model separates the compass (direction) from the map (position), and Donald Griffin's classification distinguished Type II orientation (maintaining a direction) from Type III "true navigation" — the ability to return to a goal from unfamiliar territory without familiar landmarks or route information [1][3]. True navigation requires a map that can be extrapolated beyond the animal's familiar range, and the most widely accepted implementation is a **bicoordinate gradient map** using two or more environmental variables that vary predictably with latitude and longitude [3]. Displacement experiments provide the behavioral gold standard: Perdeck's (1958) mass displacement of European Starlings from the Netherlands to Switzerland showed that juveniles continued on their inherited southwest heading (clock-and-compass) while adults corrected their course toward their normal wintering grounds (true navigation) [2][3]. Similar true-navigation responses have been documented in displaced Manx Shearwaters (5,150 km), Laysan Albatrosses (6,630 km), White Storks (2,269 km), and White-crowned Sparrows (3,860 km) [3].

### 3.2 The Magnetic Map

The Earth's magnetic field offers several parameters that vary predictably in space: **total intensity**, **inclination** (varying mainly with latitude), and **declination** (the angle between magnetic and true north, varying mainly with longitude) [48][51]. The magnetic map hypothesis proposes that birds learn the local pattern of these parameters and use them to determine position. Direct evidence comes from "virtual displacement" experiments, in which birds are placed in a magnetic coil system that reproduces the field of a distant location while all other cues remain unchanged.

**Reed warblers.** In 2008, Chernetsov, Kishkinev & Mouritsen physically displaced Eurasian Reed Warblers (*Acrocephalus scirpaceus*) 1,000 km eastward during spring migration; the birds reoriented from northeast to northwest, compensating correctly for the displacement [48]. In 2017, Chernetsov et al. (*Current Biology*) rotated the magnetic declination by 8.5° while keeping intensity and inclination constant — a virtual displacement to Scotland, ~1,450 km away. Experienced adult reed warblers changed their heading by 151° (from WSW to ESE), consistent with compensating for the virtual displacement, while juveniles became randomly oriented [48][49]. This was the first demonstration that birds can use **magnetic declination** to solve the "longitude problem" — the east-west component of position [48][49]. The 2021 study by Kishkinev et al. went further: experienced reed warblers at Lake Neusiedl, Austria, exposed to a magnetic field matching Neftekamsk, Russia (~2,700 km northeast, beyond the species' range), reoriented southwest — the correct direction back toward their migratory corridor — providing direct evidence that birds can **extrapolate magnetic map information beyond their previous experience**, as a true gradient map requires [50]. A 2024 *Proceedings of the Royal Society B* study (Packmor et al.) showed that changing only inclination and declination (while keeping intensity constant) is sufficient to trigger compensatory reorientation, suggesting total intensity may not be a necessary map component in this species [50].

**Trigeminal necessity.** Pakhomov et al. (2018, *Scientific Reports*) combined virtual displacement with nerve surgery: sham-operated reed warblers exposed to the Zvenigorod field (1,000 km east) reoriented northwest, but birds with bilaterally sectioned ophthalmic branches of the trigeminal nerve (V1) did not — demonstrating that the trigeminal nerve carries essential magnetic map information [20]. This fits the two-system model (radical-pair compass in the eye; magnetite/trigeminal intensity sensor in the beak) described in section 2.1 [6][17][18].

**Age dependence and other taxa.** Magnetic map responses are consistently **experience-dependent**: juveniles without an established navigational map fail to respond to virtual displacements (reed warblers [48]) and to magnetic pulse treatments (silvereyes [19]). The review literature identifies two classes of magnetic maps: **inherited** signpost maps used by first-time migrants (e.g., thrush nightingales increasing fuel deposition when exposed to the magnetic field of northern Egypt before the Sahara crossing) and **learned** gradient maps used for goal navigation [17][51]. Not all species appear sensitive to declination, however — European Robins and Garden Warblers did not respond to declination shifts in a 2020 study — indicating significant variation in navigational map organization across taxa [48]. A simulation study of greater white-fronted geese using daily geomagnetic field models found that a simple "geomagnetic taxis" (compass-type) strategy reproduced observed GPS trajectories better than map-based bicoordinate strategies, though multifactorial mechanisms were still indicated [51].

### 3.3 The Olfactory Map

As detailed in section 2.5, the olfactory map is the best-supported map system for **homing pigeons**, with 40 years of nerve-section, anosmia, air-filtration, and GPS-tracking experiments showing that pigeons need access to natural environmental odors to determine the home direction from unfamiliar sites [32][33][34][35]. Wallraff (2014) argues forcefully that no other cue system has survived experimental scrutiny for pigeon homing, and that olfactory navigation is not a redundant component but a necessary one [43]. The Zannoni et al. (2020) VOC analysis showed that pigeons could in principle triangulate position from three stable chemical gradients (DMS, aromatics, monoterpenes), although the mosaic model of unique site-specific odor signatures was not supported [36]. In wild migratory species, olfactory map use has been suggested by experiments on anosmic Gray Catbirds displaced 1,000 km (which failed to correct westward) and on Lesser Black-backed Gulls translocated 850–1,000 km (olfactory-nerve-sectioned birds could not compensate) [32]. Critics note that stable odor gradients beyond ~200 km have not been demonstrated, that deflector-loft results may be confounded by polarized-light effects, and that olfactory navigation is hard to reconcile with trans-oceanic migrations [3][31]. The field remains polarized, with Wallraff on one side, Gagliardo's GPS-tracking evidence on the other, and calls for standardized collaborative experiments [32].

### 3.4 The Infrasound Map

Hagstrum's infrasound hypothesis (section 2.6) is explicitly a **map** hypothesis: birds use the direction and pattern of arrival of low-frequency acoustic signals reradiated from topography to determine their position relative to home [39]. The atmospheric propagation modeling (Hagstrum 2013) provides a mechanistic explanation for long-standing anomalies in pigeon homing data, including why birds are disoriented at specific sites on specific days, and why young pigeons sometimes head in the opposite direction from home [40][41]. However, the cochlea-removal experiments and the absence of direct tests in migratory species keep the hypothesis at the level of a plausible but unproven mechanism [42][43]. Patrick et al. (2021) provide a testable framework for seabirds, proposing beacon, landmark, and gradient variants and discussing plausible detection mechanisms (interaural time/level differences, Doppler shifts, vestibular particle-motion sensing) [42].

## 4. Integration, Calibration, and Ontogeny

### 4.1 Innate Versus Learned Compasses

A crucial distinction in the navigational toolkit is that the **magnetic compass is innate** while the sun and star compasses are **learned** [1]. Hand-raised Savannah Sparrows with no outdoor experience still show axial magnetic orientation in their first autumn [1][44]. The learned celestial compasses are established in the presence of celestial rotation: Emlen showed that young buntings must watch the stars rotate to develop a functional star compass [26], and Able & Able (1990, *Nature*) demonstrated that rotating an artificial star pattern calibrates the preferred magnetic direction of young Savannah Sparrows — celestial rotation, which provides geographic directions both day and night, serves as the reference that aligns the innate magnetic compass with geography [44]. During ontogeny, celestial rotation thus provides the primary calibration reference [30][44].

### 4.2 Cue-Conflict Experiments: The Calibration Hierarchy

When cues conflict, which one wins? Muheim, Moore & Phillips (2006, *JEB*) reviewed 76 cue-conflict experiments and found a striking reversal of salience across the annual cycle [30]:

- **Premigratory period**: celestial cues — specifically sunset/sunrise polarized-light patterns near the horizon — are given greatest salience and are used to **recalibrate the magnetic compass**, in both juveniles and adults (5 of 6 experiments with horizon access showed magnetic recalibration).
- **During migration**: a majority of experiments show the opposite — birds rely primarily on the **magnetic field** and recalibrate celestial compasses to match it. In Wiltschko & Wiltschko's (1999) summary of repeated-exposure conflict experiments, all 11 samples across species (European warblers, robins, dunnocks, silvereyes, honeyeaters) ultimately followed magnetic cues, and celestial cues were recalibrated to the magnetic field [45].

The alternative explanation is that **access to the horizon** rather than season determines recalibration: birds with a full view of the sky down to the horizon recalibrated the magnetic compass in 8 of 9 studies, while birds with restricted sky views did not in 28 of 29 studies [30]. Either way, the conclusion is that under natural conditions there may be a **single calibration reference** — derived from the twilight polarized-light pattern near the horizon — to which all compass systems are aligned [27][30].

### 4.3 Daily Twilight Recalibration During Migration

Cochran, Mouritsen & Wikelski (2004, *Science*) provided the most dramatic evidence for ongoing calibration in free-flying birds. They exposed *Catharus* thrushes to eastward-turned magnetic fields during the twilight period before takeoff, then radio-tracked them for up to 1,100 km. Instead of heading north (their normal spring direction), the experimental birds flew **west**; on subsequent nights, the same individuals migrated northward again [46]. The interpretation is that the birds calibrate their magnetic compass **daily** from twilight cues (polarized light/sunset position), so an artificial magnetic shift at twilight caused them to set a wrong heading that night, but they returned to normal once the artificial field was removed [46]. This daily recalibration mechanism elegantly explains how birds can cross the magnetic equator (where the inclination compass becomes ambiguous) and cope with declination changes along their route [46][27].

### 4.4 First-Time Versus Experienced Migrants: Clock-and-Compass to True Navigation

Mouritsen's synthesis of ringing data from Scandinavian Pied Flycatchers showed that first-autumn migrants behave exactly as the "clock-and-compass" model predicts: they fly a genetically predetermined direction for a genetically predetermined duration, without compensating for displacement. Displaced young flycatchers (500 km south, 400 km west, or controls) all maintained the same SSW heading with overlapping confidence intervals [2]. Perdeck's starling experiment showed the same pattern in juveniles versus adults [2][3]. The precision of a single migratory leg is modest (angular SD ~52° for a 125-km step), yet the overall journey succeeds because the direction is averaged over many nights [2]. Experienced adults, in contrast, possess a learned map that allows true navigation, as shown by the long-distance displacement successes (albatrosses, shearwaters, storks, sparrows) and by the reed warbler virtual-displacement experiments, in which only adults (not juveniles) responded to declination shifts [3][48]. Thorup & Holland (2009) conclude that experienced birds effectively possess an "experience-based GPS," though its sensory basis (magnetic, olfactory, or both) remains unresolved [3].

### 4.5 Diurnal Versus Nocturnal Migrants

The cue hierarchy differs systematically between diurnal and nocturnal migrants. **Diurnal migrants** (starlings, pigeons, raptors, storks, cranes) rely primarily on the time-compensated sun compass, with magnetic cues as a backup (pigeons use magnetic orientation on overcast days) and landmark/olfactory maps in familiar/unfamiliar areas respectively [21][22][24]. **Nocturnal migrants** (most small passerines) face a different challenge: the sun is unavailable, so they rely on the magnetic compass as their basic all-weather tool, supplemented by the star compass and by twilight (polarized-light/sunset) cues gathered before takeoff [22]. The primacy of the magnetic compass for nocturnal migrants was demonstrated dramatically at Ottenby Bird Observatory, Sweden: European Robins tested in a nearly vertical (meaningless) magnetic field under simulated overcast showed completely random orientation, whereas controls in the natural field oriented well even under overcast [47]. Nocturnal migrants typically fly 300–600 km per night at 40–50 km/h, and the moon is not used as a compass cue [22][45].

## 5. Natural and Anthropogenic Disturbances That Disrupt Navigation

### 5.1 Natural Disturbances

#### 5.1.1 Weather: Wind, Rain, and Cloud Cover

Weather is arguably the most important natural disruptor of migration. Strong winds cause drift, and birds must actively compensate or be displaced; rain and low cloud force migrants to descend or stop. Radar studies in the Mediterranean have quantified how nocturnal migration intensity drops sharply with strong winds and precipitation (Erni, Liechti & Bruderer 2002, *JEB*) [22]. Overcast conditions remove celestial cues entirely, forcing birds to rely on the magnetic compass — and if that is also compromised (e.g., by a vertical magnetic field or electromagnetic noise), orientation collapses [47][57]. Lowery's classic 1951 radar study of trans-Gulf migrants showed that overcast skies over the Gulf of Mexico were associated with disorientation, supporting the idea that celestial cues are essential when flying over featureless terrain [22].

#### 5.1.2 Geomagnetic Storms and Disturbances

Solar activity and geomagnetic storms perturb the Earth's magnetic field on timescales of hours to days, potentially degrading both the compass and magnetic map. Moore (1977, *Science*) reported that geomagnetic disturbance influenced the orientation of free-flying nocturnal migrants, though the evidence did not prove geomagnetism is a cue [53]. The largest modern study, by Gulson-Castillo et al. (2023, *PNAS*), analyzed 23 years of weather-radar data from 37 stations in the US Great Plains (~1.7 million radar scans in fall, ~1.4 million in spring) alongside magnetometer data. During severe space weather events (geomagnetic disturbances > ~500 nT), **migration intensity decreased by 9–17%** in both seasons, and in fall there was evidence that birds had more difficulty flying against the wind (a 25.4% decrease in crosswind airspeed at high disturbance under overcast) — indicating navigational difficulty, especially among inexperienced juveniles migrating in autumn [54]. The 2023 *Scientific Reports* vagrancy study by Tonelli, Youngflesh & Tingley analyzed 2.2 million banding records of 152 species and found a strong positive association between geomagnetic disturbance and **vagrancy** (birds appearing far outside their normal range) during fall migration: a two-standard-deviation increase in disturbance was associated with a 24% increase in the vagrancy index and a 250% increase in spatiotemporally rare records, with 83% of species showing positive sensitivity, and long-distance migrants being most affected [55]. Intriguingly, **solar activity** (sunspot number) showed the opposite pattern — reducing vagrancy — possibly because solar radio-frequency noise disables the magnetoreceptor entirely, causing birds to pause migration or switch to other cues [55]. Long-term **secular variation** of the geomagnetic field (e.g., ~34° clockwise drift in declination over eastern Arctic Canada from 1900–2023) also matters: evolutionary modeling of northern wheatear migration (Wynn et al. 2023, *Movement Ecology*) shows that inherited magnetic headings and "signpost" thresholds can remain viable across a century of field drift, but only with sufficient inherited variability in orientation programs [56].

#### 5.1.3 Magnetic Anomalies

Local crustal magnetic anomalies (e.g., the Vogelsberg anomaly in Germany) can disrupt navigational performance. Wiltschko et al. (2010, *Current Biology*) showed that homing pigeons released inside the Vogelsberg anomaly showed unusually long vanishing intervals and scattered bearings — evidence of magnetic "map" confusion — and that anesthetizing the beak (which houses the magnetite map receptors) suppressed this adverse effect, confirming that the anomaly acts on the magnetic map sense [17].

### 5.2 Anthropogenic Disturbances

#### 5.2.1 Artificial Light at Night (ALAN)

Light pollution is now recognized as a major disruptor of nocturnal migration. The landmark study by Van Doren, Horton, Dokter, Farnsworth et al. (2017, *PNAS*) quantified the effect of the "Tribute in Light" memorial installation in New York City — 88 upward-pointing searchlights over the 9/11 Memorial — using weather radar, acoustic sensors, and visual observation across seven years. The results were dramatic:

- The installation influenced an estimated **1.1 million birds** over seven nights [52].
- Bird densities near the beams were **up to 20 times baseline** (over 150 times baseline in three years), with effects up to **4 km altitude** [52].
- Birds aggregated, **slowed their flight by ~1.7 m/s**, and **vocalized 1.4 times more frequently** — clear behavioral disorientation [52].
- Critically, these effects occurred **under clear skies** in an already heavily photo-polluted city, overturning the assumption that light attraction only matters in poor visibility [52].
- When the lights were turned off (22 times for ~20 minutes during the study), the behavioral disruption **disappeared within minutes** — demonstrating that targeted light management is a viable conservation intervention [52].

At the continental scale, Horton et al. (2019, *Frontiers in Ecology and the Environment*) combined 20 years of weather-radar data from 143 stations with satellite measurements of light radiance and found that ~80% of North American migratory bird species migrate at night; the 125 largest urban areas occupy only 2.1% of land area but produce 35.4% of light radiance, and mean avian light exposure in cities is **24 times the countrywide average**, with Chicago, Houston, and Dallas the highest-risk cities [52]. Because migration occurs in concentrated pulses (half of seasonal migratory activity passes a given site within ~7 nights), timed "lights-out" campaigns can substantially reduce exposure.

**Seabirds** are especially vulnerable: burrow-nesting petrels and shearwaters are attracted to artificial lights during fledging flights, leading to "fallout" and grounding. More than 56 species of burrow-nesting petrels are affected, with ~60% of affected species declining and 28 globally threatened (Rodríguez et al. 2023) [52]. Key findings include: shielding upward radiation reduces attraction by ~40% (Reed, Sincock & Hailman 1985); grounding is strongly moon-phase dependent (more during new moon, fewer during full moon; Rodríguez & Rodríguez 2009; Medina-Franco et al. 2025); offshore oil platforms and fishing vessels attract and kill seabirds via deck lighting (Wiese et al. 2001; Merkel & Johansen 2011); GPS-tracked Cory's Shearwater fledglings in the Canary Islands showed 13.4% grounding rates, with grounding probability increasing with light pollution and flight tortuosity [52]. Even partial reductions in ALAN decrease strandings by up to 57% [52].

#### 5.2.2 Anthropogenic Electromagnetic Noise (EMN)

The 2014 *Nature* paper by Engels et al. demonstrated that **anthropogenic electromagnetic noise disrupts magnetic compass orientation in migratory European Robins**. Birds tested in unscreened wooden huts on the University of Oldenburg campus could not orient magnetically, but regained full orientation when the huts were shielded with grounded aluminum screens attenuating EMN in the 50 kHz–5 MHz range. When the grounding was removed, or broadband EMN (2 kHz–9 MHz) at urban-like intensities was deliberately generated, the birds again became disoriented. At a quiet rural site ~7.5 km away, robins oriented normally without screening [57]. The effective noise levels were remarkably weak — peak intensities of ~0.1–50 nT at single frequencies and ~200–1,100 nT total over 10 kHz–5 MHz, compared to Earth's ~49,000 nT — and well below WHO/ICNIRP guidelines [57]. This was the first fully double-blinded demonstration of a behavioral effect of weak anthropogenic EMN on an intact vertebrate, and it implicates the radical-pair (cryptochrome) magnetoreception mechanism, consistent with the Ritz et al. (2004) resonance experiments showing that weak radio-frequency fields at specific frequencies and angles disorient birds [11][57]. The practical implication is that urban electromagnetic noise may degrade the magnetic compass precisely when birds need it most (e.g., overcast nights), though the effect appears localized to urban areas [57].

#### 5.2.3 Habitat Fragmentation and Climate Change

Habitat fragmentation disrupts migration at the ecological scale: stopover sites that provide food and shelter for refueling are being lost, and fragmented landscapes can force migrants to fly longer legs or make risky detours, indirectly increasing navigational demands and mortality [22]. Climate change is shifting the phenology of both migration timing and food availability: long-distance migrants such as the Pied Flycatcher (*Ficedula hypoleuca*) are arriving at breeding grounds after the peak of caterpillar abundance, reducing fitness (Both & Visser 2001, *Nature*) [22]. For navigational systems specifically, climate change can alter the stability of the cues themselves — for example, changing wind patterns alter drift and compensation demands, changing VOC emission patterns from vegetation may degrade olfactory maps, and geomagnetic secular variation shifts the very gradients that magnetic maps depend on [36][56]. The Wynn et al. (2023) model shows that inherited magnetic orientation programs can adapt to secular variation only through the standing genetic variability in heading precision — variability that may be eroded by population declines [56]. Changing day length (photoperiod) is the primary zeitgeber for the endogenous circannual programs that time migration, but it does not directly disrupt navigation; rather, climate-change-induced shifts in photoperiodic regimes can misalign the endogenous time program with actual resource availability [22].

## 6. Evidence for Long-Distance Navigational Accuracy

The accuracy of bird navigation over long distances is remarkable at two levels: the inherited clock-and-compass system of first-time migrants, and the learned true-navigation system of experienced adults.

**First-time migrants.** Ringing recoveries of Scandinavian Pied Flycatchers show that a single migratory step (~125 km) has an angular SD of ~52°, yet because the direction is averaged over hundreds of steps, the birds reliably reach their wintering areas [2]. The clock-and-compass system also includes "emergency plans" — e.g., young birds over water at dawn in autumn turn ~90° toward the sunrise until they reach land, as documented by radar observations of thrushes over the North Sea [2]. Cross-breeding experiments with Blackcaps (*Sylvia atricapilla*) from populations with different migratory directions show that the inherited directional preference is genetically controlled, and genomic studies of Swainson's Thrushes (*Catharus ustulatus*) have identified chromosomal regions associated with migratory direction at a migratory divide [2].

**Experienced migrants.** Displacement experiments document true navigation over thousands of kilometers: Manx Shearwaters displaced 5,150 km from their colony returned; Laysan Albatrosses displaced 6,630 km returned; White-crowned Sparrows displaced 3,860 km corrected their course [3]. The reed warbler virtual-displacement experiments (section 3.2) show that the underlying map has remarkable extrapolative power — birds responded to a magnetic field matching a location 2,700 km beyond their species' range with the correct homeward reorientation [50]. The authors suggest that some birds may possess a "global GPS" based on the geomagnetic field, though the sensory substrate (trigeminal magnetite receptors, radical-pair receptors, or both) and the exact field parameters used remain under active investigation [48][50].

**Free-flying songbird calibration.** The Cochran et al. (2004) twilight-recalibration experiment remains the only study to manipulate the magnetic compass of free-flying migrants and track the consequences over >1,000 km: the 90°-eastward twilight field shift caused birds to fly west for an entire night, then resume normal northward migration on subsequent nights — evidence both for the daily recalibration mechanism and for the extraordinary long-distance precision of the normal system [46].

## 7. Consensus, Controversies, and Open Questions

The scientific consensus can be summarized as follows:

1. **Multiple redundant compasses exist** — magnetic (innate, radical-pair based in the eye), solar (learned, time-compensated), stellar (learned, rotation-based), and polarized-light (calibration reference) — and they are aligned through a hierarchical calibration process centered on twilight cues [1][30][46].
2. **The magnetic field provides both compass and map information** through two distinct sensory channels: a light-dependent radical-pair compass processed in the visual system, and a trigeminal-nerve-mediated intensity/map system based on iron minerals [6][17][20].
3. **Positional (map) information is multifactorial and experience-dependent**, with the best evidence supporting magnetic gradient maps (for migrants) and olfactory gradient maps (for homing pigeons), and plausible but unproven roles for infrasound [3][32][39][43].
4. **Ontogeny matters**: juveniles use inherited vector programs; adults add learned maps that enable true navigation and compensation for displacement [2][3][48].

The major **controversies** are:

- **The receptor mechanism for the magnetic map**: iron-containing cells in the beak were claimed as magnetoreceptors, then reinterpreted as macrophages; yet the behavioral evidence for trigeminal mediation of magnetic map information is strong. The discrepancy between the well-documented neural transmission and the failure to identify the receptor structure is unresolved [6][17][20][60].
- **The role of olfaction**: Wallraff's olfactory map is rejected by some researchers as confounded, while Gagliardo's GPS-tracking data and the VOC identification study provide increasingly strong support; the debate is characterized by calls for standardized collaborative experiments [31][32][43].
- **The infrasound map**: Hagstrum's model explains several otherwise-anomalous datasets, but cochlea-removal experiments and the lack of direct tests in migrants keep it unproven [40][42][43].
- **The quantum mechanism**: the radical-pair hypothesis is supported by resonance, wavelength, and EM-noise experiments, but the replication record of monochromatic Larmor-frequency effects is mixed (disorientation in Frankfurt and at the Courish Spit, but not in Oldenburg under the most rigorous protocol), and the extraordinary sensitivity implied by some experiments (disruption at ~1 nT) is not fully explained by theory [4][12].
- **Calibration direction**: whether celestial cues calibrate the magnetic compass (premigratory) or the magnetic compass calibrates celestial cues (during migration) — or whether horizon access rather than season determines the hierarchy — remains debated [30][45].

**Open questions** for future research include: the identity and transduction mechanism of the cryptochrome-based receptor; the molecular basis of the trigeminal magnetic map; whether magnetic declination is a general or species-specific map component; the relative contributions of magnetic, olfactory, and infrasonic maps in wild migrants; the population-level consequences of ALAN and EMN for migratory survival; and how rapidly navigational programs can evolve in response to geomagnetic secular variation and climate change [4][6][48][56].
