# Light-Based Therapies in Aesthetic Medicine: Lasers, IPL, and LED for Photoaging, Skin Brightening, and Hyperpigmentation

## 1. Introduction and Scope

Light-based therapies constitute a cornerstone of modern aesthetic dermatology, offering targeted interventions for photoaging, pigmentary disorders, and skin brightening. Three principal technology families dominate this landscape: **lasers** (monochromatic, coherent, collimated light), **intense pulsed light (IPL)** (broadband, polychromatic, non-coherent light), and **light-emitting diodes (LED)** (low-intensity photobiomodulation). While all three interact with cutaneous chromophores, they differ fundamentally in wavelength specificity, pulse architecture, energy delivery, and mechanisms of action—ranging from selective photothermolysis and subcellular photothermolysis to photochemical mitochondrial stimulation.

This report synthesizes peer-reviewed evidence through August 2026, covering current clinical applications, recent scientific advancements, efficacy/safety profiles, and the specific challenges of melasma and recalcitrant hyperpigmentation for each modality.

## 2. Biophysical Principles and Target Chromophores

The therapeutic foundation of laser and IPL therapy rests on **selective photothermolysis** (Anderson & Parrish, 1983): a wavelength preferentially absorbed by a target chromophore, a pulse duration shorter than the target's thermal relaxation time, and sufficient fluence to destroy the target [1]. Three primary chromophores govern aesthetic applications:

- **Water** — targets: 10,600 nm (CO₂), 2,940 nm (Er:YAG), 1,927 nm (thulium), 1,540–1,550 nm (erbium:glass). Water absorption drives collagen denaturation (~66.8°C), wound-healing cascades, and neocollagenesis [2][3].
- **Melanin** — targets: 532 nm (frequency-doubled Nd:YAG), 694 nm (ruby), 730 nm, 755 nm (alexandrite), 785 nm, 1,064 nm (Nd:YAG). Absorption decreases with longer wavelengths, but deeper penetration and reduced hemoglobin competition favor near-infrared melanin targeting in darker skin [4][5].
- **Hemoglobin/oxyhemoglobin** — targets: 532 nm (KTP), 585/595 nm (pulsed dye laser), and portions of the IPL spectrum; relevant to the vascular component of melasma and erythematous photoaging [6][7].

**Q-switched (QS) lasers** (nanosecond pulses) and **picosecond lasers** (10⁻¹² s pulses) exploit photomechanical/photoacoustic effects—acoustic shock waves that shatter melanosomes with minimal thermal diffusion. Picosecond devices additionally generate laser-induced optical breakdown (LIOB) and intradermal cavitation when used with fractional optics [4][8].

**Subcellular selective photothermolysis** (Mun et al., 2011) underpins low-fluence QS Nd:YAG "laser toning": selective destruction of mature stage IV melanosomes while melanocytes remain intact and functionally downregulated [9][10].

**LED photobiomodulation (PBM)** operates on entirely different principles: photons (typically red 620–700 nm and near-infrared 700–1,440 nm) are absorbed by mitochondrial cytochrome c oxidase, increasing ATP production, modulating reactive oxygen species and nitric oxide, and activating transcription factors that upregulate collagen synthesis (types I and III), elastin, and TGF-β1 while downregulating matrix metalloproteinases [11][12]. The response follows a biphasic Arndt-Schulz dose-response curve: low doses stimulate, excessive doses inhibit [13].

## 3. Laser Therapies

### 3.1 Lasers for Photoaging (Wrinkles, Fine Lines, Elasticity, Dermal Remodeling)

#### 3.1.1 Ablative Fractional CO₂ Laser (10,600 nm)

Fractional CO₂ laser resurfacing remains the gold-standard invasive treatment for moderate-to-severe photoaging. The wavelength targets water, creating microscopic thermal zones (MTZs) 50–150 µm in diameter that trigger controlled dermal heating, normalization of the collagenesis/collagenolysis cycle, and re-epithelialization within 48–72 hours [2][3]. A systematic review reported average improvements of 48.5% in skin texture and 50.3% in laxity, with recovery reduced to 3–6 days versus 2–3 weeks for traditional ablative resurfacing [3]. Clinical efficacy across studies ranges from 40–60% wrinkle reduction (85% patient satisfaction), with PIH incidence of 2–5% and scarring below 0.5% [2].

A 2025 randomized split-face trial (Shanghai Ninth People's Hospital; ChiCTR2400087320) optimized CO₂ AFL parameters for static periorbital wrinkles in 30 Chinese patients. Low-energy/high-density treatment (10–12.5 mJ, 15% coverage) proved most efficacious, achieving a 68.2% efficacy rate with significant Fitzpatrick wrinkle score reductions at 3 and 6 months (p = 0.016 and p = 0.001). Notably, 1% hydroquinone cream applied nightly before and after treatment significantly reduced PIH incidence (p = 0.02/0.04) and was recommended as essential peri-laser care in darker skin types [14].

#### 3.1.2 Fractional Er:YAG (2,940 nm) and Erbium:Glass (1,540–1,550 nm)

The Er:YAG laser's 2,940 nm wavelength coincides with water's absorption peak, producing more superficial vaporization than CO₂ with less collateral thermal damage. Full-field treatment is generally limited to Fitzpatrick skin types I–II; types III–IV require conservative parameters, and types V–VI only fractionated beams with great care [15]. Visible rhytid improvement occurs 3–8 weeks post-treatment with increased dermal collagen at 6 weeks. A 2025 systematic review and meta-analysis (6 studies, 497 patients) found Er:YAG produced the highest rate of "excellent" outcomes (20%) among resurfacing modalities, with Er:YAG + radiofrequency yielding the best overall skin quality restoration [16].

Non-ablative fractional lasers (NAFLs) at 1,540–1,550 nm create MTZs up to 1,000 µm deep while preserving the stratum corneum, enabling same-day recovery. A 2022 study of a high-power 1,540 nm erbium:glass laser (15 patients, five monthly sessions) demonstrated a 49.5% reduction in skin roughness volume (4.7 ± 0.9 to 2.6 ± 0.6 mm³; p < 0.001) with only 24–48 hours of downtime [17]. A 2025 study of 1,550 nm NAFL for neck rejuvenation (19 patients, three sessions) showed significant improvement in the Wrinkle Severity Rating Scale (p < 0.001), increased dermal thickness from 1.15 ± 0.20 mm to 1.30 ± 0.23 mm (p < 0.001), and no serious adverse events [18].

A cautionary histological finding: Borges et al. (2020) demonstrated that while both 1,540 nm and 2,940 nm fractional lasers induce reorganization of collagen types I and III and fibroblast activation, morphometric analysis showed significantly *lower* collagen fiber density at 3 months post-treatment (attributed to edema), suggesting the early "skin-lifting" effect reflects neocollagenesis plus edema [19].

#### 3.1.3 Fractional Thulium Laser (1,927 nm)

The 1,927 nm thulium fiber laser occupies a unique niche: its water absorption coefficient is ~10× that of 1,550 nm erbium:glass but far lower than ablative lasers, concentrating energy 200–300 µm deep in the epidermis and superficial dermis. It forms microscopic epidermal necrotic debris (MENDs) containing melanin and denatured keratin that shed within 7 days, producing synchronized pigment removal, epidermal reconstruction, and collagen neogenesis [20]. A 2025 systematic review confirmed improvements in skin texture, pigmentation reduction, and scar appearance with an acceptable safety profile [21].

Clinical data demonstrate significant improvements in melanin index, elasticity, and wrinkles in Asian skin [22]. A 2025 split-face trial combining 1,927 nm thulium with supramolecular salicylic acid (SSA) in 36 patients showed superior photoaging score reduction (10.51% combined vs. 7.26% laser-only; p < 0.001) with enhanced dermal thickness, hydration, and elasticity [23]. A position paper by eight experienced US dermatologists reviewing ~50,000 procedures with the dual-wavelength 1,550/1,927 nm platform confirmed safety across all skin types, with rare, mild, transient adverse effects; four treatments at 6-week intervals increased dermal thickness by ~17% [24].

#### 3.1.4 Picosecond Lasers for Photoaging

Picosecond lasers (755 nm, 730 nm, 1,064 nm, 532 nm) deliver ultra-short pulses that fragment pigment via photoacoustic mechanisms with minimal thermal damage. Fractional picosecond handpieces create intradermal laser-induced cavitation and LIOB, stimulating collagen remodeling [4][8]. A split-face study comparing fractionated 1,064/532-nm picosecond Nd:YAG with 1,927-nm thulium found both equally effective for facial rejuvenation (significant improvements in elastosis, erythema, dyschromia, and texture; p < 0.01), though the picosecond side had significantly less crusting, peeling, and itching [25]. A July 2026 randomized split-face study showed that adjunctive skincare enhanced picosecond-laser wrinkle and texture gains [26]. Repeated picosecond treatments produce cumulative improvements in collagen density and elasticity, and are particularly suitable for darker skin types [27].

### 3.2 Lasers for Skin Whitening/Brightening

Skin brightening with lasers primarily exploits melanin-specific photothermolysis and the MEND-driven pigment clearance of fractional devices. The 1,927 nm thulium laser significantly improves periorbital brightness (L* value) after three treatments, with 4-week intervals optimal for wrinkles; its superficial 200–300 µm penetration preserves the stratum corneum, making it safe for Fitzpatrick types III–VI [20]. QS Nd:YAG "laser toning" at low fluence (1.2–2.0 J/cm², 8 mm spot) has been called "the new gold standard of melasma treatment in Asia" and produces generalized skin lightening alongside melasma clearance [9][28]. Interestingly, red light (633 nm) LED has been shown to significantly decrease melanin levels (by 15.5, p < 0.005), suggesting photobiomodulatory skin-brightening effects independent of photothermolysis [29].

### 3.3 Lasers for Hyperpigmentation and Melasma

#### 3.3.1 Solar Lentigines: Q-Switched and Picosecond Lasers

A prospective, randomized, split-face trial (2020) compared 532-nm picosecond vs. 532-nm QS Nd:YAG for solar lentigines in 20 Korean women (Fitzpatrick III–V). The picosecond laser achieved significantly higher clinical clearance (mean quartile improvement score 2.95 vs. 1.8; p < 0.05) with a trend toward lower PIH (5% vs. 30%; p = 0.057) [30]. A 2024 ASLMS presentation (Manuskiatti et al.) demonstrated that fractional-beam microlens array (MLA) delivery reduced PIH incidence by ~81% compared with full-beam 532-nm picosecond treatment in Fitzpatrick III–V patients: at 6 months, PIH was 16% (fractional) vs. 88% (full-beam), with no significant difference in lesional clearance [31].

A 2025 randomized clinical trial comparing 730-nm, 532-nm, and 694-nm lasers for freckles and solar lentigines found the 730-nm picosecond laser produced the least histological damage, making it a potentially preferable option for pigmentary conditions; no significant differences emerged in global aesthetic improvement or satisfaction [32]. A 2026 case series using a novel 532-nm nanosecond "vasculature salvage laser surgery" system with contact cooling and automatic lesion recognition achieved complete resolution of refractory solar lentigines after a single session with no PIH at 3 months [33]. A systematic review of 41 solar lentigines trials (2025) reported IPL efficacy of 74.6–90%, Q-switched lasers 36.4–76.6%, and picosecond lasers 67.9–93.0%, with PDL and IPL least associated with PIH [34].

The Q-switched ruby laser (694 nm), historically the first pigment-selective laser, remains effective for solar lentigines and labial lentigines (dramatic clearing after 1–2 treatments at ~10 J/cm²) in Fitzpatrick I–II, with transient hypopigmentation the most common adverse effect [35]. QS alexandrite (755 nm) shows high efficacy for café-au-lait macules with low recurrence [36].

#### 3.3.2 Melasma: Q-Switched Nd:YAG Laser Toning

Low-fluence QS Nd:YAG (LFQSNY) "laser toning" is the most extensively studied laser approach for melasma. A 2022 PRISMA systematic review (42 studies; 1,736 patients) characterized the parameters: 1,064 nm, 6–10 mm spot, 0.5–3.8 J/cm², 1–10 passes, sessions at 1–2 week intervals, endpoint of faint erythema. Mean mMASI reductions of ~54% are typical (e.g., 3.19 ± 2.64 to 1.46 ± 1.06 in 40 Korean patients) [9][10]. However, **mottled hypopigmentation (MH)** occurs in ~10–11.9% of East Asian patients, is associated with excessive cumulative laser energy, and can persist for years (spontaneous resolution in <10% after 2 years). Recurrence rates at 3 months reach 64–100% in some studies [10]. A retrospective study of aggressive "frequent laser toning" (3×/week for 2 months) found that all 23 patients developed confetti-like hypopigmentation that failed to repigment after 4 months of rest, leading to the recommendation that toning intervals shorter than 2 weeks be avoided [37]. Ultrastructural comparison confirmed that QS 1,064 nm toning produces subcellular-selective melanosome destruction with minimal epidermal disruption and normal fibroblasts, whereas QS ruby causes necrotic cellular damage and earlier recurrence [38]. For recalcitrant melasma, expert consensus recommends reserving lasers as a last-line option, with larger spot sizes (8–10 mm), longer intervals, and conservative cumulative energy [39].

#### 3.3.3 Picosecond Lasers for Melasma

The picosecond evidence base for melasma is mixed. A randomized split-face study (2024) comparing 755-nm picosecond alexandrite with DLA vs. 1,064-nm QSNYL (three sessions) found short-term efficacy only for QSNYL (mMASI significantly lower at 6 months, p = 0.022), no significant between-laser difference, and 58.8% recurrence at 2 years; reflectance confocal microscopy showed dendritic melanocytes infiltrating the dermis after picosecond treatment [40]. Conversely, a 2023 randomized controlled trial (60 patients, Fitzpatrick III–IV) found non-fractional 1,064-nm picosecond Nd:YAG (PSNYL) superior to 755-nm picosecond alexandrite and 2% hydroquinone (improvement rates 35.9% vs. 25.5% vs. 24.0%) with no hypopigmentation [41]. A 2025 randomized trial comparing combined QS + long-pulse Nd:YAG (QLNYL) vs. 755-nm picosecond alexandrite found QLNYL significantly superior in MASI improvement (p = 0.010), with lower pain scores (p = 0.018), lower PIH, and 0% vs. 10.5% recurrence at 24 weeks, concluding "there is no need to completely replace traditional devices" [42]. Overall, picosecond lasers appear comparable or modestly superior to QS devices for melasma, with better pain profiles, but high-quality comparative data remain limited [43].

#### 3.3.4 Vascular Lasers for Melasma

The vascular component of melasma is now well-established: aberrant angiogenesis, VEGF upregulation, and telangiectatic erythema are histologically and dermoscopically evident in melasma lesions [6][7]. A randomized split-face study (Kong et al., 2018) combined pulsed dye laser (595 nm) with QSNY toning in 17 Korean women. While overall MASI improvement was similar between sides, the **seven patients with visibly widened capillaries on dermoscopy showed significantly greater improvement on the PDL-combined side (p = 0.026)** with a tendency toward relapse prevention [6]. A systematic review of PDL for vascular melasma confirmed that PDL + low-fluence QSNY is safe and effective specifically in patients with demonstrable vascularity, and that antivascular therapy (including tranexamic acid) improves outcomes [44]. Expert guidance now recommends dermoscopic or colorimetric assessment for vascularity and targeted antivascular therapy in affected patients [7].

#### 3.3.5 Laser-Assisted Drug Delivery (LADD) for Melasma

LADD uses fractional lasers (CO₂, Er:YAG, thulium, or picosecond) to create microchannels that enhance topical drug penetration. A systematic review (2023) of 12 prospective controlled studies found LADD with tranexamic acid (TXA), kojic acid, or hydroquinone effective for melasma up to 6 months, with outcomes superior to monotherapy up to 3 months and no increased complication risk [45]. Key studies:

- **1,927-nm thulium + topical TXA** (Wang et al., 2021): MASI improved by 1.1 points at 30 days, 3.5 at 90 days, and 2.5 at 180 days in 7 patients; investigator-rated improvement in 6/7 subjects [46].
- **Fractional thulium + TXA vs. saline** (Wanitphakdeedecha et al., 2020): significant melanin index improvements maintained longer on the TXA side (through 3 months); authors suggested repeat regimens every 3–4 months for recalcitrant melasma [47].
- **Fractional CO₂ + 10% TXA vs. 20% ascorbic acid** (Eassa et al., 2025; 40 patients): TXA side showed significantly greater reductions in melanin (p = 0.001), vascularity (p = 0.01), and hemi-MASI (p = 0.009); no PIH observed despite Fitzpatrick III–V skin [48].
- **Fractional CO₂ or microneedling + TXA vs. TXA cream alone** (Kamal et al., 2023): combination groups significantly outperformed monotherapy, with fractional CO₂ requiring meticulous patient selection due to PIH risk in types III–IV [49].
- A 2025 randomized trial (160 patients) confirmed laser + topical TXA was superior to laser alone for melasma area, depth, long-term control, and side-effect profile [50].

## 4. Intense Pulsed Light (IPL) Therapies

### 4.1 Device Characteristics and Mechanisms

IPL uses a xenon flashlamp to emit polychromatic, non-coherent light across 400–1,400 nm, with cut-off filters (515–755 nm) shaping the emission spectrum. This versatility allows simultaneous targeting of melanin, hemoglobin, and water, with larger spot sizes enabling rapid treatment of broad areas [51][52]. The clinical effect relies on selective photothermolysis: for pigmented lesions, melanin is heated and eliminated as microcrusts over 7 days; for rhytids, dermal heating stimulates fibroblast activity and neocollagenesis; for vascular targets, oxyhemoglobin absorption (peaks at 418, 542, 577 nm) drives vessel coagulation [51][53]. Effects are cumulative, typically requiring 3–6 sessions at 2–4 week intervals [54].

### 4.2 IPL for Photoaging

A split-face RCT in 24 Chinese women (Li et al., 2010) demonstrated significant improvement in global photoaging scores (3.02 to 1.22) with four IPL sessions, with 87.5% rating improvement as excellent or good; histology confirmed significantly increased collagen fibers on treated sides [55]. A 2026 retrospective study of 236 patients treated with modern optimized pulse technology (M22, Lumenis) found significant improvements in erythema area (640 to 372.4 mm²), pigmentation (577.5 to 370.6 mm²), and wrinkle index (all p < 0.001); regular maintenance treatment was the strongest predictor of good response (OR = 13.62), while Fitzpatrick type IV predicted poorer outcomes (OR = 0.12) [56].

The proprietary **BroadBand Light (BBL)** variant demonstrated remarkable molecular evidence of rejuvenation: a Stanford pilot study found that three BBL treatments reversed the expression of 1,293 of 2,265 aging-associated genes toward youthful patterns (827 of these are NF-κB-bound; p = 1.2 × 10⁻⁷⁵), with clinical improvements in fine wrinkling (p = 0.03) and pigmentation (p = 0.02) [57]. A long-term retrospective study found that while treated skin aged a median of 9 years over 5–11 years of annual BBL, blinded evaluators estimated participants appeared to have aged only 2 years—described as the longest blinded evaluation of IPL-type treatment effects [58].

A 2025 innovation used a 755-nm IPL filter (755–1,200 nm) to target the reticular dermis, producing immediate facial tightening and significant VISIA wrinkle reduction in 61 patients, with 63.9% reporting minimal pain (VAS 0–1) and only one case of PIH [59]. However, a histological study (El-Domyati et al., 2011) found no statistically significant changes in extracellular matrix proteins after six IPL sessions, underscoring that IPL's dermal remodeling is modest compared with fractional resurfacing [60]. Combining IPL with ablative fractional CO₂ significantly improved elasticity (p = 0.004), pore size (p = 0.039), and wrinkles (p = 0.015) vs. IPL alone in a split-face Chinese study [61].

### 4.3 IPL for Skin Whitening/Brightening

A split-face comparison of non-ablative 1,064-nm QS laser vs. IPL in 12 Asian patients found IPL produced more significant skin-tone improvement and lightening after only three treatments, although the laser continued improving through sessions four and five while IPL plateaued [62]. IPL is widely used in Asia for generalized skin brightening, targeting epidermal melanin and improving complexion evenness; in the 2026 long-term study, pigmentation area decreased by ~36% with regular IPL [56].

### 4.4 IPL for Hyperpigmentation and Melasma

#### 4.4.1 Solar Lentigines

A 2015 objective study of intensive targeted IPL (500–635 nm) in 40 Japanese patients (Fitzpatrick III–V) demonstrated significant clearing after a single session: mean gray-level values increased from 143.7 ± 8.6 to 162.9 ± 9.8 (p < 0.0001), with 40% of subjects achieving >20-point improvement and 90% satisfaction; no PIH or recurrence was observed [63]. The 2025 systematic review confirmed IPL efficacy of 74.6–90% for solar lentigines, with a favorable PIH profile compared with QS lasers and cryotherapy [34].

#### 4.4.2 Melasma

IPL is regarded as moderately effective for melasma, with epidermal and mixed types responding better than purely dermal disease [64]. Across key studies:

- 43% mean MASI improvement after 2–3 sessions; 70% after four OPT-IPL sessions [64]
- IPL + Kligman's formula: 49.4% MASI decrease vs. 2.4% for Kligman alone at 6 months; 93.8% reduction in another study [64][65]
- IPL + hydroquinone: 40% melanin index reduction vs. 12% with hydroquinone alone [64]
- A network meta-analysis of 59 RCTs (2,812 participants) ranked IPL second overall among 14 melasma treatments (SUCRA 79.6%), behind QS Nd:YAG (85.0%) and ahead of ablative fractional laser (78.6%) and triple combination cream (75.8%) [66]

A landmark combination protocol (Na et al., 2012) used one IPL session followed by four weekly low-fluence QS Nd:YAG sessions in 20 Korean women with mixed-type melasma, achieving a 59.35% MASI decrease (8.54 to 3.52) with significant reductions in melanin and erythema indices (both p < 0.001), no PIH, and 60% of patients requiring no further treatment [67]. A 2026 meta-analysis of 31 controlled trials (2,801 patients) confirmed that QS laser + IPL combination is superior to monotherapy: MASI SMD = −1.20 (95% CI −1.36 to −1.05), clinical efficacy RR = 1.10, and recurrence RR = 0.54 (8.5% vs. 17.8%), though patient satisfaction showed no significant difference—highlighting the gap between objective and patient-reported outcomes [68].

Two 2026 studies evaluated IPL + TXA combinations. A randomized placebo-controlled trial (36 Chinese patients, Fitzpatrick III–IV) found IPL + 3% TXA cataplasm achieved significantly greater mMASI reduction than IPL alone (−50.52% vs. −38.49%; p < 0.05), with a 36.31% melanin index decrease vs. −1.28% (p = 0.02), indicating TXA prevented the early PIH rebound seen with IPL monotherapy [69]. A retrospective study (29 patients) of IPL + TXA microneedling showed higher satisfaction (86.7% vs. 71.4%) and lower recurrence (6.7% vs. 21.5%) than IPL alone [70]. IPL also outperformed 50% glycolic acid peels in a 2026 RCT (56.5% vs. 28.2% achieving ≥50% mMASI reduction; p < 0.05) [71].

#### 4.4.3 Recent IPL Advancements

- **Electro-Optical Synergy (ELOS)**: combines IPL with bipolar radiofrequency, allowing optical fluence reduction by up to 60% while enhancing efficacy; clinical studies show significant improvement in wrinkles (24–33%) and pigmentation (38–62%) [72][73].
- **Optimized pulse technology (OPT)**: square-pulse delivery with stable energy improves safety and efficacy [52].
- **IPL + TXA combinations** (topical or microneedling) represent the most significant recent advance for melasma, reducing recurrence and preventing the early pigmentary rebound [69][70].
- **755-nm filter IPL** for dermal remodeling and tightening [59].

## 5. LED Photobiomodulation Therapies

### 5.1 Mechanisms and Wavelength Penetration

LED therapy (photobiomodulation, formerly low-level laser therapy) uses non-thermal, low-power light (typically 5 mW/cm² to 5 W/cm²; fluences 4–18 J/cm²) absorbed primarily by mitochondrial cytochrome c oxidase. This triggers increased ATP, nitric oxide release, ROS modulation, and downstream transcription factor activation, promoting collagen I/III synthesis, elastin production, anti-inflammatory cytokine shifts, and reduced MMP activity [11][12][74]. The biphasic dose-response (Arndt-Schulz law) means lower doses are often more effective than higher ones [13].

Wavelength-specific penetration and effects:

- **Blue light (400–470 nm)**: penetrates ≤1 mm; antibacterial, sebum-reducing; **upregulates melanogenesis**—not indicated for melasma [75][76]
- **Green light (490–570 nm)**: targets melanocytes; suppresses MITF and tyrosinase expression [77]
- **Yellow/amber (570–595 nm)**: penetrates 0.5–2 mm; reduces NF-κB phosphorylation and COX-2; influences melanogenesis [76][78]
- **Red light (620–700 nm)**: deepest visible penetration (~4–5 mm); stimulates fibroblast collagen production; reduces MMPs [76][79]
- **Near-infrared (700–1,440 nm)**: deepest penetration (up to 40 mm at 850 nm); anti-inflammatory, pro-circulatory [76][79]

### 5.2 LED for Photoaging

The landmark Lee et al. (2007) prospective, randomized, placebo-controlled, double-blinded split-face trial (76 completers, Fitzpatrick III–IV) remains foundational: 830 nm and/or 633 nm LED twice weekly for 4 weeks produced significant wrinkle reduction (max 36%) and increased elasticity (max 19%), with histological evidence of increased collagen and elastic fibers, ultrastructurally activated fibroblasts, increased TIMP-1/2, and no adverse effects [80].

More recent high-quality studies include:

- **Couturaud & Le Fur (2023)**: 20 volunteers, 630 ± 10 nm LED mask (15.6 J/cm², 21.7 mW/cm²) twice weekly for 3 months achieved progressive improvements—crow's feet depth −38.3%, facial sagging −24.8%, skin firmness +23.6%, dermal density +47.7%, roughness −23.8%—with effects persisting 1 month after cessation. Red-only light was selected to minimize pigmentation risk in phototypes V–VI [81].
- **Park et al. (2025)**: multi-center, randomized, double-blind, sham-controlled trial of a home-use 630 nm LED + 850 nm IRED mask (60 Asian participants, Fitzpatrick II–V; 9 min, 5×/week, 12 weeks). Independent-rater improvement rates were 86.2–89.3% in the active group vs. 16.7% in sham (p = 0.000), with significant crow's feet score differences at 8, 12, and 16 weeks; adverse events occurred in only 6.67% [82].
- **Bragato et al. (2025)**: randomized sham-controlled trial (95 women, 45–60 years; 660 ± 10 nm, 8.05 J/cm²) found two weekly sessions for 4 weeks sufficient to improve FACE-Q satisfaction (73.4–79.6% vs. control, p < 0.05), with significant ImageJ-measured reductions in glabellar and periorbital wrinkle length [83].
- **Wunsch & Matuschka (2014)**: 136 volunteers, 30 sessions over 15 weeks of red/NIR light significantly improved complexion, roughness, and collagen density vs. controls [84].
- **Khoury & Goldman (2009)**: 660 nm LED produced a 31% mean increase in type-1 procollagen and an 18% decrease in MMP-1 in human reconstructed skin; >90% of treated individuals showed reduced rhytid depth and roughness [85].

A 2022 systematic review and meta-analysis (31 studies) found LED highly effective for skin rejuvenation with remarkable consistency (SMD = −3.45; 95% CI −3.84 to −3.06; I² = 33%) [86]. The 2023 protocol for a Brazilian RCT notes that PBM is indicated for mild photoaging and fine wrinkles—not as a substitute for volume replacement or deep rhytid correction [87].

### 5.3 LED for Skin Whitening/Brightening

Approximately a quarter of participants in the Park et al. 2025 home-mask RCT spontaneously noted skin-whitening/brightening effects, though this was not objectively measured [82]. Objective data come from cellular and small clinical studies:

- Red light (633 nm) significantly decreased melanin levels (15.5 units; p < 0.005) in the Avci et al. review [29]
- **505 nm green LED** (Mima et al., 2025) reduced melanin production in B16 melanoma cells and 3D skin models by suppressing MITF and tyrosinase gene expression, with human facial intervention confirming decreased pigmentation [77]
- Green light (500–570 nm) is reported to reduce pigmentation by up to 80% in some patient series, acting on melanocytes in the upper epidermis [88]
- LED photoprevention (660 nm) provided SPF-15-like protection against UV-B erythema in 85% of subjects, including protection against PIH [89]

### 5.4 LED for Hyperpigmentation and Melasma

A 2024 integrative review (Galache et al.) systematically examined PBM for melasma. Key findings: red (630 nm), amber (585/590 nm), and infrared (830/850 nm) wavelengths at 1–20 J/cm² modulate tyrosinase activity and downregulate MITF, TYR, TRP-1, and TRP-2 in vitro; amber light (585 nm) has a proposed triple action of melanogenesis downregulation, autophagy promotion, and VEGF reduction. **Critically, blue light (415–470 nm) upregulates melanogenesis and is contraindicated for melasma**—a caution for combination acne devices. Clinical trials identified included Barolet (2018), Mpofana (2018), and Dai (2022), all showing MASI reductions with PBM [75].

The Barolet 2018 pilot study combined weekly microdermabrasion with 940-nm pulsed PBM (13.5 J/cm², 90 mW/cm²) for 8 weeks in 7 women with refractory dermal melasma: MASI improved from 11.4 to 4.7 at week 12 (p < 0.001), with three of four patients maintaining improvement at 12 months. The proposed mechanism involves p53-mediated downregulation of melanin biosynthesis and "photoprevention"—preconditioning skin against future UV damage [90].

A 2025 randomized double-blind pilot (21 women) comparing 585-nm amber LED PBM vs. 5% liposomal TXA cream found no significant MASI differences within or between groups, but the study was underpowered (51%) and terminated early; both treatments improved quality of life [91]. A 2025 study of 570/590-nm yellow combined with red and infrared LED (30 patients, 8 weeks, 3×/week) found both wavelengths significantly reduced pores, wrinkles, texture, and brown spots, with the 570-nm composite more effective for wrinkles and brown spots (p < 0.05) [92].

The 2025 network meta-analysis of 15 melasma treatment modalities found intradermal PRP ± oral TXA most effective, with no LED/PBM modality ranked among the top options—reflecting the paucity of robust PBM-melasma RCTs rather than proven inefficacy [93]. Expert guidance recommends PBM as an adjunct to standard melasma therapy, not monotherapy [94].

### 5.5 Home-Use LED Devices

The home-use LED market has expanded rapidly, with FDA-cleared (510(k)) devices using red, NIR, amber, green, and blue wavelengths [95]. Evidence quality varies substantially: clinical PBM systems operate at 100+ mW/cm², while most at-home masks deliver 20–40 mW/cm²; a 2025 pilot study found wide heterogeneity in consumer device wavelength output, power stability, and irradiance, with beam divergence (~74°) substantially reducing real-world energy delivery [82]. The AAD notes that some studies show subtle-to-noticeable results for fine lines, wrinkles, dark spots, and rough skin, but advises that people with darker skin tones are more sensitive to visible light and may experience hyperpigmentation [96]. The 2025 JAAD consensus affirmed that red-light PBM does not induce DNA damage and endorsed PBM for multiple dermatologic indications [97].

## 6. Melasma: Refractory Challenges and Emerging Solutions

Melasma remains the most challenging pigmentary disorder treated with light-based therapies. Its chronic, relapsing nature, multifactorial pathogenesis (genetic, hormonal, UV/visible light, vascular, inflammatory), and predilection for Fitzpatrick III–VI skin make it uniquely refractory [98][99].

### 6.1 Core Challenges

- **High recurrence rates**: 64–100% at 3 months in some QSNY toning studies; 47.7% relapse within 3 months even in well-responders [10][100]
- **Heat exacerbation**: unlike stable lentigines, melasma melanocytes are hyperactive and may produce *more* pigment in response to thermal injury, causing rebound hyperpigmentation [101]
- **Mottled/confetti hypopigmentation**: from excessive QS laser toning; largely irreversible [37]
- **Dermal and vascular components**: melanophages, solar elastosis, basement membrane disruption, and angiogenesis contribute; "dermal melasma" is now understood as chronic damage rather than a distinct entity [7][98]
- **Blue-light/HEVL activation**: high-energy visible light (400–450 nm) activates opsin-3, driving persistent pigmentation independent of UV [75][99]
- **PIH risk**: QS lasers carry ~25% PIH risk in skin of color (up to 47% for lentigines); picosecond lasers ~4.65% [102]

### 6.2 Emerging Solutions

**Combination therapy is the dominant paradigm.** The 2021 network meta-analysis found 87% of combination-vs.-single studies favored combination approaches [66]. Evidence-based combination strategies include:

1. **Triple combination cream (hydroquinone 4% + tretinoin 0.05% + fluocinolone acetonide 0.01%)** remains first-line; 2025 EADV Delphi consensus no longer uses Wood's lamp to guide therapy, and a 2026 meta-analysis found TC cream outperforms picosecond laser monotherapy [99][103].
2. **Oral tranexamic acid** (250–500 mg twice daily, 8–12 weeks) reduces MASI by up to ~59–60%; a 2026 RCT found oral TXA + hydroquinone superior to fractional Er:YAG + HQ (60.5% vs. 38.5% improvement) and intradermal TXA (19.0%), though oral TXA carries GI and thromboembolic precautions [104][105].
3. **Dual-mode 1,064 nm lasers**: a 2025 Shanghai study combined low-fluence QS (1.4–3.0 J/cm²) with microsecond-pulse (250 µs, 10–15 J/cm²) delivery to address both melanin and vascular/photoaging components. Treatment-side relapse at 3 months was significantly lower (4.8% vs. 23.8%; p = 0.047), with maintained melanin index reduction at 12 months (p < 0.05) [106].
4. **Vascular-targeted therapy**: PDL (595 nm) in patients with dermoscopic telangiectasia; TXA (which decreases VEGF receptor expression) [6][7].
5. **Laser-assisted drug delivery** with TXA or vitamin C via fractional CO₂, Er:YAG, thulium, or picosecond lasers [45][48].
6. **Novel wavelengths**: 730-nm picosecond laser (high melanin selectivity with deep penetration) showed 88.0% clinician-rated efficacy for acquired dermal melanocytosis vs. 59.0% for 1,064 nm [107]; 785-nm QS picosecond + TXA cream achieved 75–95% melasma clearance at 3 months in 11 patients [108].
7. **Intradermal PRP ± oral TXA** ranked as the best treatments in the 2025 network meta-analysis, though injection-related discomfort limits use [93].
8. **Photobiomodulation** (red/amber/NIR) as a non-invasive adjunct that downregulates melanogenic enzymes without thermal injury [75][90].
9. **Photonic Risk Index (PRI)**: a proposed five-parameter instrument (phototype 25%, pigmentation depth 20%, wavelength 15%, fluence 20%, recurrence history 20%) to stratify melasma patients by treatment risk and guide conservative parameter selection [99].

## 7. Safety and Adverse Effect Profiles

### 7.1 Lasers

- **Fractional CO₂**: transient erythema (3–7 days), edema (1–3 days); PIH 2–5% (lower with hydroquinone pre-treatment and ethnic-skin protocols); hypopigmentation <1%; scarring <0.5% [2][14]
- **Er:YAG**: less PIH than CO₂; erythema may persist 3–4 months after full-field ablation; herpes reactivation, milia, transient dyspigmentation [15]
- **QS Nd:YAG toning**: mottled hypopigmentation (~10–11.9% in East Asians; largely irreversible), rebound hyperpigmentation in types IV–V, pain (VAS ~3) [9][10][37]
- **Picosecond lasers**: PIH ~4.65% overall (vs. 25% for QS in skin of color); transient erythema and crusting; rare hypopigmentation [4][30][102]
- **PDL**: transient purpura (with long-pulse settings), rare PIH, especially in type IV–V [6]

### 7.2 IPL

IPL's most common side effects are transient pain and erythema (2–48 hours). Other effects include edema, crusting, blistering, and post-inflammatory hyper- or hypopigmentation—particularly in tanned or darker skin. Paradoxical hypertrichosis may occur in hair-removal contexts. HSV reactivation can be prevented with prophylactic antivirals. PIH risk is generally lower than QS lasers for lentigines but significant in Fitzpatrick IV–VI if fluences are too high [54][64]. The 2026 QS+IPL meta-analysis found no significant increase in adverse reactions with combination therapy (RR = 0.92) [68].

### 7.3 LED/PBM

LED is among the safest dermatologic modalities: no UV emission, no DNA damage, and no documented carcinogenic risk [96][97]. Mild transient erythema or dryness may occur. **Blue light (400–500 nm) carries a "blue light hazard"** (photochemical retinal injury; skin cytotoxicity) and upregulates melanogenesis—caution is advised in melasma patients [75][76]. Darker skin types may be more susceptible to PBM-induced hyperpigmentation, warranting conservative parameters [97]. Contraindications include photosensitive dermatoses, photosensitizing medications, and active skin cancers [95][96].

## 8. Comparative Synthesis and Clinical Recommendations

The three modality families occupy distinct therapeutic niches:

| Indication | Laser (first-line evidence) | IPL | LED/PBM |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Photoaging (moderate-severe)** | Fractional CO₂, Er:YAG (60–80% improvement); 1,550 nm NAFL (mild-moderate) | Moderate improvement; best for dyschromia + mild rhytids; 3–6 sessions | Mild-moderate improvement; 8–12+ sessions; excellent safety; home-use feasible |
| **Photoaging (mild)** | 1,927 nm thulium; picosecond fractional | BBL/OPT IPL | Red/NIR LED |
| **Skin brightening** | QS Nd:YAG toning; 1,927 nm thulium | IPL (rapid early response) | Red/green LED (modest) |
| **Solar lentigines** | 532 nm picosecond > QS (lower PIH); 730 nm (least damage) | 74.6–90% efficacy; low PIH | Limited evidence; green LED emerging |
| **Melasma** | Low-fluence QS Nd:YAG (combination only); dual-mode 1,064 nm; vascular PDL in selected patients | Combination with QS laser or TXA; epidermal/vascular types | Adjunctive red/amber/NIR; blue light contraindicated |

Key cross-cutting conclusions:

1. **For photoaging, ablative fractional resurfacing delivers the greatest magnitude of improvement** (40–60% wrinkle reduction) but carries the highest downtime and PIH risk; NAFL and picosecond platforms offer acceptable efficacy with minimal recovery and are preferred in skin of color [2][16][24].
2. **For lentigines, picosecond technology has largely supplanted QS lasers** due to superior clearance and markedly lower PIH, particularly with fractional MLA delivery [30][31].
3. **For melasma, no monotherapy is curative.** The evidence consensus across 2024–2026 literature is that energy-based devices should be used as part of a multimodal strategy: photoprotection (including iron-oxide/visible-light blockers), triple combination cream, oral TXA in recalcitrant cases, and carefully parameterized lasers (low-fluence QS, dual-mode, or picosecond) with combination therapy reducing recurrence and adverse events [10][99][103].
4. **IPL's role in melasma is best defined as an adjunct**—particularly for the vascular/erythematous component and epidermal pigmentation—with combination protocols (IPL + QS Nd:YAG, IPL + TXA) showing the strongest evidence [67][68][69].
5. **LED/PBM is a low-risk, high-accessibility adjunct** for photoaging and, emerging evidence suggests, for melasma via non-thermal melanogenesis downregulation; its role in skin of color requires conservative dosing and avoidance of blue light [75][82][96].
6. **Patient satisfaction does not always track objective improvement**: the 2026 QS+IPL meta-analysis found no significant satisfaction benefit despite superior MASI reduction, and several LED studies show placebo-response rates of 16–25%—underscoring the need for expectation management and sham-controlled trial designs [68][82].

---

## 9. Conclusion

The scientific landscape of light-based aesthetic medicine has evolved dramatically over the past five years. Fractional ablative and non-ablative lasers remain the workhorses of photoaging treatment, with optimized parameter selection (low-energy/high-density CO₂, 1,927-nm thulium, dual-wavelength platforms) expanding safety in skin of color. Picosecond technology has transformed pigmentary lesion treatment, offering photoacoustic melanin fragmentation with substantially reduced PIH. IPL continues to evolve through optimized pulse architectures, vascular-targeted filters, and rational combination protocols. LED photobiomodulation has matured from adjunct novelty to evidence-based therapy, with multiple sham-controlled RCTs confirming efficacy for photoaging and emerging data supporting its role in hyperpigmentation—provided blue light is avoided in melasma.

For melasma specifically, the field has converged on a sobering but actionable consensus: chronic management with combination therapy, vascular assessment, TXA (topical, oral, or laser-delivered), and conservative, well-spaced laser sessions with strict photoprotection. The most promising recent advances—dual-mode 1,064-nm lasers, 730/785-nm picosecond platforms, PDL for vascular melasma, LADD with TXA, and PRP-based biologics—target the disease's multifactorial pathogenesis rather than melanin alone. Future research priorities include standardized parameter reporting, longer follow-up (>12 months), head-to-head device comparisons, and sham-controlled trials in Fitzpatrick IV–VI populations.
