# Transgender Themes in Middle Eastern and North African Cinema: Identities, States, and Screens

## 1. Introduction

Middle Eastern and North African (MENA) cinema has, over the past three decades, produced a small but significant corpus of films engaging transgender themes — from Iranian state-sanctioned narratives of sex reassignment to Turkish and Israeli family dramas, from observational documentaries to diasporic experiments. These films emerge from a region where transgender existence is legally, theologically, and socially contested in unusually complex ways: homosexuality is criminalized — sometimes capital — across much of the region, while Iran, paradoxically, has institutionalized sex reassignment surgery as a state-subsidized, clerically sanctioned practice. Transgender-themed cinema from the MENA region thus cannot be read through the templates of Euro-American trans representation. It demands a theoretical framework that synthesizes trans studies, feminist and queer film theory, postcolonial critique, and area-specific knowledge of Islamic jurisprudence, censorship regimes, and colonial legal legacies.

The field is nascent but consolidating. A forthcoming *Handbook of Trans Cinema* (edited by Douglas Vakoch) explicitly solicits chapters on trans cinema from Algeria, Egypt, Iran, Iraq, Israel, Lebanon, Morocco, Palestine, Saudi Arabia, Turkey, and the UAE, indicating that MENA trans cinema has become a recognized subfield of global trans film studies [1]. Foundational scholarship includes Kaveh Bassiri's survey of transgender identity in Iranian cinema [2], Maria Abdel Karim's analysis of queer representation in Middle Eastern women's films [3], and work in transnational feminist approaches to MENA media [4]. This paper offers a comprehensive overview of the topic, grounded in trans theory and film theory, with in-depth case studies of five films: *Facing Mirrors* (Iran, 2011), *Be Like Others* (Iran/USA, 2008), *Crossing* (Turkey/Sweden, 2024), *Melting Away* (Israel, 2012), and *Adam Barfi* (Iran, 1995). It situates these films within the broader socio-political, legal, religious, and cultural landscape of the MENA region, attending to the colonial genealogies of sexual criminalization, the role of Islamic jurisprudence, censorship mechanisms, and the dynamics of diaspora and exile.

## 2. Theoretical Framework

### 2.1 Trans Studies Foundations

Contemporary trans studies provides the conceptual vocabulary for analyzing transgender cinematic representation. Susan Stryker's founding essay "My Words to Victor Frankenstein Above the Village of Chamounix: Performing Transgender Rage" (1994) reclaims the figure of the monster as an allegory for the transsexual's relationship to medical authority. Stryker writes: "Like the monster, I am too often perceived as less than fully human due to the means of my embodiment; like the monster's as well, my exclusion from human community fuels a deep and abiding rage in me." The essay challenges readers to "investigate your nature as I have been compelled to confront mine" — to discover "the seams and sutures in yourself" [5]. This framework is directly applicable to MENA contexts, where transgender bodies are constructed, certified, subsidized, and disciplined by interlocking medico-legal and religious apparatuses.

Sandy Stone's "The Empire Strikes Back: A Posttranssexual Manifesto" (1987/1991), written in response to Janice Raymond's transphobic *The Transsexual Empire*, critiques the medical feedback loop through which transsexuals must perform diagnostic narratives to access surgery, and the demand to "pass" that requires erasing one's trans history. Stone proposes the "posttranssexual" subject who "reads oneself aloud" and writes "oneself into the discourses by which one has been written" [6]. As this paper will show, Iran's state apparatus of trans recognition constitutes one of the most extreme instances of the medical-diagnostic feedback loop Stone describes — while simultaneously creating conditions for what Afsaneh Najmabadi calls "transing and transpassing" across sex-gender walls.

Jack Halberstam's *Female Masculinity* (1998) and *In a Queer Time and Place: Transgender Bodies, Subcultural Lives* (2005) introduced the "transgender gaze" and the concept of queer temporality. Halberstam defines queer time as "the perverse turn away from the narrative coherence of adolescence – early adulthood – marriage – reproduction – child rearing – retirement – death" [7]. This is especially salient in MENA contexts where the marriage imperative functions as a powerful social technology: as Najmabadi's research shows, social pressure to marry drives some Iranians toward transition, and same-sex couples face partnership dissolution when partners feel compelled to marry [43]. Halberstam's analysis of the transgender gaze — developed through readings of *Boys Don't Cry* and *By Hook or By Crook* — argues that trans spectatorship reverses the psychoanalytic economy of lack, offering a model for analyzing how MENA films position trans and cis viewers alike.

Julia Serano's *Whipping Girl* (2007) introduces the concept of **transmisogyny**: the specific marginalization of trans women and transfeminine people, distinct from transphobia in general. Serano argues that "the majority of jokes made at the expense of trans people center on 'men wearing dresses' or 'men who want their penises cut off'" — that is not transphobia, but transmisogyny [8][9]. She also theorizes "oppositional sexism" (the belief that female and male are rigid, mutually exclusive categories) and "traditional sexism" (the belief that masculinity is superior to femininity), and documents the cultural obsession with transfeminine expression — "effemimania." As demonstrated below, transmisogyny operates in distinctive ways in MENA contexts, where MtF transsexuals face greater stigma than FtMs partly due to cultural associations with being *kuni* (the receptive partner in anal penetration) — a stigma with no equivalent for transmasculine individuals [43].

Paul B. Preciado's *Testo Junkie* (2008/2013) theorizes the "pharmacopornographic era," in which the pharmaceutical industry, pornography, and late capitalism jointly regulate bodies. Preciado's self-administered testosterone becomes a political act of "biodrag" — the "pharmacopornographic production of somatic fictions of femininity and masculinity" [10]. This framework illuminates how hormones and surgery circulate through MENA state apparatuses, from Iran's subsidized surgeries to Lebanon's hormone black market. C. Riley Snorton's *Black on Both Sides* (2017) demonstrates that racialization and transness are historically co-constituted, reminding us that gender mutability has always been differentially available and regulated across populations [11]. The anthology *Trap Door: Trans Cultural Production and the Politics of Visibility* (2017) articulates the central paradox of trans representation: increased visibility has coincided with intensified violence and legal suppression. As the editors write, "this is the trap of the visual: it offers — or, more accurately, it is frequently offered to us as — the primary path through which trans people might have access to livable lives" [12]. Cáel M. Keegan's *Lana and Lilly Wachowski: Sensing Transgender* (2018) proposes reading film as a technology that can "disrupt, rearrange, and evolve the cinematic exchange with the senses in the same manner that trans* disrupts, rearranges and evolves discrete genders and sexes," assuming a trans* spectator rather than a cisgender one [13]. Finally, Howard Chiang's recent critique of Western-centrism in trans studies — his call for "transtopia" as a method that understands non-Western categories like *hijra*, *waria*, *kathoey*, and *mukhannath* on their own terms — is essential for any serious engagement with MENA trans material [14].

### 2.2 Film Theory and the Cinematics of Gender

Laura Mulvey's "Visual Pleasure and Narrative Cinema" (1975) remains the foundational text of feminist film theory. Mulvey argues that classical Hollywood cinema is structured by the male gaze: woman functions as "bearer of meaning, not maker of meaning," displayed as passive spectacle ("to-be-looked-at-ness") while the male protagonist controls the action. The film spectator's pleasure derives from scopophilia (erotic looking) and narcissistic identification, organized through three looks: the camera's, the audience's, and the characters' [15]. Mulvey's framework — and its critiques, notably bell hooks' "oppositional gaze" — requires rethinking for MENA cinema, where, as Negar Mottahedeh shows, post-revolutionary Iranian cinema developed a cinematic grammar that explicitly refuses Hollywood's voyeuristic codes [19].

Judith Butler's theory of gender performativity, from *Gender Trouble* (1990) and *Bodies That Matter* (1993), holds that gender is not an internal identity but a "doing" — a ritualized, citational performance constrained by the "heterosexual matrix." "There is no gender identity behind the expressions of gender; that identity is performatively constituted by the very 'expressions' that are said to be its results." Butler argues that drag can be subversive to the extent that it exposes the imitative structure of all gender [16]. While Butler's work has sometimes been misread as claiming gender is voluntary, her emphasis on the coercive, iterative production of gendered embodiment is crucial for analyzing films in which characters must perform gender for state certifiers, families, and border officials.

Queer and trans film theory has extended these insights. Halberstam's concept of the transgender gaze and subsequent scholarship — such as Joshua Bastian Cole's trans reading of *Mulholland Drive* — attend to the ways trans spectators "see through" normative gender and recognize trans kinship on screen [17]. Third Cinema theory, articulated by Argentine filmmakers Fernando Solanas and Octavio Getino in "Toward a Third Cinema" (1969), provides a postcolonial framework: the camera is "the inexhaustible expropriator of image-weapons"; Third Cinema is "the decolonization of culture," made against both Hollywood (First Cinema) and the bourgeois auteur film (Second Cinema) [18]. Though Latin American in origin, Third Cinema was conceived as tricontinental, and its emphasis on clandestine production, collective creation, and revolutionary spectatorship resonates with the conditions under which queer and trans MENA cinema circulates today — from Tunisia's underground Mawjoudin Queer Film Festival to diaspora streaming platforms.

Negar Mottahedeh's *Displaced Allegories: Post-Revolutionary Iranian Cinema* (2008) is indispensable for understanding Iranian film aesthetics. Mottahedeh argues that Ayatollah Khomeini's injunction to "cleanse" cinema of Western influence, combined with modesty codes governing the representation of women's bodies, produced a distinct cinematic language: Iranian cinema rejects the shot-reverse-shot economy of the gaze, uses extreme long shots to substitute for heterosexual looking, and draws on indigenous traditions like *ta'ziyeh* performance. Mottahedeh provocatively calls this the "apotheosis of 1970s feminist gaze theory" [19]. This framework explains why Iranian trans-themed films look so different from their Western counterparts — and why they are legible to Iranian audiences as political interventions in their own right.

Finally, Jasbir Puar's concept of **homonationalism** — the fusion of homosexuality with nationalist and imperialist agendas, whereby Western states deploy gay rights to justify intervention in Muslim-majority countries — is essential for situating MENA queer and trans cinema within global power dynamics [20]. Relatedly, Joseph Massad's *Desiring Arabs* (2007) argues that Western-led LGBTQ activism (the "Gay International") imposes Western identity categories on Arab societies, "heterosexualizing a world that is being forced to be fixed by a Western binary" [21]. While Massad's argument has been criticized for erasing Arab queer agency, the debate it ignited frames the politics of identity and representation in MENA trans cinema: films made for international festival audiences must negotiate the Orientalist gaze even as they speak to local realities.

### 2.3 Toward a Framework for MENA Trans Cinema

A robust framework for MENA trans cinema must hold several registers simultaneously. First, it must read films through trans studies concepts — Stryker's monstrous constructedness, Stone's posttranssexuality, Serano's transmisogyny, Halberstam's queer temporality — while historicizing these concepts in non-Western contexts. Second, it must deploy film theory's attention to cinematic form — the gaze, editing, mise-en-scène, genre — to understand how films encode and resist state and religious ideologies of gender. Third, it must attend to what the 2020 *Hypatia* article "Cross-Dressing and Gender (Tres)Passing" calls the "transgender move": a temporary space of political and agential potential that cross-dressing and passing figures open up in post-1990s Iranian cinema, produced in part by censorship codes that ban direct representation of sexuality [62]. Fourth, it must foreground the work of scholars like Afsaneh Najmabadi, whose ethnography of Iranian transsexuality reveals that the medico-legal apparatus of diagnosis and "filtering" — designed to separate "true transsexuals" from homosexuals — has paradoxically created porous boundaries and social spaces for gays and lesbians. As one pre-operative FtM transsexual in Tehran told Najmabadi: "Once I was diagnosed as TS, I started having sex with my girlfriend without feeling guilty" [43][44]. This is the counterintuitive, locally specific reality that any theory of MENA trans cinema must be able to accommodate.

## 3. The Socio-Political, Legal, and Cultural Landscape of the MENA Region

### 3.1 Colonial Legacies and Legal Frameworks

Most MENA states inherited laws criminalizing homosexuality from French or British colonial administration [23]. The British-Indian Penal Code of 1860, including its Section 377 criminalizing "carnal intercourse against the order of nature," was devised, in Lord Macaulay's words, to "inculcate European morality into resistant masses." Nearly two-thirds of the 69 countries where homosexuality is illegal today were once under British control [25]. The Ottoman Empire decriminalized sodomy in 1858, but British occupation reimposed bans in Iraq [22]. French colonial law had a different but equally consequential trajectory: the Napoleonic Code decriminalized same-sex activity in 1791, yet colonial-era French codes in North Africa — such as Tunisia's Article 230 of the 1913 Penal Code, which criminalizes "sodomy" with up to three years' imprisonment — continue to structure the legal persecution of LGBTQ+ and trans people [23]. Morocco's Article 489 (838 prosecutions between 2017 and 2020) similarly dates from the post-independence penal code but is continuous with colonial-era regulation [23][25].

Post-independence states have not merely retained colonial laws; they have often expanded and weaponized them. As Human Rights Watch documents, Egyptian authorities criminalize LGBTQ+ people under the rubric of "debauchery" (*fujur*) — a term absent from the original 1951 prostitution law but interpreted by the Court of Cassation to mean "sex between men." Article 9(c) of Law 10/1961 punishes "habitual" practice of debauchery with 3 months to 3 years' imprisonment [33]. Since the 2013 coup, President Abdel Fattah al-Sisi's government has dramatically expanded anti-LGBT crackdowns — including the 2017 arrests of over 60 people at a Mashrou' Leila concert — using them to demonstrate religiously conservative credentials while repressing broader dissent [25][33]. In 2024, an Egyptian court explicitly interpreted the Cybercrime Law as criminalizing homosexuality [23]. Most MENA states lack standardized legal gender recognition procedures; where they exist, they require surgical and hormonal interventions, expensive forensic medical examinations, and can take 3–10 years. Of 19 identified legal gender recognition cases in the region, 73.67% resulted in unfavorable outcomes [23].

Country-specific legal landscapes vary widely:

- **Iran**: Homosexuality is punishable by death, yet sex reassignment surgery is legal, state-subsidized, and (since the 1987 Khomeini fatwa) religiously sanctioned. Transgender people are recognized only if they undergo SRS; non-binary genders are not recognized. The process requires parental approval, psychiatric evaluations, virginity tests, chromosomal tests, and approval from the Family Court and Legal Medicine Organization [26]. Some 200–370 surgeries are performed annually, with the Imam Committee providing interest-free loans [29]. Between 1987 and 2001, 200 males and 70 females submitted sex change petitions to the National Legal Medical Board, of which 214 were approved [43]. Human rights organizations report that the pre-surgery process is abusive, surgeons are often ill-trained, and the government fails to meet WPATH standards [26]. A 2021 study found that 92% of trans women in Iran had faced verbal or emotional violence and over 70% physical violence [26]. Crucially, the BBC and human rights groups document that gay men and lesbians are pressured toward surgery as a "cure": Arsham Parsi of the Iranian Railroad for Queer Refugees estimates that 45% of those who have had surgery are not transgender but gay [27].
- **Turkey**: Same-sex activity is legal — a rare exception in the region — but Pride marches have been banned since 2015, and transgender people face pervasive discrimination and violence. Legal gender change is possible [22][25].
- **Egypt**: No explicit criminalization of homosexuality, but the "debauchery" provisions function as a de facto ban. The Egyptian Medical Syndicate's sex reassignment committee stopped granting approvals, and Al-Azhar's religious authorities oppose gender-affirming surgery except for intersex individuals. An underground medical industry has emerged; a 26-year-old trans man died in 2021 after an unregulated procedure. Detainees are subjected to forced anal examinations constituting torture [24][33].
- **Lebanon**: Article 534 of the Penal Code (acts "contrary to the laws of nature") has been interpreted by courts not to apply to same-sex acts, but Article 521 explicitly criminalizes "men disguising themselves as women" with up to 6 years' imprisonment. A 2016 appeals court allowed a trans man to change his documents, but procedural obstacles remain. The economic crisis raised the price of a three-shot supply of hormones from $1.96 to $100 [13][23][24].
- **Tunisia**: Article 230 (1913) criminalizes sodomy; 1,458 convictions have been recorded since the 2011 Revolution, with 121 in 2019 alone. In 2018, a Tunisian court granted legal gender recognition to a trans man who had received gender-affirming care in Germany — a landmark — but the judiciary generally refuses gender marker changes. The Tunisian Medical Association banned forced anal exams in 2017 [23].
- **Morocco**: Article 489 criminalizes same-sex relations; no legal pathway for gender recognition; LGBTQI+ organizations cannot legally register [23].
- **Iraq**: In April 2024, parliament passed an anti-LGBT law criminalizing same-sex relations (10–15 years), "promotion" of homosexuality, and "biological sex change based on personal desire and inclination" (1–3 years), while carving out exceptions for intersex "normalization" surgeries. The UN estimates 750 killings of LGBT people in Iraq since 2003 [40][41][22].
- **Saudi Arabia**: Same-sex activity is punishable by death under Wahhabist Sharia; gender expression is criminalized ("men behaving like women" and vice versa). Dozens of transgender people are arrested annually; two transgender Pakistani nationals were tortured to death by police in 2017; trans woman Eden Knight died by suicide in 2023 after being forced to detransition. Over 2,000 sex reassignment surgeries have been performed, but the Ministry of Health does not permit sex change operations [23][26].
- **UAE**: The 2021 Federal Penal Code removed stoning and flogging but Article 409 criminalizes consensual same-sex conduct; gender-affirming care is explicitly banned (allowed only for intersex "correction"); changing legal gender has been illegal since 2019 [42].
- **Israel**: The most LGBTQ+-supportive state in the region. Same-sex activity has been legal since 1988; transgender people can change legal gender without surgery since 2015; a 2024 law allows removal of deadnames from ID cards. However, Palestinian and human rights organizations criticize Israel's "pinkwashing" — deploying its LGBTQ+ record to obscure its occupation and military operations in Gaza [22][20].

### 3.2 Islamic Jurisprudence and Gender Variance

Islamic jurisprudence has historically recognized gender diversity more capaciously than contemporary state laws suggest. Classical fiqh developed the category of the *khuntha* (intersex person), acknowledging roughly 1.7% of the population as having legitimate claims to social and legal recognition. Jurists distinguished biological sex (*jince*) from self-identification (*iqrar*), establishing a two-step process: sex assignment at birth based on external genitalia, and review at puberty (idha balagh) using indicators such as menstruation, pregnancy, beard growth, and sexual orientation. Importantly, the *khuntha* is the only person who can truly reveal their gender through *iqrar* — self-declaration [31]. The term *mukhannath* (effeminate man) described individuals who appeared feminine and functioned socially in roles typically carried out by women. *Mukhannathun* existed in pre-Islamic Arabia and early Islam as servants, musicians, and marriage brokers; figures like the celebrated singer Ṭuways ("Little Peacock") were prominent in early Islamic culture. Persecution began under the Umayyad caliphate, when Caliph Sulaymān ordered the castration of Medinan *mukhannathun* [32].

Modern fatwas on sex reassignment surgery are divided. In the late 1980s, two landmark rulings legalized SRS under Islamic law. In Iran, Ayatollah Khomeini issued a fatwa in 1987 (following earlier rulings) after the activist Maryam Hatoon Molkara — beaten by guards — confronted him in person, revealing her breasts to prove she was a woman trapped in a man's body [28]. The fatwa made Iran the only Islamic country where sex reassignment is officially recognized, leading *The Guardian* to observe that "the Islamic Republic of Iran occupies the unlikely role of global leader for sex change" [29]. In Egypt, Sunni Mufti Muhammad Sayyid Tantawi issued a fatwa in June 1988 permitting SRS for the *mukhannath* in the case of Sayyad Abd Allah/"Sally," framing surgery as *tas'heeh* (correction of a concealed condition) rather than *taghyeer khalq Allah* (altering God's creation). The Islamic Fiqh Council of the Muslim World League affirmed this reasoning at Mecca in 1989 [30][31]. Both fatwas used the principle of necessity (*maqasid al-shari'a*).

However, prevailing Sunni authorities distinguish between permitted "sex correction" for intersex individuals and prohibited "sex change" for transgender people, and states including Egypt, the UAE, Jordan, Oman, and Morocco have institutionalized this distinction [23]. Progressive Muslim scholars counter that Qur'an 4:119 (often cited against transition) originally referred to pagan practices of mutilating animals for idols, and that Qur'an 42:49–50 — "He grants females... males... a mix of males and females... and causes whomever He pleases to be barren" — affirms gender diversity as divine creation. They further note that early Islamic society accepted *mukhannathun* [30]. Jewish tradition in the region similarly recognizes gender diversity: the Talmud discusses the *androgynos*, and classical Jewish law identifies at least six genders, including the *aylonit* and *saris*. These theological resources complicate any monolithic account of "Islam vs. transgender rights," even as state laws and social attitudes remain deeply oppressive.

### 3.3 Censorship and the Film Industry

Film censorship in the Arab world has deep roots. Egypt institutionalized censorship in 1914 under the Palace and the English Embassy. As Dina Mansour documents, censorship decisions are subjective and discretionary: *The Yacoubian Building* (2006) was released uncut despite explicit sexual content, while *Cairo Exit* (2009) was banned for depicting interfaith and premarital relationships. Long-term censorship has promoted commercial escapism and discouraged films that reflect societal problems [35]. Across the MENA region, censorship of LGBTQ+ content has intensified: the 2022 Disney/Pixar film *Lightyear* was banned across at least ten MENA countries for a same-sex kiss; Algeria banned *Barbie* (2023) for "promoting homosexuality and other Western deviances" [64][34]. Iran censors LGBT-related content with penalties up to death [34]. Lebanon's Article 521 enforces a legal regime in which trans women's presence in public is itself criminalized, and the state has banned films for LGBT content [13][34]. Egypt's Media Syndicate prohibits media statements supporting homosexuality [25].

Filmmakers respond with a range of strategies: allegory, symbolism, historical displacement, and self-censorship. Queer Arab cinema dates back to the homoerotic subtexts of Egyptian classical cinema — the 1950s period of social critique, before the stringent 1971 censorship codes that pushed queer representation toward the monstrous and pathological. The advent of digital production and international distribution allowed independent queer filmmaking to flourish in the 2000s [38]. Covert representation (as in Nadine Labaki's *Caramel*, 2007) avoids censorship; overt representation (as in Maryam Keshavarz's *Circumstance*, 2011, or Maysaloun Hamoud's *Bar Bahar*, 2016) is more impactful for social change but invites bans, death threats, and fatwas [3]. Festival circuits provide alternative spaces: the Mawjoudin Queer Film Festival in Tunis — the only queer film festival in the Arab world — operates on a "patronage system" with undisclosed venues and no-photo policies; the online "Aflamuna" festival (2020), launched by Cinema Al Fouad, Mawjoudin, and Jeem in partnership with Human Rights Watch, was dedicated to Sarah Hegazy, an Egyptian queer activist who died after torture in prison [37][38].

### 3.4 Contemporary Activism and the Diaspora

The post-2011 uprisings had contradictory effects: they galvanized LGBTQ+ organizing but also provoked intensified repression in Egypt, Tunisia, and elsewhere. Human Rights Watch's report "Audacity in Adversity" (2018) documents dozens of organizations operating under severe constraints through community-building, digital security training, and strategic alliance with feminist and human rights movements [36]. Organizations include Helem (Lebanon), Shams and Mawjoudin (Tunisia), Rasan and IraQueer (Iraq), Al-Qaws and Aswat (Palestine), and regional networks like MantiQitna. Post-Arab Spring wins include the end of forced anal exams in Lebanon and Tunisia, and the Lebanese court rulings rejecting the use of Article 534 against same-sex acts [36][23].

The diaspora is central to MENA queer and trans cultural production. Filmmakers working in Europe and North America produce films that cannot be screened at home, using international co-productions, crowdfunding, and festival circuits. The Arab Film and Media Institute's 2026 "Queer Arab Films" list includes over 50 features, documentaries, and shorts, from Amrou Al-Kadhi's *Layla* (2024) about a non-binary Arab drag queen to Leyla Bouzid's *In A Whisper* (2026), nominated for the Golden Bear at Berlin [61]. Trans and queer MENA artists in exile navigate dual constraints: as playwright Raphaël Aimé Khouri (a trans Arab artist living between Berlin and Egypt) describes, his work in the Middle East must always be shown "behind closed doors, by invitation only," and as a trans person he can no longer enter Jordan on his own passport [39]. These conditions shape the aesthetics and distribution of the films discussed below.

## 4. Case Studies

### 4.1 *Facing Mirrors* (Iran, 2011, dir. Negar Azarbayjani)

**Narrative.** *Facing Mirrors* (*Ayenehaye Rooberoo*) is the first Iranian feature film with a female-to-male transgender protagonist to be written, produced, and screened inside Iran. The film centers on two characters: Adineh/Eddie (Shayesteh Irani), a pre-operative trans man from an upper-class family who is seeking a passport to undergo sex reassignment surgery abroad, and Rana (Ghazal Shakeri), a religious, conservative taxi driver from a working-class background. Their lives intersect by chance when Rana's car is repossessed and she begins driving for a company that assigns Eddie as her passenger. Despite initial disgust — Rana throws Eddie out of her taxi upon learning he is "a woman who wants to become a man" — the two women form an unlikely friendship. Rana helps Eddie secure his passport, and Eddie helps Rana confront the patriarchal expectations (an imprisoned husband, a predatory creditor) that constrain her life. A remarkable scene shows Rana struggling to introduce Eddie to her young son — as "uncle" or "aunt" — a moment that crystallizes the film's central question: what vocabulary exists for transgender existence in contemporary Iran? [46][47][48].

**Cinematic techniques.** The film's title announces its guiding metaphor: mirrors, reflection, and self-perception structure the visual field. Azarbayjani uses intimate framing and close two-shots to chart the emotional convergence of the two protagonists, and the taxi — a mobile, semi-public space — becomes the stage for their developing trust. The film deliberately refuses both the Orientalist exoticism of Western queer films about the region and the melodramatic victim narrative; its aesthetics are those of Iranian social realism, with location shooting in Tehran and naturalistic performances. Critics note a weakness: the Ajam Media Collective's queer Iranian analysis argues that the film fails to grant its protagonists full subjectivity, showing them "as mere (queer) sexual objects as opposed to subjects of their own destiny" [47]. Yet the same review acknowledges that the film disrupted Orientalist narratives by presenting Iranian characters with agency, complexity, and resilience [47].

**Cultural context.** The film's Iranian production and reception must be understood against the fatwa-sanctioned regime of trans recognition described above. As *The Guardian* reported on the film's release, producer Fereshteh Taerpour initially found it "very strange... that sex-change operation is permitted in Iran... The authorities give loans and even issue new ID cards after the surgery, something that is not legal in many countries." Trans rights campaigner Shadi Amin offered the film's sharpest gloss: "In a democratic society, a sex-change operation is an option for transsexuals, but in Iran it's an obligation for their survival" [46]. *Facing Mirrors* received approval from Iranian authorities and was screened widely, including at Qom's Mofid University — a religious seminary — where it sparked debate. The film won awards at over 64 international festivals, including Best First Feature at San Francisco's Frameline Festival (2012) and honors at Iran's Fajr Film Festival [46][48].

**Engagement with transgender identities.** *Facing Mirrors* is notable for what it does not do: it does not frame Eddie's transness as a pathology, a sin, or a Western import. Eddie is portrayed as confident, self-knowing, and supported (if ambivalently) by his family, who fund his surgery. The film's radical gesture is to place a trans man at the center of a mainstream Iranian narrative and to show a religious woman's journey toward acceptance. Yet the casting of cisgender actress Shayesteh Irani in the role of Eddie — and the film's emphasis on Eddie's pre-operative body — raises the question of whether Iranian cinema can yet represent trans embodiment without a cisgender intermediary. Bassiri's survey notes that FTM characters in Iranian cinema often face coercive gender transitions, while MTF roles typically feature comedic elements; *Facing Mirrors* partially breaks this pattern by humanizing its FTM protagonist [2]. The film's ending — Eddie leaves for surgery abroad, Rana returns to her family — offers no utopian resolution, but it does insert "a thought-provoking and relatable narrative of queerness in the public imaginary" of Iran [47].

### 4.2 *Be Like Others* (Iran/USA/Canada/UK/France, 2008, dir. Tanaz Eshaghian)

**Narrative.** *Be Like Others* (also known as *Transsexual in Iran*) is a 74-minute documentary that explores the lives of transgender women in Iran, centered on Dr. Bahram Mir-Jalali's Mirdamad Surgical Centre in Tehran. The film follows two main subjects. Ali Askar, a 24-year-old man who does not identify as transgender but is feminine in appearance, sees surgery as his only viable option in a society where effeminate men are presumed homosexual and subject to persecution. He undergoes surgery, becoming Negar, but is disowned by his family, becomes depressed, and turns to sex work. Anoosh, another feminine young man, transitions to become Anahita and is initially happy and engaged, though her fiancé grows distant after the surgery. The film also features Vida, an "alpha-tranny" counselor who mentors patients, and a chorus of surgeons, clerics, and legal officials who explain the fatwa regime [49][50].

**Cinematic techniques.** The film is an observational documentary in the verité tradition: intimate access to surgical procedures, family confrontations, and state institutions. Variety called it "a powerful window into a once-hidden side of the country" and "a model of non-dogmatic filmmaking on a highly charged topic" [49]. Eshaghian — an Iranian-American director — positions the camera as a witness rather than an advocate, allowing subjects to speak at length. One of the film's most disturbing sequences shows a patient being asked, on the operating table, whether she would undergo surgery if she could live freely as a gay man; her answer is no. The film's non-judgmental form forces viewers to sit with the horror of that answer [50].

**Cultural context.** The film's explicit context is the asymmetry of Iranian law: "In the Islamic Republic of Iran, homosexuality is punishable by death. Twenty-five years ago, however, the Ayatollah Khomeini wrote a fatwa pronouncing transsexuality legal and allowing for sex-change operations" — as one surgeon states in the film. *Be Like Others* documents the state-subsidized transition pipeline: government loans, official certification, new identity documents — and the social death that often follows. The film's central argument, articulated by multiple subjects, is that many Iranians pursue sex reassignment not because they identify as transgender, but because "identifying as transsexual rather than homosexual allows them to live free from harassment" [49][50]. This is the "filtering" process Najmabadi describes, seen from the inside.

**Engagement with transgender identities.** The film raises profound ethical questions about whether Iran's trans-friendly legal framework is liberating or coercive. As one Letterboxd reviewer summarized: "the compulsion to undergo these operations shows how homosexuality is seen as beyond the pale" [49]. The film's achievement is to show that state recognition and social violence are not opposites but co-produced: the Iranian state recognizes the transsexual as a legal category precisely in order to foreclose homosexual existence. This complicates any triumphalist Western narrative about Iran being "ahead" of the West on trans rights — a point that resonates with Puar's critique of homonationalist frameworks [20]. Eshaghian's film suggests that what Western observers often cite as evidence of Iranian "liberalism" is in fact a mechanism of heteronormative governance. The film won three Teddy Awards at the Berlin International Film Festival, was nominated for the Sundance Grand Jury Prize, and received a GLAAD Media Award nomination [49]. In 2019, Bassiri identified *Be Like Others* as part of a documentary tradition on Iranian transsexuals that has gained prominence since 2003 [2].

### 4.3 *Crossing* (Turkey/Sweden/Denmark/France/Georgia, 2024, dir. Levan Akin)

**Narrative.** *Crossing* follows Lia (Mzia Arabuli), a retired schoolteacher from Batumi, Georgia, who sets out to find her long-lost niece Tekla, a transgender woman. Accompanied by Tekla's former neighbor, the impulsive teenager Achi (Lucas Kankava), Lia travels to Istanbul — the city where Tekla was last seen. They encounter Evrim (Deniz Dumanlı), a trans woman who works as a legal advocate for Istanbul's trans community and who helps them navigate the city's queer underground. As Lia searches for her niece, the film's emotional arc shifts: the question of whether Tekla will be found gives way to the question of whether Lia herself can change. The director has said the film's key "crossing" is "a change of mind and heart," and the film ultimately asks whether Lia needs to be found and forgiven rather than Tekla [51][52][53].

**Cinematic techniques.** The film is a road movie that doubles as a city symphony: Akin's camera moves through Istanbul's streets, bathhouses, bars, and trans neighborhoods, mapping the city as a space of refuge and risk. The Guardian praised it as "a thoroughly intelligent, emotionally engaging and robustly performed movie" [52]; the Rotten Tomatoes consensus calls it "a tender odyssey... a humanist triumph" [51]. Akin deliberately avoids the gritty victim narrative common to trans cinema: Variety notes he "makes a calculated choice to raise awareness of the trans community in Istanbul... through representation rather than manipulation" [51]. The film's tonal control — warm, comic, and melancholy by turns — and its use of Turkish, Georgian, and English reflect the multilingual reality of its characters.

**Cultural context.** *Crossing* is a co-production between Sweden, Denmark, France, Turkey, and Georgia, and its making was shaped by the region's politics. Akin, raised in Sweden by Georgian parents, delayed production by a year to conduct location research in Istanbul and rewrote the script after immersing himself in the city's trans community and its Lubunca slang (the queer argot of Turkish trans subculture) [53]. His previous film, *And Then We Danced* (2019), about a gay Georgian dancer, provoked violent protests and death threats from the Georgian Orthodox Church and ultra-conservative groups; Akin chose to make *Crossing* in Turkey regardless [53]. The film premiered at the 74th Berlin International Film Festival, opening the Panorama section, and won the Teddy Award Jury Prize for LGBTQ-themed films [51].

**Engagement with transgender identities.** *Crossing* is groundbreaking in its production ethics: Akin cast real trans community members in front of and behind the camera. Evrim is played by Deniz Dumanlı, a trans actress and human rights advocate, in her first film role. Akin's casting director in Turkey was a trans man, Bulut Sezer, and trans crew members worked in costume and other departments [51][53]. Akin has said: "I wanted Evrim to be a resilient character that faces the world with bravado. Deniz really has that spirit within herself" [53]. The film is unusual in centering an older cisgender protagonist who must learn to see a trans person — and, by extension, her own capacity for love. Its key line — "I love you regardless" — reframes acceptance not as tolerance but as kinship. The film won the Guldbagge Award for Best Film, Best Director, and Best Cinematography, the IFFI ICFT UNESCO Gandhi Medal, and the GLAAD Media Award for Outstanding Film – Limited Release [51]. As of 2026, it has not yet been the subject of extensive peer-reviewed scholarship, but it is likely to become a canonical text in both Turkish cinema studies and global trans film studies.

### 4.4 *Melting Away* (Israel, 2012, dir. Doron Eran)

**Narrative.** *Melting Away* (*Nemas Ba-Geshem*) was the first Israeli feature film to deal with parents coping with a transgender child. The premise is stark: Shlomo Shapira discovers that his teenage son Asaf likes to wear women's clothing. With his wife Galia's silent consent, he refuses to let Asaf into the house on a rainy night, hoping the lesson will force the boy to change. Four years later, Shlomo is dying of cancer. Galia hires a private investigator who finds Asaf — now Anna, a singer performing in Tel Aviv. Anna visits her dying father in the hospital disguised as a private nurse, slowly winning his heart as Galia also comes to accept her daughter. The film's title refers to a song Anna performs near the end, but also to the "melting away" of the prejudices, expectations, and resentments between parent and child [54][55].

**Cinematic techniques.** The film moves between past and present in a non-linear structure, intercutting the night of Asaf's rejection with the hospital scenes of reconciliation. The nurse's disguise plot generates sustained dramatic irony: the audience knows who Anna is; Shlomo does not. Director Doron Eran auditioned 120 actors before casting Hen Yanni in the lead dual role; Yanni's performance, praised by the Jewish News of Northern California for "build[ing] and honor[ing] our emotional investment in the characters' destinies," won a special breakthrough award at the Tel Aviv International LGBT Film Festival and an Ophir Award nomination for Best Actress [55][54].

**Cultural context.** The film's genesis is explicitly political. Eran conceived it after the 2009 Tel Aviv gay centre shooting, in which a gunman killed two people and wounded 15 at a Bar Noar event. Eran observed that "some of the parents don't want to come and visit their children because they don't want their neighbors to see on the TV that their kids are homosexuals and lesbians. That was the idea and motivation behind picking up this script again" [55]. The film thus intervenes in a specifically Israeli context: a state with relatively liberal legal structures for LGBTQ+ people, coexisting with families and communities in which rejection remains common. Eran's stated message is universalist: "I have three children and I think that a parent, when you decide to make a kid, you must understand that you must love them with no conditions" [55].

**Engagement with transgender identities.** *Melting Away* is significant for shifting narrative focus from the transgender subject to the family's journey — a choice that has both strengths and limitations. The film argues that the parents' adjustment is their own work, and that the trans child's role is not to educate or forgive but to be met halfway. This framework reflects a broader Israeli cinematic tendency and has been used in community contexts: the film was screened by Jewish Family Services programs to spark dialogue about family acceptance [55]. However, the film's casting of a cisgender actress in the role of a trans woman, and its emphasis on parental redemption, means it risks assimilating Anna's transness into a family melodrama of forgiveness. The film won audience awards at the Boston Jewish Film Festival, ImageOut Rochester, and Shalom Europa IFF [54][55]. It has not yet generated extensive English-language academic scholarship, but it is an essential data point for comparative analysis of trans cinema in Israel versus the Arab-majority MENA states.

### 4.5 *Adam Barfi* (Iran, 1995/1997, dir. Davood Mirbagheri)

**Narrative.** *Adam Barfi* (*Snowman*) is the genealogical precursor to Iranian trans cinema: a comedy about a middle-aged Iranian man, Abbas (Akbar Abdi), who is desperate to emigrate to America. Stranded in Turkey, he is persuaded by Iranian acquaintances that his only route to a US visa is to become a woman and marry an American. Abbas transforms into "Dorna," and the film follows the comic and dramatic complications that ensue. The film creates what one video essay describes as a "border zone" identity across three axes: Iran→America (migration), male→female (gender), and old→new Iranian cinema (temporal), with Turkey as the purgatory point where all three converge. In the ending, Dorna must die so that Abbas can return to Iran — suggesting that in-between states cannot survive, that the film's world is ultimately binary [56][57].

**Cinematic techniques.** *Adam Barfi* is a star vehicle for Akbar Abdi, Iran's beloved comic actor, and it mobilizes the full repertoire of Iranian popular cinema: slapstick, sentiment, and social satire. The film is shot by cinematographer Maziar Partow with music by Farhad Fakhreddini [56]. Critically, it draws on the *ta'ziyeh* passion-play tradition, in which male performers play female roles without any illusion of realism — a mode of cross-gender performance entirely distinct from Western mimesis. The POSITIF video essay analyzes the film through Judith Butler's gender performativity (gender as a role "hacked" through signs and repetition), the Sheikh San'an allegory from Attar's *Mantiq al-Tayr*, and the nostalgic "tipe" stock characters of pre-revolutionary *Film Farsi* [56].

**Cultural context.** The production history of *Adam Barfi* is as revealing as the film itself. Made in 1994 by the Howzeh Honari (Art Bureau), the film was banned for three years after a virulent attack in the Kayhan newspaper. The ban was signed by Ezzatollah Zarqami just before the Fajr Film Festival. Only after director Davoud Mirbagheri's supporters arranged a private screening for President Hashemi Rafsanjani and Ayatollah Khamenei — who deemed it unproblematic — was the film released in 1997. It became the year's highest-grossing film in Iran. Its release was met with violent attacks by Ansar-e Hezbollah groups, who assaulted cinemas and audiences (including a pregnant woman who suffered a miscarriage) without having seen the film [56]. The film thus paradoxically operates within the Islamic Republic's propagandistic framework of "cultural invasion" while exposing the instability of the very gender categories the state seeks to fix.

**Engagement with transgender identities.** *Adam Barfi* is not a film about transgender identity in the modern sense: it is about instrumental cross-dressing. Yet it is a crucial text in the genealogy of gender non-conformity in Iranian cinema. Kaveh Bassiri's survey identifies it as a key film illustrating the pattern whereby male-to-female roles in Iranian cinema carry comedic elements, while female-to-male characters face coercive gender transitions [2]. R. Kheshti's 2009 *Hypatia* article "Cross-Dressing and Gender (Tres)Passing" reads *Adam Barfi* as central to the construction of Iran's image as "transsexual capital of the world," and the 2020 *Hypatia* follow-up argues that Iranian censorship codes have made cross-dressing and passing "ubiquitous tropes" in New Iranian Cinema, opening a "transgender move" of agential potential [62][2]. The film's insistence that Dorna must die so Abbas can live is read by critics as an allegory of the Islamic Republic's investment in binary gender — even as the film's own pleasures derive from Dorna's vibrant, subversive existence. *Adam Barfi* demonstrates that contemporary Iranian trans cinema does not emerge from a vacuum but from a decades-long, contradictory history of cross-dressing comedy, state censorship, and popular fascination.

## 5. Beyond the Core: A Broader Corpus

The five films analyzed above are part of a larger, still-emerging corpus. Iranian documentary continues to engage trans themes: *This is Not Me* (2023, dir. Saeed Ghalipour) follows two trans men navigating legal recognition and surgery in Iran; it cannot be screened legally in Iran and was showcased at the Boston Festival of Films from Iran in 2023. Director Ghalipour notes that while sex reassignment is "the only legal form of being queer" in Iran, trans people still face invasive medical checks, court proceedings, and social ostracism, and many face suicide due to community rejection [58]. In the Gulf, *Only Men Go to the Grave* (UAE, 2016, dir. Abdulla Al Kaabi) is the first Arab Gulf film to tackle homosexuality and gender identity candidly; it passed state censors and won Best Emirati Film at the Dubai International Film Festival, with LGBTQ characters portrayed as traditional Arab mothers and wives rather than Westernized elites [59]. Diasporic documentary is also expanding the field: Aaron Jagger's 2025 study analyzes *Cinema Fouad* (Lebanon, 1993) and *Sultana's Reign* (2023) as records of transgender and gender-nonconforming Palestinians' diasporic experiences, read through Judith Butler, Hamid Naficy, and decolonial theory [60].

It is equally important to delimit the corpus. Several films often associated with MENA queer cinema are not transgender-themed: *Zenne Dancer* (Turkey, 2012), based on the honor killing of gay man Ahmet Yildiz, concerns gay identity; *Bar Bahar* (In Between, 2016, Palestine/Israel) centers three Palestinian women, one a lesbian; Maryam Keshavarz's *Circumstance* (2011) is a lesbian romance; and *The Man Who Sold His Skin* (Tunisia, 2020) concerns a refugee's tattooed body, not trans identity. These films matter contextually — they map the broader queer cinematic landscape in which trans films intervene — but they should not be conflated with transgender representation [3][61]. The AFMI's 2026 list also includes *Layla* (2024), about a non-binary Arab drag queen, and *In A Whisper* (2026, Tunisia/France), which expands the queer Arab canon further [61].

## 6. Key Debates: Representation, Authenticity, and the Politics of Visibility

The MENA trans corpus crystallizes several debates in trans film studies. First, the **paradox of visibility**: as *Trap Door* argues, increased trans representation has coincided with heightened violence and legal suppression; visibility is "offered to us as the primary path through which trans people might have access to livable lives," yet representation does not remedy poverty, murder, or police violence [12]. This is starkly visible in Iran, where state recognition of transsexuality coexists with 92% rates of violence against trans women [26]. Second, **transnormativity**: media scholars identify a pattern whereby only narrow, binary, "respectable" trans narratives gain mainstream visibility, excluding non-binary, poor, and gender-nonconforming subjects. MENA trans cinema — particularly its documentary mode — often resists transnormativity by foregrounding subjects who transition under coercion or who reject the state's terms (as in *Be Like Others*).

Third, **cisgender casting and authorship**. *Facing Mirrors* casts cisgender actress Shayesteh Irani; *Melting Away* casts cisgender actress Hen Yanni; *Adam Barfi* uses a cisgender male comic actor. Only *Crossing* centers a trans actress in a trans role. In Western trans media discourse, cis casting has become a flashpoint; in the Iranian context, the question is more complex, since a trans actor playing a trans role openly could face legal and social jeopardy. The debate recalls Susan Stryker's observation that "the more range we have for telling different kinds of trans stories, the less difference it makes as to whether or not a trans person is playing that role" — the reverse is also true: where representation is scarce, casting decisions carry enormous weight. Fourth, **authenticity and the politics of categories**. Najmabadi's research shows that Iranian trans identity is produced through a medico-legal apparatus with no equivalent to Western identity politics: "What does it mean that concepts of gender, sex, and sexuality — along with their (in)distinction from, and relations to, one another — have been formed in a context that has not been shaped to any substantial degree by the identity politics of gender and sexuality, or by queer activism and queer critical theory?" [43]. Any critical reading of MENA trans cinema must resist imposing Western categories while also refusing the Orientalist fascination with Iran's "sex-change factory."

Finally, **the politics of international reception**. The Ajam Media Collective's comparison of *Facing Mirrors* with *Circumstance* is instructive: *Circumstance*, a film that conforms to "Orientalist fantasies about oppressed Muslim women and queer people," received far more Western media attention than *Facing Mirrors*, which disrupts those fantasies [47]. This dynamic — what we might call the festival-complex gaze — shapes which MENA trans films get funded, screened, and canonized. Akkadia Ford's concept of the "Trans New Wave" — independent transgender cinema produced since 2008 outside the studio system — provides a further frame for understanding these films as part of a global movement with regional specificities [63].

## 7. Conclusion

Transgender-themed cinema from the Middle East and North Africa is a small but consequential body of work that demands new theoretical syntheses. Reading *Facing Mirrors*, *Be Like Others*, *Crossing*, *Melting Away*, and *Adam Barfi* together reveals a region-specific cinematic grammar: the pervasive presence of the state (certifying, subsidizing, criminalizing), the negotiation of Islamic jurisprudence, the legacy of colonial law, the pressures of family and marriage, and the constant mediation of an international gaze that oscillates between Orientalist horror and homonationalist appropriation. These films do not simply "represent" transgender people; they interrogate the very conditions under which transgender existence becomes legible — as medical diagnosis, as legal category, as family crisis, as comic disguise, as human right.

The theoretical resources of trans studies — Stryker's monster, Stone's posttranssexual, Halberstam's queer time, Serano's transmisogyny, Preciado's pharmacopornographic biopolitics — find their limits and their extensions in these texts. Film theory — from Mulvey's gaze to Butler's performativity to Mottahedeh's displaced allegories to Third Cinema's guerrilla aesthetics — illuminates the formal strategies through which MENA filmmakers register and resist the regimes of gender that structure their societies. And the scholarship of Najmabadi, Bassiri, Chiang, and others reminds us that the categories of "transgender," "homosexual," and "MENA" are themselves historically produced, contested, and always already entangled with colonialism, religion, and state power.

As the field consolidates — with the *Handbook of Trans Cinema* and a new generation of scholars and filmmakers — the films discussed here will likely become anchor texts. They deserve to be read not as regional curiosities or as data points for Western theory, but as cinematic interventions that reimagine what it means to live, love, and become otherwise in a region too often reduced to stereotypes. The mirrors they hold up to their audiences — Iranian seminary students, Istanbul's trans community, international festival-goers — reflect not only the faces of their characters but the seams and sutures of the social orders that produce them.
