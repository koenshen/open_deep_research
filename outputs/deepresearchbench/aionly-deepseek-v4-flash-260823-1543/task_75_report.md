# Therapeutic Interventions to Modulate Plasma Metal Ion Concentrations for Cardiovascular Disease Prevention and Treatment: A Comprehensive Review

## Executive Summary

This comprehensive review examines the role of plasma metal ions in cardiovascular disease (CVD) pathophysiology and evaluates whether therapeutic interventions aimed at modulating these ions represent effective preventive or therapeutic strategies. The evidence reveals a complex, often U-shaped relationship between metal ion concentrations and cardiovascular health—both deficiency and excess can be detrimental. While mechanistic studies strongly support the involvement of zinc, copper, selenium, magnesium, iron, and calcium in CVD pathogenesis, the clinical trial evidence for supplementation-based interventions remains largely inconclusive. The most robust clinical evidence exists for intravenous iron therapy in heart failure with iron deficiency (now supported by Class I, Level A recommendations in ESC guidelines) and magnesium supplementation for blood pressure reduction. However, major trials—including SELECT (selenium), TACT2 (EDTA chelation), and FeAST (iron reduction)—have failed to demonstrate benefit, and some interventions (calcium supplements, high-dose selenium) may increase risk. Emerging approaches, including ferroptosis inhibitors, copper-modulating therapies, nanoparticle delivery systems, and nutrigenetic-guided interventions, represent promising frontiers, though most remain in preclinical or early clinical stages. The evidence strongly suggests that future strategies should focus on restoring metal homeostasis rather than simple supplementation, and that genetic factors significantly modify individual responses to metal modulation.

## 1. Introduction

Cardiovascular diseases remain the leading cause of death globally, accounting for an estimated 20 million deaths annually, with the burden increasing by 60% over the past three decades [Cuproptosis and CVD: PMC/Reviews in Cardiovascular Medicine, 2025]. Metal ions play fundamental roles in cardiovascular physiology, serving as cofactors for antioxidant enzymes, structural components of proteins, regulators of ion channels, and signaling molecules. However, the therapeutic potential of modulating these ions has been a subject of intense debate and investigation.

This review addresses four key questions: (1) Which plasma metal ions have been implicated in CVD pathophysiology and through what mechanisms? (2) What specific therapeutic interventions have been studied? (3) What is the clinical evidence base from randomized controlled trials, meta-analyses, and large observational studies? (4) What emerging therapeutic approaches are on the horizon?

## 2. Plasma Metal Ions Implicated in Cardiovascular Disease: Mechanisms and Pathophysiology

### 2.1 Zinc (Zn)

Zinc is an essential micronutrient involved in over 300 enzymes and 2,000 transcriptional factors, with critical roles in cell differentiation, DNA synthesis, immune function, and antioxidant defense [Zinc as a Biomarker of Cardiovascular Health: Frontiers in Nutrition, 2021]. The human body contains 2–3 g of zinc, with approximately 57% in skeletal muscle and 29% in bone; heart and blood plasma contain 0.4% and 0.1% of body zinc, respectively [Zinc deficiency and cellular oxidative stress: Acta Pharmacologica Sinica, 2018].

**Mechanisms in CVD Pathophysiology:**

Zinc protects against oxidative stress through multiple indirect mechanisms, including competing with redox-active metals (iron, copper) for binding sites, stabilizing sulfhydryl groups, accelerating metallothionein synthesis, and serving as a cofactor in copper-zinc superoxide dismutase (SOD1) [Zinc deficiency and cellular oxidative stress: Acta Pharmacologica Sinica, 2018]. Zinc deficiency leads to increased activation of NF-κB and inflammatory cytokine expression (IL-1, IL-6, TNF-α, IFN-γ), while zinc supplementation suppresses NF-κB activation [Zinc deficiency and cellular oxidative stress: Acta Pharmacologica Sinica, 2018].

Zinc deficiency contributes to endothelial dysfunction through disruption of eNOS and Nrf2/HO-1 signaling, leading to impaired nitric oxide bioavailability [Role of zinc in atherosclerosis: J Xiangya Medicine, 2025]. Intracellular zinc homeostasis is regulated by 24 zinc transporters found within human heart muscle tissue, including ZIP transporters (zinc influx) and ZnT transporters (zinc efflux), along with metallothioneins [Zinc in Cardiovascular Functions and Diseases: IJMS, 2023].

**Cardiovascular Consequences:**

Both zinc deficiency and zinc overload can accelerate atherosclerosis. Zinc deficiency affects 17% of the global population, up to 35% in low-income populations [Zinc as a Biomarker of Cardiovascular Health: Frontiers in Nutrition, 2021]. Lower serum zinc levels are associated with higher CVD risk, particularly in vulnerable populations including diabetes patients and those undergoing coronary angiography [Zinc as a Biomarker of Cardiovascular Health: Frontiers in Nutrition, 2021]. The National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) found that zinc intake at recommended levels (OR = 0.82; 95% CI: 0.69–0.98) was associated with an 18% lower risk of ASCVD over 10 years [Role of zinc in atherosclerosis: J Xiangya Medicine, 2025].

A 2025 study from the University of St Andrews found that low zinc was associated with a higher risk of developing major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE) and increased risks of various circulatory diseases [Abnormal plasma/serum magnesium, copper and zinc concentrations: Nutrients, 2025]. Serum zinc levels fall significantly after myocardial infarction, with prognostic implications, and there is an inverse association between plasma zinc concentrations and postoperative atrial fibrillation [Zinc deficiency and cellular oxidative stress: Acta Pharmacologica Sinica, 2018].

### 2.2 Copper (Cu)

Copper is an essential trace element obtained from diet (vegetables, shellfish, meat, seeds, nuts), existing in two ionic forms: Cu⁺ (cuprous) and Cu²⁺. Copper metabolism involves absorption via CTR1, transportation via chaperone proteins (COX17, CCS, ATOX1, ATP7A, ATP7B), and excretion [Copper Ions: The Invisible Killer of Cardiovascular Disease: Molecular Medicine Reports, 2024].

**Mechanisms in CVD Pathophysiology:**

Excess copper cycles through oxidation/reduction generating hydroxyl radicals via Fenton chemistry, causing DNA damage, lipid peroxidation, and GSH oxidation. Copper-mediated Fenton reactions disrupt the TCA cycle, inducing proteotoxic stress and cell death [Copper Ions: The Invisible Killer of Cardiovascular Disease: Molecular Medicine Reports, 2024].

The copper-zinc superoxide dismutase system (SOD1) catalyzes the conversion of superoxide anion to hydrogen peroxide and dioxygen. The copper ion acts as an electron transfer center, while the zinc ion serves as a structural scaffold [Cu/Zn Superoxide Dismutase (SOD1): Chemistry LibreTexts]. SOD1 knockout causes hypercholesterolemia and hypotriglyceridemia with irregular lipid accumulation in liver, heart, kidney, muscle, and testis [Impact of SOD1 Knockout on Lipid Metabolism in Mice: Cornell eCommons, 2009].

Dysregulation of copper ions can disturb lipid metabolism, resulting in oxidative stress, mitochondrial damage, endothelial cell dysfunction, and induce atherosclerosis, arrhythmia, and cardiomyopathy [Copper Ions: The Invisible Killer of Cardiovascular Disease: Molecular Medicine Reports, 2024]. Copper deficiency reduces cytochrome c oxidase (CCO) activity and mitochondrial function, leading to cardiomyocyte stiffening and fatal CVD. Copper excess alters energy metabolism by binding to thioctylated proteins in the TCA cycle [Copper Ions: The Invisible Killer of Cardiovascular Disease: Molecular Medicine Reports, 2024].

**Epidemiological Evidence:**

A systematic review and meta-analysis of 16 observational studies (41,322 participants) found that high serum copper levels were associated with increased risk of total stroke (pOR: 1.49, 95% CI 1.22–1.82), myocardial infarction (pOR: 1.31, 95% CI 1.17–1.46), and cardiovascular mortality (pOR: 1.60, 95% CI 1.39–1.86) [Serum copper levels and risk of MACE: Frontiers in Cardiovascular Medicine, 2023]. The St Andrews study (2025) confirmed that high copper was associated with a higher risk of developing MACE, while low copper was specifically associated with heightened risk of venous and lymphatic disease [Abnormal plasma/serum magnesium, copper and zinc concentrations: Nutrients, 2025].

**Cuproptosis:**

In 2022, Tsvetkov et al. molecularly defined cuproptosis—a novel form of programmed cell death mediated by copper ions. Cuproptosis arises from Cu⁺ binding to lipoylated TCA cycle enzymes, triggering protein oligomerization, Fe-S cluster destabilization, and proteotoxic stress that culminates in cell death [Cuproptosis and CVD: PMC/Reviews in Cardiovascular Medicine, 2025]. This discovery has opened new therapeutic avenues for copper-related cardiovascular pathology.

### 2.3 Selenium (Se)

Selenium is a vital trace element that plays its biological functions through 25 selenoproteins in humans, incorporating selenium as the redox-active amino acid selenocysteine (Sec), the 21st naturally occurring amino acid encoded by the UGA codon [Selenium, a Micronutrient That Modulates Cardiovascular Health via Redox Enzymology: Nutrients, 2021].

**Selenoproteins and Cardiovascular Function:**

Key selenoproteins involved in cardiovascular function include:

- **Glutathione Peroxidases (GPx1, GPx3, GPx4):** GPx1 detoxifies intracellular H₂O₂ and protects against ischemia-reperfusion injury, homocysteine-induced endothelial dysfunction, and doxorubicin cardiotoxicity. GPx1 activity was a strong predictor of future cardiovascular events, with a hazard ratio of 0.29 (95% CI, 0.15–0.58; p < 0.001) for the lowest tertile compared to the highest tertile [Selenium, a Micronutrient That Modulates Cardiovascular Health via Redox Enzymology: Nutrients, 2021]. GPx3 scavenges ROS in extracellular spaces and regulates platelet homeostasis and thrombosis. GPx4 protects cellular lipids from oxidative damage and protects against ferroptosis and atherogenesis [Selenoproteins and Cardiovascular Stress: Thrombosis and Haemostasis, 2015].

- **Thioredoxin Reductases (Txnrd1, Txnrd2):** Txnrd2 is essential for cardiac development; its knockout causes fatal dilated cardiomyopathy in mice. Mutations in the human TXNRD2 gene result in dilated cardiomyopathy [Selenium, a Micronutrient That Modulates Cardiovascular Health via Redox Enzymology: Nutrients, 2021].

- **Selenoprotein P:** Transports selenium to tissues; can impair angiogenesis by attenuating VEGF signaling [Selenium, a Micronutrient That Modulates Cardiovascular Health via Redox Enzymology: Nutrients, 2021].

**Epidemiological Evidence:**

A systematic review and meta-analysis of 49 studies (61 cohorts) found that selenium levels were significantly lower in patients with heart failure (SMD = −0.98, 95% CI: −1.34 to −0.62), myocardial infarction (SMD = −3.46, 95% CI: −4.43 to −2.85), and coronary heart disease (SMD = −0.47, 95% CI: −0.64 to −0.28) compared to healthy controls [Selenium concentration is associated with CVD: J Trace Elem Med Biol, 2022]. A 50% increase in selenium levels corresponded to a 24% reduction in coronary incident risk [Selenium as a Modulator of Redox Reactions in CVD: Antioxidants, 2024].

However, excess selenium (>900 μg/day) can be toxic. NHANES data (2011-2016, n=6,683) showed serum Se ≥120 μg/L was associated with hypertension (OR=1.46), and levels >150 μg/L strengthened the association (OR=1.69) [Selenium as a Modulator of Redox Reactions in CVD: Antioxidants, 2024]. A U-shaped association exists between serum selenium and mortality in hypertensive patients, with lowest mortality at 136 μg/L [Selenium as a Modulator of Redox Reactions in CVD: Antioxidants, 2024].

### 2.4 Magnesium (Mg)

A 70 kg human contains 20-24 g of magnesium (60% in bones, 35% in soft tissues, only 1% in blood). The current RDA is 420 mg daily for males and 320 mg daily for females above 31 years. The modern Western diet provides only 200-300 mg/day, down from 410 mg at the beginning of the 20th century [Magnesium and cardiovascular system: Shechter, Cardiacos.net]. It is estimated that 25% or more of the United States population experiences chronic, latent Mg²⁺ depletion [Magnesium in hypertension: Frontiers in Physiology, 2024].

**Mechanisms in CVD Pathophysiology:**

Magnesium is considered nature's physiologic calcium blocker. Five key mechanisms by which Mg²⁺ influences blood pressure and cardiovascular health include: (1) **Vascular tone**: Mg²⁺ reduces vascular smooth muscle contraction by blocking L-type Ca²⁺ channels, supporting prostacyclin and nitric oxide release, and promoting vasodilation; (2) **Sympathetic tone**: Mg²⁺ attenuates sympathetic nervous system activity by modulating NMDA receptor activity and blocking N-type Ca²⁺ channels; (3) **Renal K⁺ and Na⁺ handling**: Mg²⁺ depletion promotes urinary K⁺ wasting and influences aldosterone secretion; (4) **Renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system**: Mg²⁺ directly reduces aldosterone secretion; (5) **Pro-hypertensive inflammation**: Mg²⁺ depletion activates NLRP3 inflammasome and stimulates isolevuglandin production [Magnesium in hypertension: Frontiers in Physiology, 2024].

**Cardiovascular Consequences:**

Higher circulating magnesium levels are associated with a 30% lower risk of cardiovascular disease events [Del Gobbo et al., 2013]. Abnormal magnesium levels were associated with a higher risk of developing MACE, and low magnesium was associated with increased risks of various circulatory diseases [Abnormal plasma/serum magnesium, copper and zinc concentrations: Nutrients, 2025]. Hypomagnesemia is common in hospitalized patients, especially elderly with CAD and/or chronic heart failure, and is associated with increased incidence of diabetes, metabolic syndrome, and mortality from CAD [Magnesium and cardiovascular system: Shechter, Cardiacos.net].

### 2.5 Iron (Fe)

Iron is essential for oxygen transport via hemoglobin and myoglobin. The recommended daily intake is 18 mg/day for women and 10 mg/day for men. Iron deficiency is the most prevalent malnutrition-related condition and affects up to 75% of patients with heart failure [The molecular and metabolic landscape of iron and ferroptosis in CVD: Nature Reviews Cardiology, 2023].

**Mechanisms in CVD Pathophysiology:**

Free Fe²⁺ reacts with hydrogen peroxide via the Fenton reaction to generate lipid peroxides, driving oxidative damage [An emerging double-edged sword role of ferroptosis in CVD: IJMM, 2024]. Iron uptake in cardiomyocytes depends on TFR1-mediated endocytosis of transferrin-bound iron, while iron export is mediated by ferroportin (the only known iron exporter). The hepcidin-ferroportin axis is crucial for cardiac iron homeostasis [The molecular and metabolic landscape of iron and ferroptosis in CVD: Nature Reviews Cardiology, 2023].

**Ferroptosis:**

Ferroptosis is a recently identified form of regulated cell death, distinct from apoptosis, necroptosis, pyroptosis, and autophagy-dependent cell death. It is characterized by iron-dependent phospholipid peroxidation [Ferroptosis: a cell death connecting oxidative stress, inflammation and CVD: Cell Death Discovery, 2021]. Three essential features define ferroptosis: dysfunction of lipid peroxide clearance, presence of redox-active iron, and oxidation of polyunsaturated fatty acid-containing phospholipids. The core regulatory pathway involves the cystine/glutamate antiporter (system Xc⁻), glutathione (GSH), and glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4). Depletion of GSH or inactivation of GPX4 leads to accumulation of lipid peroxides and ferroptosis [Ferroptosis: a cell death connecting oxidative stress, inflammation and CVD: Cell Death Discovery, 2021].

**Cardiovascular Consequences:**

Ferroptosis contributes to endothelial dysfunction, chronic inflammation, and foam cell formation in atherosclerosis. Inhibition of ferroptosis (e.g., by ferrostatin-1) alleviates atherosclerosis in mouse models [Ferroptosis: a cell death connecting oxidative stress, inflammation and CVD: Cell Death Discovery, 2021]. In myocardial ischemia/reperfusion injury, ferroptosis occurs mainly during reperfusion rather than ischemia, with GPX4 levels decreasing during MI [The Emerging Role of Ferroptosis in CVD: Frontiers in Pharmacology, 2022]. Iron overload disorders—including hereditary haemochromatosis, Friedreich ataxia, thalassaemia, and anthracycline-induced cardiotoxicity—are all associated with cardiomyopathy and heart failure [The molecular and metabolic landscape of iron and ferroptosis in CVD: Nature Reviews Cardiology, 2023].

### 2.6 Calcium (Ca)

Required intake of calcium is 1,200-1,500 mg/day [The Impact of Micronutrients: Frontiers in Physiology, 2021]. Vascular calcification is associated with aging, cardiovascular and renal diseases and results in poor morbidity and increased mortality.

**Mechanisms in CVD Pathophysiology:**

Vascular calcification is a complex, active, cell-mediated process involving transdifferentiation of vascular smooth muscle cells (VSMCs) to a bone-like phenotype, systemic inflammation, decreased anti-calcific events, and enhanced pro-calcific microRNAs [Vascular Calcification Mechanisms: Biomedicines, 2021]. High serum phosphate plays a central role in promoting vascular calcification through oxidative stress, oxidative DNA damage, cellular senescence, and increased intracellular calcium levels [Vascular Calcification Mechanisms: Biomedicines, 2021].

Approximately 98% of osteochondrogenic cells expressing Runx2 in atherosclerotic plaques are derived from VSMCs. VSMC-derived cells constitute a substantial proportion (30%–70%) of all plaque cells [Roles of VSMCs in Atherosclerotic Calcification: J Lipid Atherosclerosis, 2023]. Microcalcifications and spotty calcifications are associated with plaque instability, while macrocalcifications may stabilize plaques.

### 2.7 Toxic Metals: Cadmium (Cd)

Cadmium is a toxic heavy metal with a biological half-life of 10-30 years, accumulating mainly in kidneys, liver, and bones. Tobacco smoke is considered the most significant source of cadmium exposure; smokers have approximately twice the cadmium body burden of nonsmokers [Cadmium and cardiovascular disease: Rev Port Cardiol, 2021].

**Mechanisms and Evidence:**

Cd causes damage by inducing lipid peroxidation, disturbing antioxidant systems, disrupting endothelial integrity, promoting inflammation (via IL-6, IL-8, IL-1β, TNF-α), and stimulating smooth muscle cell proliferation, thereby accelerating atherosclerosis [Cadmium and cardiovascular disease: Rev Port Cardiol, 2021]. The pooled relative risks for CVD, coronary heart disease, stroke, and peripheral arterial disease due to cadmium exposure were 1.36 (95% CI: 1.11-1.66), 1.30 (95% CI: 1.12-1.52), 1.18 (95% CI: 0.86-1.59), and 1.49 (95% CI: 1.15-1.92), respectively [Cadmium and cardiovascular disease: Rev Port Cardiol, 2021].

In mixture effects studies, cadmium had the highest weight among metals in CVD models (weighted at 0.78), followed by mercury at 0.197 [Mixture effects of trace element levels on CVD and T2D: Scientific Reports, 2024]. At the AHA Scientific Sessions 2025, researchers found that among five toxic metals, cadmium exposure is the most significant driver of elevated CVD risk, contributing most to stroke risk (67.6%) and CHF risk (63.5%) [Cadmium Is a Major Contributor to Toxic Metal-Associated Risk for CVD: AHA 2025].

## 3. Therapeutic Interventions Studied to Modulate Metal Ion Concentrations

### 3.1 Supplementation-Based Interventions

#### 3.1.1 Zinc Supplementation

**Clinical Evidence:**

A GRADE-assessed systematic review and dose-response meta-analysis of 75 RCTs (2023) found that zinc supplementation significantly reduced triglycerides, total cholesterol, fasting blood glucose, HbA1c, HOMA-IR, C-reactive protein, interleukin-6, tumor necrosis factor-α, nitric oxide, and malondialdehyde. It also increased total antioxidant capacity and glutathione. However, no significant effects were found on LDL, HDL, insulin, systolic blood pressure, or diastolic blood pressure. The hypolipidemic effects were demonstrated primarily in patients with dyslipidemia (TG > 150 mg/dL; TC > 200 mg/dL) [Zinc supplementation and CVD risk factors: Journal of Trace Elements in Medicine and Biology, 2023].

A 2020 meta-analysis of 20 RCTs (n=1,141) showed zinc supplementation significantly decreased plasma triglyceride (SMD -0.66), VLDL (SMD -1.59), total cholesterol (SMD -0.65), fasting blood glucose (SMD -0.52), and HbA1c (SMD -0.64), but effects on blood pressure and anthropometric indices were not statistically significant [Effects of Zinc Supplementation on Cardiometabolic Risk Factors: Biol Trace Elem Res, 2020].

However, a 2010 meta-analysis of 33 interventions (n=14,238) found that among healthy individuals, zinc supplementation was associated with a significant 7% decrease in HDL cholesterol concentrations (-0.10 mmol/L, p<0.001), suggesting zinc supplementation may increase coronary heart disease risk in healthy people [Effects of zinc on plasma lipoprotein cholesterol concentrations: Atherosclerosis, 2010].

**Key Findings:**

- Long-duration (>12 weeks) and low-dose (<25 mg/day) supplementation was found to be more beneficial than short-duration and high-dose interventions [Zinc in Cardiovascular Functions and Diseases: IJMS, 2023; Zinc as a Biomarker of Cardiovascular Health: Frontiers in Nutrition, 2021]
- A 2025 RCT in HIV patients (n=95) found that 90 mg elemental zinc daily for 24 weeks increased zinc levels by 53% and decreased monocyte activation marker sCD14, but showed no significant changes in other inflammatory markers or metabolic/cardiovascular markers [Zinc Supplementation, Inflammation, and Gut Integrity Markers in HIV: Nutrients, 2025]
- NHANES data in diabetes patients (n=3,053) showed higher dietary zinc intake was associated with lower 10-year ASCVD risk (OR=0.77, 95% CI: 0.61–0.99), with stronger protective associations in patients with hypertension and dyslipidemia [Dietary zinc intake and ASCVD risk in diabetes: Thrombosis Journal, 2025]

#### 3.1.2 Selenium Supplementation

**Major Clinical Trials:**

The **SELECT Trial** (Selenium and Vitamin E Cancer Prevention Trial) was a large-scale, double-blind, placebo-controlled RCT of over 35,000 men assessing whether selenium (200 μg/day L-selenomethionine) and vitamin E (400 IU/day) could prevent prostate cancer. The trial was stopped early in 2008 because no protective benefit was found. Neither selenium nor vitamin E reduced the incidence of prostate cancer after 7 years, and vitamin E was associated with a 17% increased risk of prostate cancer (HR 1.17, 99% CI 1.004-1.36, P = .008). No differences were found in cardiovascular events or total deaths between groups [SELECT Trial: Wikipedia; Selenium and Vitamin E Cancer Prevention Trial: NCI].

The **NPC Trial** (Nutritional Prevention of Cancer Trial, 1983-1996, n=1,004) in low-selenium areas of the eastern US found that selenium supplementation (200 μg/day) was not significantly associated with any CVD endpoint: all CVD (HR=1.03, 95% CI: 0.78-1.37), myocardial infarction (HR=0.94, 95% CI: 0.61-1.44), stroke (HR=1.02, 95% CI: 0.63-1.65), and all CVD mortality (HR=1.22, 95% CI: 0.76-1.95). The lack of association persisted when stratified by baseline plasma selenium concentrations [Effects of Selenium Supplementation on CVD: Am J Epidemiology, 2006].

The **Cochrane Systematic Review** (2013, 12 RCTs, n=19,715) found no statistically significant effects of selenium supplementation on all-cause mortality (RR 0.97, 95% CI 0.88-1.08), CVD mortality (RR 0.97, 95% CI 0.79-1.2), non-fatal CVD events (RR 0.96, 95% CI 0.89-1.04), or all CVD events (RR 1.03, 95% CI 0.95-1.11). There was a small, non-significant increased risk of type 2 diabetes (RR 1.06, 95% CI 0.97-1.15). Selenium significantly increased alopecia (RR 1.28, 95% CI 1.01-1.62) and dermatitis (RR 1.17, 95% CI 1.0-1.35) [Selenium supplements for the prevention of CVD: Cochrane, 2013].

**Key Findings:**

- The limited trial evidence does not support the use of selenium supplements in the primary prevention of CVD
- The association between selenium and cardio-metabolic outcomes is likely to be U-shaped
- A 2025 NHANES study (n=39,372) found that as dietary selenium intake increased, CVD risk decreased to a threshold (inflection point at 135.28 μg/d), beyond which the protective effect weakened and higher intake could increase risk [Association between dietary selenium intake and CVD risk: Scientific Reports, 2025]
- The **SELEQT-HF Trial** (NCT07234422) is actively recruiting to evaluate selenium and CoQ10 in heart failure (Phase 3, n=1,100, estimated completion 2030) [SELEQT-HF Trial: CenterWatch]

#### 3.1.3 Magnesium Supplementation

**Blood Pressure:**

A 2025 meta-analysis of 38 RCTs (2,709 participants) found that magnesium supplementation (median dose 365 mg/day, median duration 12 weeks) reduced systolic BP by −2.81 mm Hg (95% CI, −4.32 to −1.29) and diastolic BP by −2.05 mm Hg (95% CI, −3.23 to −0.88) compared to placebo. The most significant reductions occurred in hypertensive individuals on BP-lowering medication (SBP −7.68 mm Hg; DBP −2.96 mm Hg) and those with hypomagnesemia (SBP −5.97 mm Hg; DBP −4.75 mm Hg). No significant BP reductions were observed in normotensive participants [Magnesium Supplementation and Blood Pressure: Hypertension, 2025].

A 2011 review noted that magnesium intake of 500 mg/d to 1000 mg/d may reduce blood pressure as much as 5.6/2.8 mm Hg, and that the combination of increased intake of magnesium and potassium coupled with reduced sodium intake is more effective than single mineral intake and is often as effective as one antihypertensive drug [The Role of Magnesium in Hypertension and CVD: J Clin Hypertension, 2011].

**Heart Failure:**

A 2025 VA study of nearly 20,000 Veterans newly diagnosed with heart failure found that taking OTC magnesium supplements was associated with a 19% reduced risk of hospitalization or death, with benefits especially pronounced for those aged 80+ and those with sustained magnesium use [Magnesium supplements improve HF outcomes: VA Research News Briefs, 2025].

A target trial-emulation study of 94,239 U.S. military veterans with diabetes but no prior HF found that magnesium supplement use was associated with reduced risk of incident HF (HR, 0.94 [95% CI, 0.89-0.99]) and reduced risk of major adverse cardiac events (HR, 0.94 [95% CI, 0.90-0.97]) [OTC Magnesium Supplements and HF: U.S. Medicine/JAHA, 2025].

**Acute Myocardial Infarction:**

The LIMIT-2 trial (2,316 patients) showed 24% mortality reduction at 28 days and 20% reduction in ischemic heart disease mortality over 4.5 years. However, the larger ISIS-4 (58,000 patients) and MAGIC (6,213 patients) trials found no survival benefit from IV magnesium. Magnesium is not recommended for routine use in all AMI patients but may benefit high-risk groups such as the elderly, CHF patients, and those ineligible for reperfusion therapy [Magnesium and cardiovascular system: Shechter, Cardiacos.net].

**Other Effects:**

Oral magnesium (730 mg/day for 6 months) significantly improved endothelial function, exercise duration, and reduced exercise-induced chest pain and arrhythmias. A 3-month oral magnesium supplementation (800-1,200 mg/day) significantly reduced median platelet-dependent thrombosis by 35% compared to placebo in stable CAD patients on aspirin therapy [Magnesium and cardiovascular system: Shechter, Cardiacos.net].

#### 3.1.4 Calcium Supplementation

**Controversial Evidence:**

Calcium supplementation has been one of the most controversial areas of metal modulation for CVD. A 2021 meta-analysis of 13 RCTs (n=28,935) found that calcium supplements significantly increased the risk of CVD by 15% (RR 1.15, 95% CI 1.06–1.25) and coronary heart disease by 16% (RR 1.16, 95% CI 1.05–1.28), specifically in healthy postmenopausal women. The absolute risk difference was 8.6 per 1000 persons for CVD and 8.8 per 1000 persons for CHD [Calcium Supplements and Risk of CVD: Nutrients, 2021].

However, a 2023 meta-analysis of 11 RCTs (n=55,438) found that calcium supplements (alone or with vitamin D) were not associated with any significant hazard for CHD, stroke, or all-cause mortality, and excluded excess risks above 0.3%–0.5% per year for CHD or stroke. For calcium alone (mean daily dose 1g): MI (RR, 1.15; 95% CI: 0.88, 1.51), CHD death (RR, 1.24; 95% CI: 0.89, 1.73), any CHD (RR, 1.01; 95% CI: 0.75, 1.37), stroke (RR, 1.15; 95% CI, 0.90, 1.46) [Calcium Supplements and Risk of CVD: Curr Dev Nutr, 2023].

The JACC Focus Seminar (2021) concluded that calcium intake from food sources did not increase CVD risk, but calcium supplements might increase CVD risk, particularly for MI. The article recommends that most adults should try to meet the NAM-recommended daily allowance from food sources and avoid using supplements for cardiovascular health promotion [Vitamin D, Calcium Supplements, and Implications for Cardiovascular Health: JACC, 2021].

The USPSTF (2022) found no significant benefit for calcium (without vitamin D) on mortality, CVD, or cancer: all-cause mortality OR, 1.05 (95% CI, 0.92-1.21); CVD events OR, 1.11 (95% CI, 0.90-1.36) [USPSTF Evidence Report: JAMA, 2022].

#### 3.1.5 Iron Supplementation

**Intravenous Iron in Heart Failure:**

This represents the most clinically advanced metal-based intervention for CVD. Iron deficiency affects >50% of chronic HF patients and up to 80% of acute HF patients [Recent Trials of Iron Deficiency in HF: Know ID in HF].

The **FAIR-HF** and **CONFIRM-HF** trials showed statistically significant benefits of IV ferric carboxymaltose (FCM) vs. placebo on symptoms, functional capacity, quality of life, and NYHA class in both anemic and non-anemic HF patients. The **AFFIRM-AHF** trial (2020) showed IV FCM reduced HF hospitalizations but not CV death in acute HF patients [Recent Trials of Iron Deficiency in HF: Know ID in HF].

The **IRONMAN** trial (2022, n=1,137) of ferric derisomaltose vs. usual care in HF patients with iron deficiency found that the primary outcome (CV death or HF hospitalization) occurred in 22.4 vs. 27.5 events per 100 patient-years (p=0.07), indicating ferric derisomaltose was not statistically superior to usual care. However, sensitivity analysis censoring at September 2020 due to COVID-19 showed a relative risk of 0.76 (95% CI 0.58-1.00, p=0.047). Unplanned hospitalizations were fewer in the IV iron group (RR 0.83, 95% CI 0.71-0.97; p=0.02) [IRONMAN Trial: ACC, 2022].

The **HEART-FID** trial (2023) found that FCM did not meet the primary endpoint (all-cause mortality, HHF, 6MWD) at 99% CI, though P=0.02 suggested possible treatment effect. A meta-analysis of 4,501 patients found that FCM significantly reduced total CV hospitalizations or CV death, with greatest benefit in patients with TSAT <15% [Recent Trials of Iron Deficiency in HF: Know ID in HF].

**Regulatory Status:**

The ESC 2023 Focused Update gave IV iron a Class I, Level A recommendation for symptom alleviation and QoL improvement in HFrEF/HFmrEF, and Class IIa, Level A for reducing HF hospitalization. The ACC/AHA/HFSA 2022 guidelines gave IV iron a Class 2a, Level B-R recommendation for functional status and QoL improvement in HFrEF with iron deficiency. The FDA granted the first HF indication for IV iron (ferric carboxymaltose) in June 2023 [Recent Trials of Iron Deficiency in HF: Know ID in HF].

**Oral Iron:**

Oral iron has shown mixed or negative results in HF trials. The IRON-OUT trial demonstrated no improvement in exercise capacity, QoL, or iron stores. Oral iron is poorly absorbed in HF patients and associated with GI side effects. A 2020 AHA survey found 58% of participants treat ID in HF, but 35% use oral iron (ineffective and not recommended) [Recent Trials of Iron Deficiency in HF: Know ID in HF].

### 3.2 Chelation Therapy

#### 3.2.1 EDTA Chelation

**TACT (Trial to Assess Chelation Therapy):**

This was the first large-scale, multicenter, double-blind, placebo-controlled, 2×2 factorial study of EDTA chelation therapy for CHD, co-sponsored by NHLBI and NCCIH. It enrolled 1,708 participants aged 50+ with prior heart attacks across 134 sites in the US and Canada. Participants received 40 IV infusions of either a 500-mL EDTA chelation solution (containing 3g disodium EDTA, 7g ascorbate, B vitamins, electrolytes, procaine, and heparin) or placebo [Trial to Assess Chelation Therapy (TACT): ACC, 2013].

The primary composite endpoint (death, MI, stroke, coronary revascularization, hospitalization for angina at 5 years) was significantly lower in the chelation arm (26.5% vs. 30%; HR 0.82, 95% CI 0.69-0.99, p=0.035), an 18% reduced risk of subsequent cardiac events. However, this was driven predominantly by reduced coronary revascularization. No significant effect was found on total mortality (chelation: 10% vs. placebo: 11%; HR 0.93, 95% CI 0.70-1.25, P=0.64). Hypocalcemia was higher in the chelation arm (6.2% vs. 3.5%, p=0.008) [Trial to Assess Chelation Therapy (TACT): ACC, 2013].

In the diabetes subset (37% of participants, n=633), chelation showed a lower risk of cardiovascular events (25% vs. 38% in placebo) and a 43% lower all-cause mortality (HR 0.61, p=0.002). High-dose vitamins vs. placebo showed no significant benefit (34.2% vs. 37%; HR 0.89, p=0.21) [Trial to Assess Chelation Therapy (TACT): ACC, 2013].

**TACT2:**

This follow-up study specifically in patients with diabetes and prior MI (n=959, median age 67, 27% female) was designed to confirm the apparent benefit seen in TACT. **TACT2 did NOT reproduce the TACT results.** The primary outcome occurred in 35.6% of the EDTA group vs. 35.7% of the placebo group (HR 0.93, 95% CI 0.76-1.16, p=0.53). Secondary outcomes (cardiovascular death, MI, or stroke: 18.4% vs. 19.7%; all-cause death: 17.4% vs. 17.6%) all showed no significant benefit. EDTA did significantly reduce blood lead levels (-5.5 vs. -0.6 µg/L, p<0.001) [Trial to Assess Chelation Therapy 2 (TACT2): ClinicalTrials.gov NCT02733185, 2024].

**Regulatory and Guideline Status:**

- EDTA chelation is NOT FDA-approved for prevention or treatment of cardiovascular disease
- The ACC 2023 guideline for chronic coronary disease states that EDTA chelation is not approved by the FDA for prevention or treatment of cardiovascular disease and does not recommend its use outside of research settings
- The ACC/AHA (2014) stated chelation therapy's usefulness is uncertain for reducing cardiovascular events in stable IHD
- AHA (2016) stated it is not beneficial for claudication
- A Cochrane review found no difference between chelation therapy and placebo with regard to all-cause mortality, cardiovascular death, myocardial infarction, angina, or coronary revascularization
- Risks include hypocalcemia (potentially serious), infusion site reaction, fever, nausea, vomiting, kidney damage, delay of more effective treatment, and death [Chelation Therapy: Merck Manual Professional Edition; Chelation Therapy for Off-Label Uses: Healthy Blue Medical Policy]

#### 3.2.2 Iron Chelation in Iron Overload Disorders

Iron chelation therapy is well-established for transfusion-dependent thalassemia and hereditary hemochromatosis. The MIOT Network study (Haematologica, 2011) compared deferasirox, deferiprone, and desferrioxamine in thalassemia major patients. Global heart T2* value was significantly higher in the deferiprone group (34±11ms) than in deferasirox (21±12ms) and desferrioxamine groups (27±11ms) (P=0.0001), indicating less myocardial iron burden. LVEF was higher in the deferiprone and desferrioxamine groups vs. deferasirox group (P=0.010). Deferiprone appears to be a better agent for removing/preventing myocardial siderosis, while desferrioxamine is more effective in removing hepatocellular iron [Deferasirox, deferiprone and desferrioxamine treatment: Haematologica, 2011].

A randomized trial of deferiprone vs. desferrioxamine in 61 thalassemia patients with asymptomatic myocardial siderosis showed deferiprone (92 mg/kg/d) had significantly greater improvement in cardiac T2* MRI and LVEF compared to DFO over 12 months [Oral chelators for transfusional iron overload: PMC/Neufeld, 2006].

Combined chelation therapy (deferoxamine + deferiprone) in thalassemia major patients with established cardiac disease was superior to deferoxamine monotherapy. No cardiac events were observed among high-ferritin patients on combination therapy, whereas 4 cardiac events (p=0.0049), including three deaths, occurred in high-ferritin patients on DFO monotherapy [Intensive Combined Chelation Therapy: CTV NCT00800761].

#### 3.2.3 Copper Chelation

Copper chelators—including tetrathiomolybdate (TTM), trientine, and triethylenetetramine—have been investigated for cardiovascular indications. Tetrathiomolybdate has been studied in idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (Phase 1/2, NCT00189176) and is covered by a patent (US6855340B2) for treating inflammatory and fibrotic diseases including myocardial fibrosis. The patent describes TM as a unique anticopper drug that forms a stable tripartite complex with protein and copper, rendering copper unavailable and facilitating its clearance from the body [Safety Study of Tetrathiomolybdate in IPF: CenterWatch NCT00189176; US6855340B2: Google Patents].

A 2025 preclinical study (Wang et al., Fudan University) found that TTM treatment significantly attenuated fibrotic progression, reduced oxidative stress, and suppressed Olig-DLAT accumulation in a bleomycin-induced pulmonary fibrosis model [Tetrathiomolybdate alleviates bleomycin-induced pulmonary fibrosis: Eur J Med Res, 2025].

### 3.3 Iron Reduction through Phlebotomy

**FeAST Study (Iron and Atherosclerosis Study, 2007, n=1,277):** This multicenter, single-blind, RCT investigated whether reducing body iron stores through phlebotomy (every 6 months) lowers cardiovascular risk in patients with peripheral artery disease. Results showed no significant differences between groups. All-cause death: 23% control vs. 20% iron-reduction (HR 0.85, 95% CI 0.67–1.08, P=0.17). Death plus nonfatal MI and stroke: 32% control vs. 28% iron-reduction (HR 0.88, 95% CI 0.72–1.07, P=0.20). A significant benefit was observed in the youngest patients (age 43–61), with improved all-cause mortality (HR 0.47, P=0.02) [Reduction Of Body Iron Stores And Cardiovascular Outcomes: ScienceDaily/JAMA, 2007].

A 2012 randomized trial of phlebotomy in 64 patients with metabolic syndrome found that systolic BP decreased significantly from 148.5 to 130.5 mmHg in the phlebotomy group vs. 144.7 to 143.8 mmHg in controls (difference -16.6 mmHg, p<0.001), with significant reductions in blood glucose, HbA1c, LDL/HDL ratio, and heart rate [Effects of phlebotomy-induced reduction of body iron stores on metabolic syndrome: BMC Medicine, 2012].

### 3.4 Dietary Modifications

**Mediterranean Diet:**

A 2025 meta-analysis of 14 observational studies (n=424,502) found that high adherence to the Mediterranean diet was significantly associated with a reduced risk of incident heart failure (OR = 0.77, 95% CI: 0.63–0.93, p = 0.007) and lower all-cause mortality in patients with HF (OR = 0.88, 95% CI: 0.78–0.99, p = 0.03) [Mediterranean and DASH Diet Patterns and Heart Failure: Life/MDPI, 2025].

The landmark PREDIMED trial—halted early due to overwhelming benefit—showed approximately a 30% relative cardiovascular risk reduction with the Mediterranean diet supplemented with olive oil or nuts. The primary advantage of the Mediterranean diet appears to lie in its synergy among various cardio-protective nutrients and foods; no specific component has been shown to be as beneficial as the whole [The Mediterranean Diet, its Components, and Cardiovascular Disease: PMC/Mayo Clinic, 2015].

**DASH Diet:**

The same 2025 meta-analysis found that high adherence to the DASH diet was significantly associated with a reduced risk of incident HF (OR = 0.83, 95% CI: 0.70–0.98, p = 0.03), but showed no significant association with all-cause mortality in HF patients (OR = 0.89, 95% CI: 0.75–1.05, p = 0.15) [Mediterranean and DASH Diet Patterns and Heart Failure: Life/MDPI, 2025].

A clinical trial (NCT05669404) is currently comparing salt restriction, DASH diet + salt restriction, and Mediterranean diet + salt restriction on BP levels in adults with high normal BP or grade 1 hypertension [Effects of the DASH vs. the Mediterranean Diet Combined With Salt Restriction: ClinicalTrials.gov NCT05669404].

### 3.5 General Principles from Supplementation Trials

The USPSTF (2022, 84 studies, n=739,803) concluded that vitamin and mineral supplementation was associated with little or no benefit in preventing cancer, cardiovascular disease, and death, with the exception of a small benefit for cancer incidence with multivitamin use (OR, 0.93; 95% CI, 0.87-0.99). Beta carotene was associated with an increased risk of lung cancer and other harmful outcomes [USPSTF Evidence Report: JAMA, 2022].

The Methodist DeBakey Cardiovascular Journal review (2019) concluded that the evidence to date fails to support the use of exogenous supplements of vitamins and minerals for the prevention or treatment of cardiovascular disease in individuals without dietary deficiencies. The review noted that folic acid showed a benefit—a reduction in stroke risk by 20% and a reduction in cardiovascular disease risk—while niacin was found to increase the number of deaths [Supplemental Vitamins and Minerals for Cardiovascular Disease: Methodist DeBakey Cardiovascular J, 2019].

A Frontiers in Physiology mini-review (2021) concluded that both deficiency and excess of micronutrients (calcium, zinc, vitamin D, selenium) can harm cardiovascular health. Supplements cause rapid, sustained increases in serum levels that may be detrimental. The authors advise obtaining micronutrients from dietary sources rather than supplements until more clinical research is conducted [The Impact of Micronutrients: Frontiers in Physiology, 2021].

## 4. Emerging Therapeutic Approaches

### 4.1 Ferroptosis Inhibitors

Ferroptosis inhibition represents one of the most promising emerging therapeutic strategies for CVD. Ferroptosis is defined as an iron-catalyzed form of regulated cell death, critically dependent on phospholipid peroxidation of cellular membranes [Ferroptosis: a cell death connecting oxidative stress, inflammation and CVD: Cell Death Discovery, 2021].

**Classification of Ferroptosis Inhibitors:**

Four classes have been identified: (1) **Class 1 — Iron chelators** (e.g., deferoxamine/DFO), targeting upstream Fenton reactions; (2) **Class 2 — Radical trapping agents in the cytosolic compartment** (e.g., UAMC-3203, repurposed drugs like omeprazole, rifampicin, carvedilol); (3) **Class 3 — Lipophilic radical trapping antioxidants** (e.g., ferrostatin-1, liproxstatin-1, Vitamin E, Vitamin K, 7-dehydrocholesterol); (4) **Class 4 — Inhibitors of plasma membrane rupture** (e.g., anti-NINJ1 monoclonal antibodies, glycine) [Ferroptosis-based advanced therapies: Cell Death & Differentiation, 2024].

**Key Developments:**

- **UAMC-3203** is highlighted as a superior successor to ferrostatin-1 with improved potency, stability, and solubility, recommended as the reference compound for ferroptosis research. It has shown efficacy in mouse models of iron overload, cardiac arrest, and multiple sclerosis [Comprehensive review of ferroptosis inhibitors: Trends in Pharmacological Sciences, 2023].
- **Liproxstatins** (Lip-1, Lip-2) have nanomolar potency, with Lip-2 showing improved PK properties.
- **Dexrazoxane**, an iron chelator used clinically for cardioprotection in doxorubicin-induced cardiotoxicity, abolishes ferroptosis and suppresses lipid peroxidation.
- **CuATSM** is in clinical trials for ALS and Parkinson's disease and functions as a radical-trapping antioxidant; CuATSP is 20-fold more active.

**Clinical Status:**

No anti-ferroptotic therapy has been tested in clinical trials for cardiovascular disease. A major limitation is the lack of reliable biomarkers for ferroptosis detection in serum or tissue biopsies. The side effect profile of ferrostatins appears limited based on preclinical data and trials testing Vitamin E (in NASH) and Vitamin K2 (in kidney transplant recipients). Solid organ transplantation is proposed as an ideal clinical scenario for testing ferrostatins [Ferroptosis-based advanced therapies: Cell Death & Differentiation, 2024].

### 4.2 Cuproptosis-Targeted Therapies

Since the molecular definition of cuproptosis in 2022, there has been intense interest in developing copper-modulating therapies for CVD. Key therapeutic strategies include:

- **Copper chelators:** Tetrathiomolybdate, trientine, and triethylenetetramine for inhibiting Cu buildup
- **Small-molecule inhibitors:** DCAL50 inhibits copper chaperone proteins ATOX1 and CCS, blocking intracellular copper transport
- **Copper ion carriers (ionophores):** Elesclomol and disulfiram facilitate copper entry into cells, increasing ROS and triggering cell death
- **Copper-based nanocarriers and complexes**

A review in Trends in Pharmacological Sciences (2023) concluded that only therapies that focus on restoring optimal Cu level and homeostasis in patients with CVDs may be beneficial [Copper homeostasis and cuproptosis in CVD therapeutics: Trends in Pharmacological Sciences, 2023].

### 4.3 Metal-Based Nanoparticles

**Iron Oxide Nanoparticles (IONPs):** Used for MRI contrast enhancement, targeted drug delivery, macrophage imaging, and stem cell therapy support. Fe3O4-PFH-DiR@CS-DS NPs for vulnerable plaque treatment achieved a 49.4% decrease in the degree of vascular stenosis. Ferumoxytol (Feraheme), FDA-approved for iron deficiency anemia, is used as an MRI contrast agent providing a safer alternative to gadolinium-based agents and enabling tracking of apoptosis, inflammation, and angiogenesis post-AMI [Metal-Based Nanoparticles for Cardiovascular Diseases: IJMS/MDPI, 2024].

**Gold Nanoparticles (AuNPs):** Provide 2.7 times more contrast than iodine, have higher absorption, and longer circulation time. Can be functionalized with targeting molecules (e.g., collagen-targeting peptide CNA35-coated AuNPs for detecting myocardial scar tissue). AuNPs can enhance stem cell therapy for cardiac repair [Metal-Based Nanoparticles for Cardiovascular Diseases: IJMS/MDPI, 2024].

**Cerium Oxide Nanoparticles (CeO2 NPs):** Valued for antioxidant properties due to dual valence states (Ce3+ and Ce4+). Act as in vivo mimetics for endogenous antioxidants like superoxide dismutase. Mn-CeO2 nanospheres with SOD/CAT-like activity serve as antioxidant nanozymes [Metal-Based Nanoparticles for Cardiovascular Diseases: IJMS/MDPI, 2024].

**Metal-Organic Frameworks (MOFs):** MOFs are porous structures composed of metal ions coupled with organic ligands, offering high surface area, tunable porosity, and chemical stability. Publications on MOFs for drug delivery grew from 11 in 2010 to 618 in 2023. First MOFs as drug carriers (MIL-100 and MIL-101, 2006) demonstrated high ibuprofen loading and sustained release over 3–6 days. A 2025 review by Ge et al. in Coordination Chemistry Reviews connects the fundamental design principles of MOFs to their preclinical cardiovascular applications [Advances in Metal-Organic Frameworks for Cardiovascular Therapy: ScienceDirect, 2025].

### 4.4 Nutrigenetic and Personalized Medicine Approaches

**Selenium Nutrigenetics:**

The SELENOP rs3877899 polymorphism significantly modifies selenium response. A 2018 study found that CC homozygotes who consumed fish and mussels (~50.3 μg selenium/day) had significantly higher plasma selenoprotein P levels compared to T-allele carriers (difference: -4.68 ng/mL, 95% CI: -8.49, -0.871, p=0.018) [SELENOP rs3877899 polymorphism and selenium response: Genes & Nutrition, 2018].

The GPX1 rs1050450 (Pro198Leu) polymorphism is the most studied selenoprotein SNP. The rare T allele is associated with reduced GPx1 activity (confirmed in 7 studies) and lower plasma selenium. This association is well-established in populations with plasma Se <100 μg/L, but not in populations with high Se status (>120 μg/L) where selenoproteins are already saturated [Influence of nutrigenetics on biomarkers of selenium status: USP Repository].

The SEPP1 rs7579 polymorphism significantly modifies the association between selenium and lipid changes. A 2021 prospective cohort study (n=1,621) found that among CC genotype carriers, higher selenium was strongly associated with adverse lipid changes, while among T allele carriers, these associations were not significant or were reversed. Significant gene-environment interactions were found for TC, LDL-C, non-HDL-C, TC/HDL-C ratio, and LDL-C/HDL-C ratio. These findings warn against widespread use of selenium-fortified foods or selenium supplements, especially among rs7579 CC genotype carriers [SEPP1 rs7579 polymorphism and lipid changes: Frontiers in Nutrition, 2021].

**Iron Genes and Hemochromatosis:**

Hereditary hemochromatosis affects about 1 million people in the United States. HFE gene variants (C282Y, H63D, S65C) predict hemochromatosis risk. C282Y homozygotes have increased risk of diabetes (HR 1.72), liver disease (HR 2.22), but not heart disease (HR 1.01). The HFE rs1799945 GG genotype is associated with increased risk of arterial hypertension (OR=2.53), and meta-analysis shows the G allele increases CAD risk by 6% in Caucasians [Genetic Markers Associated with Ferroptosis in CVD: Future Pharmacology, 2025].

### 4.5 Ongoing Clinical Trials

**SELEQT-HF (NCT07234422):** This Phase 3, pragmatic, registry-based randomized controlled trial is evaluating selenium and CoQ10 supplements in 1,100 adults with chronic heart failure (NYHA II to ambulatory IV). The intervention is selenium and coenzyme Q10 added to standard HF therapy vs. placebo. Key inclusion criteria include elevated NT-proBNP levels. Data collection leverages existing Netherlands Heart Registration infrastructure. Estimated completion: December 31, 2030, with expected median follow-up of 2 years. Active recruiting centers include Amphia Ziekenhuis (Breda) and University Medical Center Groningen [SELEQT-HF Trial: CenterWatch].

**NCT05669404:** A single-blind, 3-month RCT (n=240) comparing salt restriction, DASH diet + salt restriction, and Mediterranean diet + salt restriction on BP levels in adults with high normal BP or grade 1 hypertension. This is one of the first clinical trials designed to directly compare the effects of the DASH diet and Mediterranean diet alongside salt restriction on BP levels and cardiometabolic risk factors [Effects of the DASH vs. the Mediterranean Diet Combined With Salt Restriction: ClinicalTrials.gov NCT05669404].

**NCT04531267:** A clinical trial investigating the effect of personalized calcium and magnesium supplementation on blood pressure in hypertensive patients aged 50-79 to achieve a dietary calcium:magnesium ratio of 2.3 [Ratio of Dietary Calcium to Magnesium on Cardiovascular Risk: ClinicalTrials.gov NCT04531267].

## 5. Mixture Effects and Multi-Trace Element Interventions

A randomized controlled trial of 96 critically ill participants found that intravenous supplementation of multi-trace elements (zinc, copper, selenium, chromium, and manganese) significantly improved outcomes: 28-day mortality was 18 deaths in the interventional group vs. 32 in the control group; 90-day mortality was 20 vs. 35; mean ICU stay was lower for the interventional group (10.60±3.42 days) vs. controls (16.83±3.00 days) (p<0.0001) [Effects of parenterally administered trace elements: European Journal of Cardiovascular Medicine, 2023].

A study using G-computation analysis (450 participants; Scientific Reports, 2024) found that the mixture effect of nine trace elements (As, Cd, Hg, Pb, Ni, Cr, Cu, Fe, Zn) was significantly associated with CVD (OR 4.17, 95% CI 2.16–7.69) and T2D (OR 11.96, 95% CI 5.65–18.26). Cadmium had the highest weight in the CVD model (0.78), followed by Hg (0.197). For T2D, the most weighted elements were Pb (0.32), Ni (0.19), Cr (0.17), and Cd (0.14) [Mixture effects of trace element levels on CVD and T2D: Scientific Reports, 2024].

Hair mineral analysis has emerged as a promising non-invasive method for assessing long-term exposure to trace elements and metals. Patients with CVD consistently exhibit elevated levels of toxic elements (As, Hg, Pb, Cd, Ni, Al) and reduced concentrations of protective essential micronutrients (Mg, Mn, Zn, Cu, Fe, Cr, Li) in hair samples [Essential and Toxic Elements in Cardiovascular Disease: IJMS, 2025].

## 7. Conclusions and Future Directions

The evidence to date presents a complex picture. While mechanistic studies convincingly demonstrate the involvement of multiple metal ions in cardiovascular pathophysiology, the translation of these findings into effective therapeutic interventions has been challenging.

**Key Conclusions:**

1. **The U-shaped relationship is a recurring theme.** For selenium, zinc, copper, and iron, both deficiency and excess can be detrimental to cardiovascular health. This makes simple supplementation approaches risky and explains many of the null or negative trial results.

2. **The strongest clinical evidence supports intravenous iron therapy for heart failure with iron deficiency**, which now has Class I guideline recommendations and FDA approval. This is the only metal-based intervention that has achieved widespread clinical adoption.

3. **Magnesium supplementation shows consistent but modest benefits for blood pressure reduction**, particularly in hypertensive individuals and those with hypomagnesemia, with a 5 mm Hg reduction in SBP corresponding to approximately 10% reduction in major cardiovascular events.

4. **Major trials have failed to demonstrate benefit for selenium supplementation** (SELECT, NPC, Cochrane meta-analysis) and **EDTA chelation therapy** (TACT2), and some interventions (calcium supplements, high-dose selenium, zinc in healthy individuals) may increase risk.

5. **Emerging approaches hold promise but require further research.** Ferroptosis inhibitors, copper-modulating therapies targeting cuproptosis, metal-based nanoparticles, and nutrigenetic-guided interventions represent exciting frontiers. However, no anti-ferroptotic therapy has been tested in clinical trials for CVD, and most nanoparticle platforms remain in preclinical stages.

6. **Personalized approaches based on genetic factors may be essential.** Nutrigenetic polymorphisms in SELENOP, GPX1, SEPP1, and HFE genes significantly modify individual responses to selenium and iron, suggesting that "one-size-fits-all" supplementation approaches are unlikely to succeed.

7. **Dietary patterns may be more effective than individual supplements.** The Mediterranean diet, which provides a balanced intake of multiple metals and other nutrients, has demonstrated consistent cardiovascular benefits, while individual supplements have largely failed to replicate these effects in clinical trials.

**Future Research Priorities:**

- Development and validation of reliable biomarkers for ferroptosis detection in serum and tissue
- Clinical trials of ferroptosis inhibitors (UAMC-3203, ferrostatin-1 analogs) in appropriate CVD populations
- Further investigation of cuproptosis-targeted therapies in cardiovascular contexts
- Large-scale, genetically-informed trials of selenium and zinc supplementation
- Continued development of targeted nanoparticle delivery systems for metal-based therapeutics
- Integration of AI and machine learning for nanoparticle drug delivery system design
