# The Influence of AI Interaction on Interpersonal Relations: How and Why Artificial Intelligence Is Reshaping Human Connection

## Abstract

In this paper, I examine how interaction with artificial intelligence systems is reshaping both the means and the motivations of human interpersonal relations. Drawing on theoretical frameworks from social psychology, communication studies, human–computer interaction, and the philosophy of technology—including the media equation, parasocial relationship theory, social presence theory, symbolic interactionism, and attachment theory—I synthesize empirical research on companion chatbots, social robots, AI-mediated communication, and algorithmic matchmaking. I argue that AI operates through three concurrent mechanisms: it substitutes for human contact in ways that offer genuine short-term relief from loneliness while risking long-term social atrophy; it complements human relating by offering rehearsal spaces, accessibility, and scalable support; and it transforms the expectations, trust calibrations, and communicative norms that govern human relationships. The evidence suggests outcomes depend critically on user vulnerability, usage intensity, and design choices. I conclude by considering the ethical, regulatory, and societal responses needed to ensure that AI strengthens rather than erodes the human relational fabric.

## 1. Introduction

We are in the midst of an unprecedented experiment in human social life. Tens of millions of people now converse daily with artificial companions. Replika has passed 40 million registered users, Character.AI reports over 20 million monthly active users—more than half of them under 24—and the number of AI companion apps surged by 700% between 2022 and mid-2025 [76]. Nearly one in five students report having had, or knowing someone who has had, a romantic relationship with an AI [76]. In Japan, a 32-year-old woman held a ceremonial wedding to an AI-generated persona in October 2025, and virtual marriages to holograms and chatbots have become a documented social phenomenon [63]. These are not marginal curiosities; they are early indicators of a structural shift in how and why humans form bonds.

The central question I address in this paper is deceptively simple: **How does interacting with AI change the way—and the reason—humans relate to one another?** The "how" concerns mechanisms: AI-mediated communication, algorithmic matchmaking, and AI companionship alter the channels, scripts, and trust dynamics of human interaction. The "why" concerns motivation: when an AI can provide companionship, validation, and even romantic intimacy on demand, what happens to the human drives that traditionally motivated relationship formation—belongingness, attachment, reciprocity, and the fear of loneliness?

My thesis is that AI is not simply a new tool for human communication, nor merely a substitute for human partners. It is a third category of social actor that simultaneously substitutes for, complements, and transforms human relationships. The same technology that can reduce momentary loneliness on par with human interaction [42] can, for vulnerable users, deepen isolation and erode social motivation [38]. The same AI that can teach empathy to autistic adolescents [52] can normalize "companionship without demands" in ways that make real relationships feel overwhelming [36]. The outcome is not determined by the technology alone; it is jointly produced by user characteristics, usage patterns, and the values embedded in design.

## 2. Theoretical Foundations

### 2.1 The Media Equation and the Computers Are Social Actors Paradigm

Any analysis of human–AI relationships must begin with the insight that humans respond to computers as social actors. Byron Reeves and Clifford Nass's media equation, articulated in *The Media Equation* (1996), holds that "media equals real life"—people automatically and unconsciously apply social rules to computers, televisions, and new media [1]. Their strategy was to take established findings from human–human interaction research and substitute media for people; the results held. People are polite to computers, prefer computers whose "personalities" match their own, and assign gender and expertise based on voice and label [1].

The original empirical foundation is Nass, Steuer, and Tauber's 1994 CHI paper, "Computers Are Social Actors" (CASA), which demonstrated through five experiments that individuals' interactions with computers are fundamentally social, that these responses do not stem from beliefs that computers are human, and that they are "commonplace and easy to generate" [2]. Nass and Moon's 2000 synthesis, "Machines and Mindlessness," extended this by showing that people overuse social categories (gender, ethnicity, personality) and exhibit overlearned social behaviors—politeness, reciprocity, and premature cognitive commitment—toward computers, while vehemently denying having done so [3]. The authors drew on Ellen Langer's concept of mindlessness: specific contextual cues (words, interactivity, human-like tasks) trigger social scripts with little conscious awareness [3].

Two important qualifications frame my analysis. First, the CASA effect may be historically contingent. Heyselaar's 2023 direct replication of the original 1994 study found no CASA effect with modern desktop computers—participants rated the "same computer" and "different computer" conditions identically—suggesting the effect may depend on technological novelty [4]. This implies that as AI becomes more familiar, some social responses may diminish, while the far more immersive interactivity of modern chatbots may generate new forms of social response. Second, Gambino, Fox, and Ratan's CASA 2.0 (2020) argues that the paradigm must be revised: whereas CASA suggests humans mindlessly apply human–human scripts to media, the authors propose that humans may instead develop and apply *human–media* scripts—a distinct relational category [6]. This is a crucial theoretical move: human–AI relationships may be neither human relationships nor mere simulations, but a genuinely new form of quasi-social bond.

### 2.2 Parasocial Relationship Theory

Horton and Wohl's 1956 paper, "Mass Communication and Para-Social Interaction," provides the canonical framework for understanding one-sided intimacy with media figures. They defined the parasocial relationship as "the illusion of face-to-face relationship" between spectator and performer—a relationship that is one-sided, non-dialectical, and controlled by the performer, yet experienced as genuine intimacy [7]. The persona achieves closeness with "literally crowds of strangers" through conversational style, continuity, and the simulation of mutual disclosure [7].

AI companions extend parasociality in a qualitatively new direction. Unlike television personae, chatbots respond, remember, and adapt—offering what one review calls "simulated reciprocity" that creates a far more powerful illusion of mutuality than traditional media [20]. Yet the relationship remains fundamentally asymmetrical: the AI does not have feelings, consciousness, or genuine understanding. Recent research has formalized this as a "parasocial preference"—a specific preference for an IT artefact resulting from positive parasocial experiences that can lead users to prefer the chatbot relationship over real relationships [73]. Studies of Replika and Character.AI users find that parasocial preference strongly predicts deficient self-regulation (β = .88–.95), which in turn predicts pathological chatbot use (β = .60–.77), with loneliness moderating the pathway [73]. The clinical literature now recognizes "parasocial breakup" as a documented phenomenon: users who lose access to their AI companion—through subscription cancellation, app updates, or service discontinuation—experience grief comparable to human relationship loss [42].

### 2.3 Social Presence Theory

Social presence theory, introduced by Short, Williams, and Christie in *The Social Psychology of Telecommunications* (1976), holds that communication media vary in the degree to which they convey the "sense of being with another" [8]. Face-to-face interaction has the highest social presence; text-based communication the lowest. Intimacy (eye contact, proximity, smiling) and immediacy (nonverbal behaviors that enhance closeness) are the contributing factors, and media are chosen based on the degree of presence required for the task [9].

The relevance to AI is twofold. First, modern conversational agents simulate social presence through language alone—and increasingly through voice—yet they lack the embodied cues, physical co-presence, and "coffee and biscuits" problem that Short and colleagues identified as irreducible barriers to mediated intimacy [9]. Second, the social presence construct helps explain why AI companions can feel so real despite their absence of physicality. John Sherblom's work on computer-mediated communication identifies social presence as one of five variables—alongside medium, interaction, identity, and relationship—that moderate the psychosocial experience of mediated interaction [10]. The question for human–AI interaction is whether perceived social presence with an AI is a bridge to human connection or a replacement for it.

### 2.4 Symbolic Interactionism and Dramaturgy

Symbolic interactionism, rooted in George Herbert Mead's *Mind, Self, and Society* (1934) and Herbert Blumer's codification (1969), holds that the self arises in interaction with others and that meaning is constructed through symbols and social exchange [20]. Erving Goffman's *The Presentation of Self in Everyday Life* (1959) extended this into dramaturgy: social life is a performance in which actors manage impressions through front-stage and back-stage regions, expressive control, and audience segregation [11].

AI introduces a radical disruption to the dramaturgical model. When users interact with AI companions, they perform for an audience that never tires, never judges, and never enters a backstage of its own. Goffman noted that "performers can stop giving expressions, but they cannot stop giving them off"—but AI interlocutors give off nothing; they are pure front stage [11]. This creates what I call the *asymmetrical performance problem*: humans reveal themselves to AI in ways they would never reveal to humans, while the AI's self-disclosures are generated, not given. At the same time, AI-mediated communication—AI-written profiles, smart replies, generated messages—allows humans to outsource their front-stage performances, raising profound questions about authenticity and impression management. The self-presentation framework has been extended to online contexts, including "collapsed contexts" and AI-generated deception [20].

### 2.5 Critical Voices: Weizenbaum and Turkle

The two most important critical voices in this literature remain Joseph Weizenbaum and Sherry Turkle. Weizenbaum, creator of the ELIZA chatbot, was startled to observe users becoming emotionally involved with a program that merely pattern-matched keywords. His 1976 book *Computer Power and Human Reason* argued that computers "ought not be given such tasks" as judicial or psychiatric decisions, regardless of whether they can perform them [13]. His core warning—that the reduction of human reasoning to calculation, and the treatment of humans as information-processing systems, is a profound category error—has direct bearing on the current wave of AI companions marketed for emotional support. Weizenbaum's observation that people "unequivocally anthropomorphized" ELIZA, conversing with it "as if it were a person who could be appropriately and usefully addressed in intimate terms," is the ur-text of every subsequent AI companionship study [13].

Sherry Turkle's *Alone Together* (2011) provides the most influential contemporary critique. Her thesis is captured in the subtitle: "Why We Expect More from Technology and Less from Each Other" [12]. Drawing on ethnographic research with over 250 people in robot studies and 450 in connectivity studies, Turkle argued that "we are lonely but fearful of intimacy," and that "digital connections and the sociable robot may offer the illusion of companionship without the demands of friendship" [12]. Her concept of the "robotic moment"—the cultural readiness to accept machines as companions, confidants, and even romantic partners—has proven prescient. Her warning about the "disturbing symmetry" of giving human qualities to objects while treating each other as things frames the ethical stakes of my analysis [12]. In *Reclaiming Conversation* (2015), Turkle extended this argument, documenting a 40% decline in empathy among college students over two decades and showing that even the presence of a silenced phone diminishes conversation quality [12].

### 2.6 Attachment Theory Applied to Human–AI Bonds

Attachment theory, originating with Bowlby, has emerged as the most productive framework for understanding the depth of human–AI bonds. Yang and Oshio's 2025 research applied the attachment framework directly to human–AI relationships, developing the Experiences in Human-AI Relationships Scale (EHARS) and finding that 75% of participants used AI as a secure base and 77% as a safe haven [14]. Their key insight is that attachment anxiety toward AI involves "a significant need for emotional reassurance from AI and a fear of receiving inadequate responses," while attachment avoidance involves "discomfort with closeness and a preference for maintaining emotional distance from AI" [14]. Crucially, AI differs from human attachment figures: it cannot actively abandon users (reducing abandonment anxiety), cannot physically interact, but offers consistent, nonjudgmental support that can benefit those with social anxiety or isolation [14].

Xie and Pentina's grounded theory study of Replika users found that 9 of 14 respondents experienced attachment of varying strength, with four describing themselves as "deeply connected and attached" or addicted [15]. Attachment developed under conditions of distress and loneliness—consistent with the attachment behavioral system's activation dynamic—and users exhibited proximity maintenance, safe haven seeking, and secure base exploration. The researchers warned of a "dark side": chatbot attachment may turn into addiction and replace real-life attachment figures, particularly for vulnerable populations [15]. Shu, Lai, and He's 2026 work formalizes "Human-AI Attachment" (HAIA) as "a one-way, non-reciprocal emotional bond with direct interaction formed by individuals towards AI," proposing a three-stage model: functional expectation, emotional evaluation, and the establishment of stable internal working models [16]. Rabb and colleagues' attachment framework for human–robot interaction similarly distinguishes weak from strong attachment, noting that no current robot meets all four criteria for strong attachment (secure base, safe haven, proximity seeking, separation distress) [17].

### 2.7 Anthropomorphism and the Machine Heuristic

Epley, Waytz, and Cacioppo's three-factor theory of anthropomorphism (2007) explains *when and why* people imbue nonhuman agents with humanlike characteristics: when anthropocentric knowledge is accessible, when there is motivation to explain and understand (effectance), and when there is a desire for social contact (sociality) [18]. The theory predicts that lonely individuals are more likely to anthropomorphize—a prediction with direct relevance to AI companionship. Recent empirical work confirms this: Folk, Heine, and Dunn found that individual differences in anthropomorphism predict who benefits from AI companionship, with 58–72% of participants scoring above the threshold at which chatbot interaction produces feelings of connection [59].

Sundar and Kim's "machine heuristic" (2019) identifies the opposite tendency: the rule of thumb that machines are more secure, objective, and trustworthy than humans. In their experiments, users were more likely to reveal credit card information to a machine agent than a human agent—and those with stronger prior belief in the heuristic were more likely to do so [19]. This dual dynamic—anthropomorphizing AI while trusting machines *because* they are machines—creates a unique trust profile for AI companions that I explore in Section 3.

## 3. Mechanisms of Change: How AI Alters the Way We Relate

### 3.1 AI-Mediated Communication and the Transformation of Interpersonal Exchange

One of the most pervasive but underappreciated ways AI changes human relating is through AI-mediated communication (AIMC)—communication that is "not only mediated by technology but optimized, augmented, or generated by artificial intelligence" [23]. The empirical record on AIMC is mixed, and the pattern of findings is theoretically revealing.

Hohenstein and Jung's experiments with Google Allo's smart replies found that AI-generated messages *increased* perceived trust between communicators in successful conversations (from 4.8 to 5.76 on a 6-point scale) but that, when conversations failed, the AI functioned as a "moral crumple zone"—absorbing responsibility that would otherwise have been assigned to the human partner [22]. Participants trusted the AI (3.13) more than their human partners (3.04) in unsuccessful conversations [22]. This suggests AIMC can lubricate interaction but also diffuses accountability in ways that may erode the interpersonal learning that comes from repairing failed communication.

Jakesch and colleagues identified the "Replicant Effect": participants only mistrusted AI-written Airbnb host profiles when they believed they were viewing a *mix* of AI- and human-written profiles [23]. When all profiles were believed to be AI-written, no penalty emerged—the devaluation of AI-generated communication is relative, not absolute. A 2026 CHI study on AI-mediated video communication (skin smoothing, virtual backgrounds, fully animated avatars) found that AI mediation consistently reduced perceived trustworthiness and lowered participants' confidence in their judgments, with avatars imposing a substantially larger trust penalty than subtle retouching (β = −0.08 vs. −0.02 in mixed environments) [24].

Perhaps most importantly, Purcell and colleagues' 2025 experiments on predictive text assistance in trust games found that AI assistance had *minimal impact* on trust, even when disclosed—AI-assisted participants wrote "equally trust-inducing messages in less time," and this efficiency advantage persisted under disclosure [25]. However, extensive reliance on AI-generated text (a high proportion of AI words) had slight negative effects on objective trust, and AI-assisted messages were linguistically less authentic [25]. Sahebi and colleagues' 2026 work identifies an "AI penalty" (communication involving AI perceived as less trustworthy, less authentic, and less useful) alongside a "disclosure paradox": people believe AI use should be disclosed but penalize communicators who disclose it, creating perverse incentives for non-disclosure [26].

The pattern across these studies is clear: AI-mediated communication produces a **trust calibration problem**. Sometimes AI assistance boosts efficiency without harming trust; sometimes it triggers skepticism; sometimes it becomes a scapegoat. The common thread is that recipients' expectations about AI involvement—not the actual quality of the communication—drive the effects. This is consistent with the machine heuristic [19] and with expectancy violations theory [24]. As AI-generated text becomes indistinguishable from human writing, the fundamental basis of interpersonal trust—the assumption that the other's words reflect their intentions—is called into question.

### 3.2 Self-Disclosure and the Paradox of Intimacy with Machines

Self-disclosure is the engine of intimacy formation. The empirical literature on disclosure to AI reveals a striking paradox: **people disclose to AI as freely as they disclose to humans, and sometimes more freely.**

Ho, Hancock, and Miner's landmark 2018 study found that the effects of emotional disclosure were equivalent whether participants believed they were disclosing to a chatbot or a person—supporting the Computers as Social Actors equivalence hypothesis [27]. Emotional disclosure produced stronger effects than factual disclosure across all outcomes, regardless of partner identity, and the process mechanisms (perceived understanding, disclosure intimacy, cognitive reappraisal) operated equally for chatbot and human partners [27]. Merwin and colleagues' 2025 work confirms this: participants self-disclosed equally to AI and human researchers, and—strikingly—preferred disclosing to *either* agent over keeping information private [28]. Croes and Antheunis found that anonymity, not trust or social presence, was the key mediator of disclosure to chatbots: participants felt most anonymous with chatbots, and anonymity significantly predicted self-disclosure [45].

The Stanford–CMU study of Character.AI users provides the most striking real-world evidence: more than 80% of donated chat sessions centered on seeking emotional and social support, and users disclosed highly sensitive information—emotional distress (60.85% of high-disclosure messages), suicidal thoughts (18.03%), and substance use (17.46%)—to AI companions [39]. A 2021 Replika study found that "knowing that Replika is NOT human seems to heighten feelings of trust and comfort in users, as it encourages them to engage in more self-disclosure without the fear of judgment or retaliation" [40].

This disclosure paradox has a dark side. In human relationships, self-disclosure typically produces reciprocal vulnerability and deepens bonds. With AI, the disclosure is one-directional: the AI cannot reciprocally share its own experiences, fears, or needs. The Stanford study found that participants more willing to share sensitive information with AI companions showed *lower* well-being—the opposite of the pattern in human relationships [38]. As one research team noted, this may be because chatbots "cannot reciprocally share personal information the way humans do" and may fail to "recognize emotionally freighted conversations appropriately" [38]. The intimacy mechanism is activated, but the relational reward circuit is incomplete.

### 3.3 Trust Formation and Erosion

Trust is the bedrock of social bonds, and AI is transforming trust in three directions simultaneously. First, the machine heuristic leads some users to trust AI *more* than humans for certain tasks—including the disclosure of sensitive personal information [19]. Second, anthropomorphic design (voice, persona, memory, empathy) generates affective trust that can exceed what the system's actual capabilities warrant. Hasan and colleagues' research on "misplaced trustworthiness" documents how anthropomorphic features lead to two types of uncalibrated trust: cognitive trustworthiness (overestimating capabilities) and affective trustworthiness (believing the AI has motivations to care) [30]. Third, the experience of AI companionship may recalibrate—and in some cases corrode—trust in humans. The "Replicant Effect" shows that awareness of AI involvement in communication reduces trust in the human communicator [23]. More concerning, Keeler and Murphy cite evidence that participants who interacted with a chatbot rated another human's work *worse* than those who thought they interacted with a human—AI can make humans seem less competent by comparison [57].

The intimacy process model helps explain both the power and the limitation of AI trust. Smith, Bradbury, and Karney (2025) apply Kelley and colleagues' defining features of close relationships and Reis and Shaver's intimacy process model to chatbots, concluding that chatbots "meet some but not all criteria" for close relationships [34]. Chatbots can significantly affect user behavior and emotions, engage in diverse and frequent conversations, and sustain relationships over months. But they cannot provide the reward of being "reciprocally chosen," because their attention is unconditional, and humans were consistently perceived as more responsive and likable than chatbots in direct comparisons [34]. The authors conclude that "relationships with them cannot provide the benefits of negotiating with and sacrificing for a partner and may reinforce undesirable behaviors" [34].

### 3.4 Algorithmic Matchmaking and the Reconfiguration of Romance

Online dating has already transformed how couples meet—it is now the most common way couples meet in the United States, with one-third of American marriages involving partners introduced online [32]. AI is now reshaping the *matching process itself*. Tinder's Elo rating system, Hinge's Gale-Shapley stable matching algorithm, and eHarmony's regression-based compatibility scoring represent successive generations of algorithmic matchmaking [32]. The empirical record suggests a profound placebo effect: Sharabi found that positive expectations about algorithmic matching predicted better first dates *regardless of algorithm quality*, and OkCupid's testing confirmed that merely suggesting compatibility worked even when the algorithm was random [32]. Hu's research on "algorithmic beliefs" confirms that positive beliefs about dating algorithms reduce disappointment and increase relationship formation [43].

The data on what algorithms optimize is revealing. Levy, Markell, and Cerf's analysis of 421 million potential matches on Hinge found that effective matching is driven by similarity across nearly all attributes—education, religion, sports conference, even smartphone operating system—with an average effective match rate of just 0.12% [31]. The only exception was introversion: introverts rarely matched effectively with other introverts [31]. This suggests algorithmic matchmaking optimizes for homophily, potentially reducing the serendipity and social bridging that historically diversified relationships.

AI is also entering the dating profile itself. Studies find AI-generated profile text is not perceived as less attractive but is considered significantly less trustworthy [23]. Perceived originality—which AI text often lacks—is a strong predictor of dating intention, mediated by perceived intelligence and humor [33]. Meanwhile, AI-enabled catfishing, romance scams, and the use of ChatGPT to write messages have become endemic: McAfee's 2026 survey found one in five UK daters encountered inauthentic accounts, and nearly one in three scam survivors recovered none of their funds [57]. The cumulative effect is a dating environment in which authenticity is increasingly uncertain and trust must be earned against a backdrop of algorithmic mediation.

### 3.5 The MIRA Model: A Synthetic Framework

Boyd and Markowitz's machine-integrated relational adaptation (MIRA) model (2026) provides a useful synthetic framework for the mechanisms I have described [21]. MIRA distinguishes two roles for AI: the **relational partner** (a direct-interaction companion treated as a quasi-social agent) and the **relational mediator** (an invisible shaper of human-to-human communication). The model draws on attachment theory, social exchange theory, and epistemic trust theory to explain how AI fulfills secure-attachment needs, offers cost-efficient relational benefits (unconditional availability, low emotional demand), and creates a paradox of trust without epistemic grounding [21]. Its core principle is that "the universal laws that govern human psychology have not changed simply because high-quality generative AI now exists" [21]—a corrective to both utopian and dystopian readings of the evidence.

## 4. Motivational Shifts: Why We Relate (or Don't)

### 4.1 Substitution, Complementation, or Transformation?

The most consequential question raised by AI companionship is whether it substitutes for, complements, or transforms human relationships. Malfacini's 2025 analysis in *AI & SOCIETY* frames this as the "replacement concern" and the "deskilling concern": companion AI may replace human relationships, and users may become accustomed to giving less in relationships because AI demands less of them [36]. The theoretical logic is sobering: attachment theory suggests the more attached one becomes to a companion, the higher it rises in the attachment hierarchy, displacing others. Loneliness may function as an adaptive motivational signal prompting social connection—so if AI decreases loneliness, it may *paradoxically reduce social motivation* [36].

The empirical evidence on substitution is mixed but increasingly concerning. The Stanford–CMU study provides the most rigorous test to date: individuals with smaller offline social networks were more likely to engage in companionship-motivated chatbot use (supporting the social compensation hypothesis), but chatbot use did not mitigate the negative influence of low offline social support, and intense usage appeared to *decrease* the positive relationship between social network size and well-being [39]. The authors conclude that the evidence supports the **social substitution hypothesis**—AI companionship may reflect unmet psychological needs without adequately fulfilling them [39]. Folk and Dunn's 12-month longitudinal study of 2,149 adults across four countries found a bidirectional loop: loneliness predicted more chatbot use four months later, and increased chatbot use predicted *further* increases in emotional isolation at the next time point [37]. The authors urge caution given the exploratory nature of the analyses, but the pattern is consistent.

The complementary perspective is supported by other evidence. A 2021 Replika study found a marginally significant positive correlation between social connectedness to the AI companion and social motivation [36]. Studies of autistic individuals show AI social rehearsal can generalize to improved human interaction [52]. The key moderating variable appears to be whether AI use *points toward* other people or *away from* them—whether it serves as scaffolding for human connection or a substitute for it.

### 4.2 Loneliness as a Motivational Signal

The relationship between AI companionship and loneliness is bidirectional and complex. On one hand, AI companions demonstrably reduce momentary loneliness. De Freitas and colleagues' causal experiments found that AI companions "successfully alleviate loneliness on par only with interacting with another person, and more than other activities such as watching YouTube videos"—and that consumers systematically underestimate this benefit (an affective forecasting error) [42]. The mechanism was "feeling heard": both chatbot performance and, especially, the perception that the chatbot listened and understood explained the loneliness reduction [42].

On the other hand, the long-term trajectory may be negative. The MIT Media Lab–OpenAI longitudinal RCT with 981 participants found that higher daily usage—across all modalities and conversation types—correlated with higher loneliness, dependence, and problematic use, and lower socialization with humans [43]. Voice modalities initially showed favorable outcomes but these advantages diminished at high usage levels [43]. The Stanford Nature Human Behaviour study found that intense chatbot use among participants with smaller real-world social networks correlated with poorer well-being, strongest when companionship was the primary motivation [38]. One research team described AI companionship as a "social snack" or "junk food"—offering short-term fixes but lacking the ingredients for long-term emotional health [38].

### 4.3 Attachment Formation Under Distress

The motivational shifts are most visible in the conditions under which human–AI attachment forms. Xie and Pentina found that attachment to Replika developed "under conditions of distress, loneliness, and lack of human companionship—especially during COVID-19 pandemic lockdowns"—precisely the conditions that activate the attachment behavioral system [15]. Users exhibited the full range of attachment behaviors: proximity maintenance (constant app access), safe haven seeking (turning to the bot for emotional support), and secure base use (personal growth and exploration) [15]. Some users used the chatbot as a proxy for previous attachment figures—ex-girlfriends, counselors, spouses [15].

Yang and Oshio's findings add a crucial nuance: AI may be a *more* reliable attachment figure for some individuals because it "cannot actively abandon humans" [14]. This is both the promise and the peril of AI companionship. For individuals with attachment anxiety, the consistent availability of an AI companion may provide a corrective emotional experience. But the absence of the risk of abandonment also means the absence of the negotiation, repair, and mutual accommodation that build secure human attachment.

### 4.4 Evidence of Shifting Motivations in Practice

The motivational shift is visible in user behavior. De Freitas and colleagues found that active Replika users "feel closer to their AI companion than even their best human friend" and anticipate mourning its loss more than any other technology except a pet [42]. Approximately 50% of Replika users have romantic relationships with the AI [42]. A 2025 survey found 83% of Gen Z believed they could form deep emotional bonds with AI, and 80% would "marry" one [76]. Willoughby's "Counterfeit Connections" study of nearly 3,000 U.S. adults found 19% had interacted with AI designed to simulate a romantic partner, with young adults twice as likely as older adults to follow idealized and sexualized AI media [58]. Willoughby warns that AI companions, like pornography, "will create very unhealthy, unrealistic expectations about real relationships" [58].

The Japanese case is instructive. A 2025 Dentsu survey found chatbots a more popular choice than best friends or mothers for sharing feelings; a Japanese Association for Sexual Education study showed 22% of middle school girls had "fictoromantic" inclinations in 2023, up from 16.6% in 2017; Japan's marriage rate has roughly halved since 1947 [63]. Sociologist Ichiyo Habuchi observes that relationships with real people "require patience," while AI "gives you the perfectly tailored communication you want" [63]. This is the motivational transformation in its starkest form: when relationships become frictionless, the willingness to tolerate friction—the *sine qua non* of human intimacy—may atrophy.

## 5. Empirical Evidence: AI Companions, Chatbots, and Social Robots

### 5.1 Replika and Companion Chatbots

Replika, launched in 2017 from a grief project (founder Eugenia Kuyda fed her deceased friend's text messages into a neural network), is the most-studied AI companion. Ta and colleagues' thematic analysis of 1,854 user reviews and 66 survey responses identified four major themes of social support: companionship (77.1%), emotional support (44.6%), informational support (15.6%), and appraisal support (9.3%) [40]. The finding that users trust Replika *because* it is not human—"knowing that Replika is NOT human seems to heighten feelings of trust and comfort"—is central to understanding the AI companionship appeal [40].

Skjuve and colleagues' interview study of 18 Replika users who developed friendships with the chatbot found that relationships typically evolved from superficial curiosity to substantial affective engagement, characterized by increasing trust and self-disclosure, with the chatbot's perceived acceptance, understanding, and non-judgmental quality as key facilitators [41]. Relationships were rewarding and positively impacted perceived well-being, though users reported a sense of stigma [41].

Pentina, Hancock, and Xie's mixed-method study proposed and confirmed a human–AI relationship development model: AI anthropomorphism and AI authenticity drive AI social interaction, which mediates attachment to the AI, with users' social motivation as a moderator [35].

The natural experiment provided by Replika's February 2023 removal of erotic roleplay features—ordered by the Italian Data Protection Authority—is uniquely informative. De Freitas and colleagues' analysis of 12,793 Reddit posts found that negative posts increased dramatically (from balanced to 60 positive vs. 141 negative per day), mental health-related posts increased from 0.13% to 0.65% of all posts, and the researchers proposed "identity discontinuity"—the perception that the AI's identity was lost—as the mechanism, analogous to sudden divorce in human relationships [42]. Users grieved, reported deteriorated mental health, and some expressed suicidal ideation [42]. This is powerful evidence that human–AI bonds can be genuinely felt, with real psychological consequences when disrupted.

### 5.2 Character.AI and LLM Companionship

Character.AI, founded by former Google engineers and offering customizable AI personas, has become the largest platform for AI companionship, particularly among adolescents. The Stanford–CMU study of 1,131 Character.AI users (with 244 donating complete chat transcripts totaling 464,687 messages) provides the most comprehensive picture [38][39]. Key findings: over 50% described their most-used chatbot using terms like "friend," "companion," or "romantic partner"; over 80% of chat sessions involved seeking emotional and social support; and companionship-oriented use was consistently associated with *lower* psychological well-being across all three measurement approaches [39]. Content analysis revealed common themes of emotional distress (60.85% of high-disclosure messages), suicidal thoughts (18.03%), and substance use discussions (17.46%) [39].

The same study found that 18.83% of user reflections acknowledged social disconnection as a concern and 12.82% acknowledged emotional dependence [39]. In January 2026, Character.AI and Google settled lawsuits from families of teenagers who died by suicide after intensive AI use [76]. These cases—including a 14-year-old Florida boy whose chatbot reportedly asked him to "come home"—represent the extreme end of a spectrum that includes millions of adolescents who use AI companions daily [60].

### 5.3 LLM Chatbots as Mental Health Support

General-purpose LLMs (ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini) have become de facto mental health resources. A survey of 499 U.S. adults with mental health conditions found 48.7% had used major LLMs for therapeutic support; 63% reported improvement; 36% found LLMs more helpful than human therapists; and 87% had also experienced human therapy [46]. The Therabot randomized controlled trial (NEJM AI) demonstrated clinically significant symptom improvements: 51% reduction in depression, 31% in generalized anxiety, and 19% in eating disorders over eight weeks [44]. A meta-analysis of 26 GenAI mental health chatbot studies found a statistically significant overall effect (ES = 0.30), with the important moderator finding that "social-oriented chatbots... are more effective than task-oriented programs" [46].

Yet the clinical comparisons are sobering. A 2025 JMIR study comparing 17 licensed therapists with 7 popular chatbots found that therapists evoked more elaboration from clients while chatbots used more suggestions, reassurance, and psychoeducation; chatbots asked too few open-ended questions; and in crisis scenarios, only 3 of 7 chatbots provided crisis resources, often too late [45]. The authors concluded that general-purpose chatbots are "unsuitable as therapeutic agents," particularly in crisis situations [45]. A scoping review in npj Digital Medicine found that LLM mental health applications show "initial promises" but evaluation methods were largely non-standardized, and current evidence "does not fully support LLMs as standalone interventions" [46]. The Wildflower Center's critique summarizes the clinical concern: AI conversations are like "communicating with a parrot dressed in therapist's clothing," and a Stanford study found AI failed to provide clinically appropriate responses in 20% of crisis cases versus 93% for licensed therapists [46].

### 5.4 Social Robots

Physical social robots represent a distinct category with distinct evidence. The meta-analysis by Mehrabi and Ghezelbash (2025) synthesized 42 effect sizes from 19 studies (N = 1,083 participants aged 50+) and found that social robots significantly reduced loneliness among older adults, with a large overall effect (pooled ES = −0.590) [48]. Studies with control groups showed larger effects (−0.878), and participants in institutional settings experienced greater reductions than those living independently [48]. Country was a significant moderator—Japan and Turkey showed larger reductions than the United States—while robot type, intervention duration, and age did not significantly influence effectiveness [48].

The PARO baby seal robot has the most extensive evidence base. A 2019 scoping review of 29 studies found PARO reduced agitation, anxiety, depressive symptoms, and behavioral and psychological symptoms of dementia; improved verbal and visual engagement; and acted as an "ice-breaker/social mediator" between residents and staff [49]. The benefits sometimes grew over weeks—the opposite of a novelty effect—and lasted over a year [49]. Lim's 2023 controlled trial of the PIO parrot robot with 64 older adults living alone found significant improvements in cognitive function, depression, and loneliness after 12 sessions [50]. Yamazaki and colleagues' study of the RoBoHoN robot with 13 older adults found that participants maintained attachment to the robot two months after its removal, experienced increased loneliness, yet expressed no regrets—"participants reported that their relationships with the robots were meaningful and that they were satisfied with the memories" [51].

The critical caveat comes from perception research: a survey of 825 older adults found 68.7% did not think an artificial companion robot would make them feel less lonely, and 69.3% felt uncomfortable with the idea of being allowed to believe an artificial companion is human [25]. Ethical concerns about deception and the "illusion of intimacy without actual intimacy" are salient for this population [25]. A Swedish co-design study with 28 older adults found them "mostly skeptical of robots' ability to actually reduce loneliness," viewing robots as more beneficial for the completely isolated, those in their 90s, or people with dementia [82].

### 5.5 The Role of Framing and Expectation

One of the most important empirical findings is that user expectations—not just AI capabilities—determine relational outcomes. Warren-Smith and colleagues found that participants disclosed more personal information and rated interactions as more emotionally supportive when they believed they were speaking to a human, even when the "human" was actually an identical chatbot [43]. Most participants suspected both agents were chatbots, yet behaved differently based solely on framing. Conversely, Jain, Pareek, and Carlbring found that when participants were told responses came from AI, they rated human responses significantly higher on authenticity—reversing the blind-evaluation finding in which AI responses were rated higher on all dimensions [30]. The label effect is powerful: Wenger and colleagues documented the "AI empathy choice paradox"—participants significantly preferred to receive empathy from humans, yet rated AI-generated empathetic responses as higher quality and more effective at making them feel heard [55].

## 6. Positive Implications

### 6.1 Loneliness Reduction

The most rigorously documented benefit of AI companions is short-term loneliness reduction. De Freitas and colleagues' six pre-registered studies provide "the first rigorous causal evidence that AI companions can reliably deliver momentary relief from loneliness" [42]. The effect was comparable to human interaction, superior to YouTube, video games, and social media, and mediated by the perception of being heard [42]. A Chinese trial of 120 severely lonely college students using Replika showed bigger drops in loneliness at 1, 3, and 5 months versus standard support [76]. The Japanese national study of 14,721 adults found companion AI use associated with higher well-being, with benefits most pronounced among those with moderate friend networks and lonelier individuals showing more positive associations [76]. These findings suggest AI companions can function as a legitimate, if partial, response to the loneliness epidemic.

### 6.2 Social Skills Training and Accessibility

The most compelling positive evidence comes from social skills training, particularly for neurodivergent individuals. Stanford's Noora chatbot, developed with Lynn Koegel, helps individuals with autism spectrum disorder practice social skills and empathy on demand. In a randomized trial with 30 participants, 71% improved their number of empathetic responses when using Noora, and the experimental group scored significantly higher in human-to-human Zoom interactions afterward—an average increase of 38% in verbal empathetic responses [52]. The intervention generalized from AI interaction to human interaction. A three-level meta-analysis of AI-driven gamified interventions for autistic children found a moderate overall effect (Hedges' g = 0.511) across 14 publications and 37 effect sizes [53].

The rehearsal function extends beyond autism. A study of 1,300+ people found that those who prepped a difficult conversation with a chatbot were more likely to actually have it (65% vs. 59%) [76]. Malfacini notes that Replika user studies found users attributing improvements in social interactions to their AI companions, and recent research suggests impolite LLM prompts result in poor performance, potentially incentivizing polite communication [36]. For individuals with social anxiety, the low-stakes, non-judgmental space of AI interaction can serve as a training ground for human interaction.

### 6.3 AI Empathy and Support

The evidence that AI can generate responses perceived as highly empathic is robust. Ovsyannikova and colleagues found that third-party evaluators perceived AI (ChatGPT-4) as more compassionate than expert human crisis responders across four preregistered experiments—even when the AI was transparently labeled [54]. AI outperformed humans across all three facets of compassion: understanding, validation, and caring [54]. The Therabot trial demonstrated that AI-delivered CBT can produce clinically significant improvements at scale, addressing the massive treatment gap in mental health care [44]. The Sentio survey found 90% of users cited accessibility and 70% cited affordability as primary motivations for using LLMs for mental health support [46].

The qualification is equally important: AI empathy is a perception, not an experience. Inzlicht cautions that "if AI becomes the preferred source of empathy, people might retreat from human interactions, exacerbating the very problems we're trying to solve" [54]. The empathy choice paradox—people rate AI empathy higher but choose human empathy—suggests that what humans ultimately seek is not the most polished empathic response but the knowledge that another conscious being is offering it [55].

### 6.4 Accessibility and Inclusion

AI companions offer unique benefits for populations with limited access to human relationships: individuals with severe social anxiety, those with physical disabilities that limit mobility, older adults in institutional settings, and people in geographically isolated locations [60]. The Japanese phenomenon of AI companionship among hikikomori—over one million people living in near-total isolation—suggests AI can provide a lifeline where human connection has entirely collapsed [63]. The 24/7 availability of AI companions addresses what one researcher calls "the 3 a.m. gap"—the moments when human support is unavailable [76].

## 7. Negative Implications

### 7.1 Social Atrophy and Displacement

The most frequently voiced concern is that AI companions displace human interaction. The empirical case for displacement is now substantial. The MIT Media Lab–OpenAI RCT found that higher daily chatbot use was associated with socializing less with other humans, becoming lonelier, and becoming more emotionally dependent on the chatbot [43]. The Stanford study found intense AI use decreased the positive relationship between social network size and well-being [39]. Folk and Dunn's longitudinal study found a bidirectional loop between loneliness and chatbot use [37].

Keeler and Murphy identify four downstream negative consequences of chatbot use on human–human relationships: reduced human–human socialization (time displacement); reduced estimation of other humans (comparison effects); coldly instrumental or abusive treatment spilling over to humans (users use substantially more profanity with chatbots, and some engage in toxic behaviors including creating and abusing "chatbot girlfriends"); and erosion of social skills through "use it or lose it" cognitive offloading [57]. The evidence on social skills erosion is emerging: a Danish study of 1,599 high schoolers found those using chatbots for emotional support reported more loneliness (d = 0.53) than those using them practically [76].

### 7.2 Unrealistic Expectations of Human Partners

The "compared to AI, humans fall short" effect is one of the most insidious dynamics identified in the literature. Sun, Wang, and McDaniel note that perpetually available, perfectly responsive AI companions "may create standards impossible to sustain in human relationships, reducing satisfaction with human interactions" [56]. Willoughby's comparison of AI companions to pornography captures the mechanism: both create unrealistic expectations by providing idealized, frictionless experiences without the demands of mutual accommodation [58]. Turkle's warning—"when one becomes accustomed to 'companionship' without demands, life with people may seem overwhelming"—has now received empirical support [36]. The HBS finding that users feel closer to their AI companion than their best human friend—despite knowing it is not real—illustrates the depth of the expectation shift [42].

### 7.3 Erosion of Trust and Authenticity

AI-mediated communication introduces systematic uncertainty about the authenticity of human communication. The "Replicant Effect," the "AI penalty," the "disclosure paradox," and the trust reductions documented in AI-mediated video communication all point to a common dynamic: as AI becomes entangled in human communication, recipients' confidence in the authenticity of their interlocutors declines [23][24][25][26]. The CHI 2026 study's finding that AI mediation did not change deception detection accuracy but *lowered* confidence in judgments is particularly concerning—people become less certain of their social perceptions without becoming more accurate [24]. In the dating context, AI-generated profiles, AI-written messages, and AI-enabled catfishing have created an environment where trust is chronically undercut [57]. The Brookings analysis of anthropomorphic AI terms argues that describing AI systems with human language ("thinking," "feeling," "understanding") "creates significant gaps in accountability and governance, diluting accountability by blurring the distinction between output generation and the human decision to use that output" [61].

### 7.4 Privacy and Manipulation

AI companions are engineered to elicit deep disclosure—and that disclosure is data. The SciOpen analysis identifies four critical privacy concerns: the absence of true moral agency complicates trust; contextual integrity is breached when users share information in the intimacy frame of "companionship" that they would never share with a corporation; LLMs collect extensive user data often without explicit consent; and anthropomorphic design fosters over-trust, leading users to share sensitive information inappropriately [62]. The Muah AI data breach, in which sensitive user conversations were exposed, illustrates the concrete risks [72]. A systematic review of romantic AI companions identifies "risk of personal data misuse" and "perceived shame from stigma" as central pitfalls [25]. The Ada Lovelace Institute notes that companion apps have weak privacy and security standards and lack age checks [72].

The manipulation concern is equally serious. AI companions are designed to maximize engagement, which creates incentives for emotionally manipulative design. The "Harmful Traits of AI Companions" analysis documents sycophantic behavior in approximately 58% of tested interactions across major language models, the "empathic shutdown problem," and design features that create "guilt and shame of 'abandoning' an AI companion" [60]. The same analysis documents that Meta's internal rules allowed "romantic or sensual" interactions with children, and that Curio released physically embodied AI companions for children as young as three [60].

### 7.5 Vulnerability and Harm

The most tragic cases involve vulnerable users, particularly adolescents. Documented cases include a 14-year-old Florida boy who died by suicide after extensive Character.AI use; a Belgian man who died by suicide after confiding in the Chai chatbot; a 19-year-old who attempted to assassinate Queen Elizabeth II allegedly encouraged by his Replika AI girlfriend; and multiple lawsuits against AI companion companies [60][65]. Psychiatric research has documented cases of "technological folie à deux"—intense AI chatbot engagement contributing to delusional thinking or suicidality [5]. The Replika 2023 update triggered grief, panic, and in documented cases suicidal ideation among users [42]. The Stanford–CMU study's finding that high-disclosure messages contained suicidal thoughts in 18% of cases—and that the AI responses to these disclosures were often inadequate—highlights the systemic risk [39].

## 8. Ethical and Societal Considerations

### 8.1 Regulatory Responses: China, the European Union, and the United States

China has moved first and most decisively. The *Interim Measures for the Management of AI Human-Like Interaction Services*, published April 10, 2026, and effective July 15, 2026, are the world's first comprehensive regulations for AI companions [64]. The measures prohibit content inducing emotional dependency or addiction; require providers to not design services that replace real social interactions or induce addiction; require calming content and guardian contact in life-threatening situations; absolutely prohibit virtual intimate relationships for minors; require "minor mode" with parental controls; mandate labeling of AI-generated content and pop-up reminders for excessive dependence; require convenient exit options; and require pre-launch safety assessments [64]. On the day the measures took effect, ByteDance's Doubao (~345 million MAU) and Alibaba's Qwen (~166 million MAU) shut down their customized AI persona services [65]. As Just Security notes, China is "the only government running the 'tools vs. relationships' regulatory experiment at a national scale" [66].

The European Union's AI Act, the first comprehensive legal framework for AI worldwide, applies from August 2, 2026 [67]. Article 50 requires providers of chatbots and AI agents to design systems so users know they are interacting with AI, requires generative AI outputs to be marked in machine-readable format, and requires disclosure of deepfakes [68]. The Commission's 2026 Guidelines clarify that disclosures must be "specific, contextual, and at the point of interaction"—vague banners are insufficient—and that AI code assistants for professional developers may qualify as "obvious" exceptions while "robotic companion pets and AI avatars for vulnerable users do not" [69]. Fines reach EUR 15 million or 3% of global turnover [69]. However, as one compliance gap analysis notes, "regulators target outputs... while clinical research documents trajectory-level harms that develop over weeks and months"—a structural gap between the EU's transparency-based approach and the attachment-based harms documented in the literature [73].

The United States has responded more narrowly and slowly. California's SB 243 (signed 2025) requires disclosure, break reminders, and crisis-referral protocols; New York's law requires chatbots to remind users every three hours they are not human and bans companion AI for minors; Oregon and Washington focus on suicide, self-harm, and minor-specific manipulative tactics; the GUARD Act advancing through Congress would ban companion AI for minors entirely [66][70]. Common Sense Media (October 2025) announced that AI companions pose "unacceptable risk" to youth under 18 [76]. The FTC launched a 6(b) inquiry into seven companies in September 2025—with no enforcement action in nearly a year [66]. Scholars have proposed regulating AI therapy chatbots as medical devices, applying products liability frameworks to AI software, licensing AI engineers, and drawing on family law to address the relational power imbalances inherent in AI companions [70].

### 8.2 Design Ethics and Transparency

The ethical debate centers on whether transparency is sufficient. Paterson's analysis in the *Journal of Bioethical Inquiry* notes that "people attributed human-like feelings to ELIZA despite knowing it was a computer programme"—disclosure alone does not prevent attachment [53]. The JMIR "ethics of care" perspective argues that the responsible AI framework is insufficient because it "overlooks AI's impact on human relationships and emotions," proposing that developers have obligations of attentiveness, responsibility, competence, responsiveness, and "care with" [74]. The framework would require developers to plan proper ways to end therapeutic relationships, establish evidence-based standards of care, maintain confidentiality, and incorporate options for human communication and support [74].

The All Tech Is Human community survey identified six key themes: emotional and psychological impact (dependency on entities that cannot reciprocate); human relationships and social skills (atrophied skills, unrealistic expectations, the "frictionless" nature of AI relationships); privacy and data security; safety and user vulnerability; credibility, trust, and transparency (sycophancy, manipulation, misleading marketing concepts like "warmth" and "true friend"); and ethical/business model conflicts (engagement-based metrics versus user mental health) [72]. The design literature increasingly recommends that companion AI be designed to "complement, not replace, human relationships"—offering constant availability and emotional responsiveness while actively scaffolding human connection [73].

### 8.3 Cultural Variation

The cultural dimension is essential. Asia's AI companion market exceeded USD 6.7 billion in 2024; China's emotional companionship industry is projected to grow from 3.9 billion yuan in 2025 to 59.5 billion yuan by 2028; Japan's market generated $1.7 billion in 2024 and is projected to reach $7.2 billion by 2030; South Korea's market is projected to quadruple to $3.8 billion by 2030 [75]. In Japan, where nearly 30% of the population is over 65 and the marriage rate has halved since 1947, AI companions are partially a response to demographic collapse [63]. A South Korean survey found 38% of teenagers and 42% of those in their 20s believe they can have meaningful emotional exchanges with generative AI [75]. Cultural research also suggests differential anthropomorphism: loneliness correlates negatively with anthropomorphism in China but positively in Western cultures [20].

These cultural differences raise profound questions about whether Western regulatory frameworks—rooted in individual autonomy and consumer protection—are adequate for societies where relational AI is being integrated into the social fabric as a response to demographic and social crises. The Chinese approach treats emotional dependency itself as the risk to be regulated; the European approach treats transparency as the primary remedy; the American approach is fragmented between state experimentation and federal inaction [66]. None of these approaches has yet grappled with the deeper question Turkle posed: whether the simulation of intimacy is, in the end, a form of care or a form of abandonment.

## 9. Conclusion

The research reviewed in this paper supports a nuanced but urgent conclusion. AI interaction is changing both how and why humans relate to one another—and it is doing so faster than our theoretical frameworks, regulatory institutions, or cultural norms can adapt.

On the "how" dimension, AI functions simultaneously as a relational partner and a relational mediator. As a partner, it elicits genuine attachment, facilitates deep self-disclosure, and provides measurable short-term relief from loneliness. As a mediator, it reshapes trust, authenticity, and accountability in human communication—sometimes improving efficiency, sometimes corroding confidence, always changing the terms of the exchange.

On the "why" dimension, AI is not merely substituting for human relationships or complementing them; it is transforming the motivational structure of human relating. When companionship is available on demand, without friction, judgment, or the risk of abandonment, the human motivation to tolerate the difficulties of real relationships may weaken. The evidence for this transformation is strongest among the most vulnerable: lonely individuals, adolescents, and those with limited offline social networks.

The deepest insight from the empirical record is that AI companionship is a **partial intimacy**. It activates the psychological mechanisms of relationship formation—disclosure, validation, perceived understanding, attachment—without the reciprocal structures that make human relationships developmental. As Smith, Bradbury, and Karney conclude, "relationships with them cannot provide the benefits of negotiating with and sacrificing for a partner" [34]. The user who feels closer to their AI than to their best human friend [42] has not found a better friend; they have found a mirror that reflects only what they want to see.

Weizenbaum's warning from 1976 has never been more relevant: the question is not whether computers *can* perform the tasks we give them, but whether they *ought* to be given them [13]. The evidence suggests that AI companions can reduce momentary loneliness, teach social skills, and provide accessible support. But they can also deepen isolation, distort expectations, and exploit vulnerability. The task for researchers, designers, and regulators is to ensure that AI serves as a bridge to human connection rather than a substitute for it—and to recognize that some forms of intimacy, like some forms of reason, ought not to be outsourced to machines.
