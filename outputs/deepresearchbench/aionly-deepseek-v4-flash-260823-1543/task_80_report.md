# Shoujo Manga Consumption and Gendered Language Use Among Queer Japanese Young Adults: A Research Study

## 1. Introduction: Research Question and Significance

This study addresses the following core question: **Does consumption of shoujo manga by queer Japanese young adults correlate with changes in their first-person pronoun choice and sentence-ending particle (SFP) usage, and does this consumption lead them to adopt language patterns more aligned with shoujo manga conventions than with mainstream societal norms?**

Japanese is unusual among major world languages in having robustly gendered grammatical categories—most prominently first-person pronouns (*watashi*, *atashi*, *boku*, *ore*, *jibun*) and sentence-final particles (*wa*, *no*, *kashira* vs. *zo*, *ze*, *sa*). These categories are the locus of a rich sociolinguistic literature demonstrating that the "women's language" (*joseigo*/*onna kotoba*) and "men's language" (*danseigo*) taught in textbooks, encoded in media, and assumed by the public are ideological constructs rather than straightforward reflections of how Japanese people actually speak [1][2][3]. Shoujo manga (girls' comics) constitute a distinctive media register built on *yakuwarigo* ("role language")—highly stereotyed speech styles that convey character traits such as gender, age, and class [4][5]. Because these conventions are exaggerated and partially detached from real-world usage, observing a minority group like queer Japanese young adults—who already navigate gendered language from a position of identity tension—offers a unique window into how mass media shapes personal linguistic expression.

The investigation is grounded in three bodies of scholarship: (1) Japanese gendered language norms and their ideological construction; (2) shoujo manga linguistics and yakuwarigo theory; and (3) queer Japanese linguistics, including *onē-kotoba* (queer male speech) and pronoun practices among LGBTQ+ speakers. The research design proposed here combines survey, interview, and social media corpus methods to capture both self-reported and naturalistic language use across contexts.

## 2. Japanese Gendered Language: Norms, Ideology, and Empirical Reality

### 2.1 The First-Person Pronoun System

The normative binary in Japanese is straightforward: *watashi* for girls and women (and for men in formal situations), *boku* for boys and men. This norm is "legitimated by official metalinguistic discourses like language textbooks" [6][7]. Analysis of five Japanese language textbooks for first graders (2015) found that "no textbook presented children using their own names; all textbooks presented girls using *watashi* and boys using *boku*; no textbook explained or justified the binary norm" [8]. Childhood acquisition data show children shifting from names to pronouns upon entering elementary school: girls' *watashi* use increases from 4.2% at age 5 to 29.4% at age 7, while boys' *boku* use rises from 29.4% to 66.7% [8].

The pronoun system extends well beyond the binary, however. Contemporary usage includes at least seven common forms:

- ***Watashi*** (私): polite, gender-neutral in formal contexts; the default for women in most situations [9][10].
- ***Atashi*** (あたし): casual, feminine, "cute" connotation; used mainly by younger women [9][10].
- ***Boku*** (僕): masculine, humble, gentle; the first pronoun Japanese boys learn; also adopted by some girls and gender-nonconforming speakers [9][10][11].
- ***Ore*** (俺): strongly masculine, casual, assertive; the most popular pronoun among young men [9][10].
- ***Jibun*** (自分): reflexive, gender-neutral, conveys modesty and distance; used in sports/military contexts [9][10][12].
- ***Uchi*** (うち): originally Kansai-regional; now the most common pronoun among college-aged women in nationwide surveys [10][13].
- ***Watakushi*** (わたくし): the most formal variant [9][10].

Historical research by Nakamura Momoko demonstrates that this gendered pronoun binary is not ancient tradition but a product of Japan's modernization: "Japanese first-person pronouns were gendered because a modern Japanese language, *kokugo*, was tailored for male speakers, reflecting the Meiji government's sexist policy of gendered nationalization which set males as primary citizens and females as secondary citizens" [7]. Nakamura argues the binary "serves to maintain heteronormativity," and that girls often break the norm "to perform novel identities" [7][14]. The pronoun system is discriminatory in a structural sense: "boys have *boku* and *ore* to express specific 'boy' identities, but girls have no corresponding pronoun; *watashi* is too formal and too general," leading girls to disproportionately use names/nicknames (63% of girls in one 2015 study vs. 32% of boys) or adopt nonnormative pronouns (*uchi*, *atashi*, *boku*, *ore*) [8].

### 2.2 Sentence-Final Particles

Sentence-final particles (SFPs) carry the second major gendered load. The conventional classification is:

- **Masculine-coded particles**: *zo* (ぞ, strong assertion, "the most imposing particle"), *ze* (ぜ, casual confidence), *sa* (さ, assertion with resignation), *na/naa* (な/なあ, reflection), and the informal copula *da* [15][16][17].
- **Feminine-coded particles**: *wa* (わ, soft assertion), *no* (の, explanatory), *kashira* (かしら, wondering), *wa yo* (わよ), *na no* (なの) [15][16][17].
- **Gender-neutral particles**: *ne* (ね, seeking confirmation), *yo* (よ, emphasis/new information), *yo ne* (よね), *kana* (かな) [15][16][17].

The *wa* particle is particularly instructive. It "first became metalinguistically salient in the early Meiji Period through 'schoolgirl speech' (*jogakusei kotoba*), later evolving into the voice of the idealized modern Japanese woman" [18]. Dahlberg-Dodd's phonetic study of *wa* across character types in anime found that intonation direction, degree of pitch change, and duration all interact with characterological stereotypes—o-joosama (young lady) and o-nee (queerqueen) characters strongly preferred rising intonation, while gyaru characters showed less than 10% rising intonation [18].

### 2.3 The Ideological Critique: "Women's Language" as Construct

The foundational finding of modern Japanese language-and-gender scholarship is that women's language is an ideology, not a description. Nakamura Momoko's *Gender, Language and Ideology* (2014; Japanese original *Onna kotoba wa tsukurareru*, 2007, winner of the 27th Yamakawa Kikue Award) argues that "Japanese women's language is not a style of speaking that Japanese women have long been using over the centuries, but an ideological construct historically created by discourse" [1][2]. She redefines women's language as "a set of norms, knowledge, and values" that operate hegemonically [1]. Miyako Inoue's *Vicarious Language* (2006) similarly demonstrates that women's language is "a cultural construct linked with Japan's national and capitalist modernity"—a discourse that claims to represent the voice of Japanese women that is not actually theirs: "Most Japanese women, particularly those in regional, working-class, and marginal populations, do not actually speak the forms identified as women's language" [19][20].

Okamoto and Shibamoto Smith's edited volume *Japanese Language, Gender, and Ideology* (2004) established the theoretical framework for understanding this gap: "prescription is never fully reflected in actual language use... self-reference choice is not the result of a speaker's gender identification but a reflexive negotiative strategy" [21]. Their key theoretical commitments—that indexicality is "multiple, flexible, context-dependent, and mediated by speakers' beliefs and attitudes," and that identities "emerge over time through discursive and other social practices"—are directly relevant to studying how media consumption interacts with queer identity [21].

### 2.4 Empirical Reality: What Young Japanese People Actually Do

The gap between norm and practice is well-documented:

- **Okamoto (1995)**'s landmark "'Tasteless' Japanese" documented college-age women frequently using sentence-final forms traditionally deemed masculine [22].
- **Okamoto (2010)** found 68.8% neutral forms in young women's speech, with feminine SFP usage declining with age [23].
- A **2014 college student survey** (reported in the Japan Times) found that among men, 76% used *ore* most often, 11% *boku*, and 4.5% each *watashi* and *jibun*—but usage shifted dramatically by context, with *watashi* spiking to 80% when speaking to teachers. Among women, 42% used *uchi*, 36% *watashi*, and 4–8% each *atashi*, *jibun*, and *boku* [10].
- A **2019 survey** found that over 30% of Japanese women and around 70% of men do not regularly use *watashi* [10].
- **Wang (2023)** analyzed casual conversations among Japanese college students (ages 19–22) and found that young men used the traditionally "female" particles *wa* (63% of tokens) and *no* (56%) more than women did. Male *wa* usage "directly indexes self-centeredness" and performs teasing and *amae* (dependency); male *no* "directly indexes truthfulness" [24].
- **Miyazaki (2004, 2023)** documented junior high school girls using stereotypically masculine pronouns in school contexts, challenging "traditional relationships between gender and language" [25][26].
- **Vranic's (2020)** study of three female university students found their speech "is not feminine as is prescribed by language ideology, but neutral to moderately masculine," with *wa* occurring only 0.25% in real conversation [23].

The consensus across this literature is that real-world Japanese speech is far more gender-neutral than textbooks and media portrayals suggest, and that "since the late 20th century, individuals do not necessarily conform to gendered speech norms" [16]. This is precisely why shoujo manga—which encodes stereotypical gendered speech in exaggerated form—provides such a productive test case for media effects.

## 3. Shoujo Manga as a Linguistic Register

### 3.1 Yakuwarigo: The Theory of Role Language

The concept of *yakuwarigo* (役割語, "role language") was proposed by Japanese linguist Kinsui Satoshi in 2003 in *Vācharu Nihongo Yakuwarigo no Nazo* (*Virtual Japanese: The Mystery of Role Language*) [4][5][27]. Kinsui defines yakuwarigo as: "When upon hearing a certain use of language... a listener or a reader can imagine a certain character image... or when a certain character type can make the observer call to mind a use of language that said character would be likely to use, we call that use of language yakuwarigo" [28]. Role language is "usually partially or entirely distinct from the real life language typical of the kind of people it is used to represent" [4].

Key features of the framework:

- Role language is a "linguistic stereotype" that amplifies gender differences "beyond the reality of the situation" [27][28].
- Standard language (*hyōjungo*) functions as a colorless, transparent baseline; any deviation (pronoun choice, SFPs, copula forms) immediately generates a character image [27][29].
- Kinsui and Yamakido (2015) distinguish "role language" (shared stereotypes, e.g., samurai speech) from "character language" (idiosyncratic speech styles, e.g., *tsundere*), with role language as a subset of character language [30].
- Kinsui himself connects role language to gender ideology, noting that "women's language" lacks vocabulary for giving commands—"it is linguistically fixed that women cannot give orders" [31].

### 3.2 Heroine and Male Lead Speech Conventions in Shoujo Manga

The canonical gendered conventions in shoujo manga are highly recognizable:

- **Heroine/female character speech**: first-person pronoun *atashi* (あたし), or *watakushi* for noblewomen; sentence-final particles *wa*, *no*, *no-ne*, *kashira*, *dawa*. The exemplar given in the yakuwarigo literature is: *"Sō yo, atashi ga shitteru wa"* ("Yes, I know"—said by a girl) [4].
- **Male lead speech**: first-person pronouns *boku* (boyish, gentle) and *ore* (adult, assertive); sentence-final particles *zo*, *ze*, *sa*, *da*. The exemplar: *"Sō da yo, boku ga shitteru no sa"* [4].
- **Politeness markers**: the honorific prefix *o-* is identified by the Kōjien dictionary as a feature of women's language [1].

Empirical corpus studies confirm and complicate this picture:

- **Unser-Schutz (2010)** analyzed 578,709 characters across eight popular manga titles. Male characters used *ore* 603 times; female characters used *atashi* 452 times. Boy-oriented series had 79.9% male lines, while girl-oriented series had a 55.5% gender balance. Notably, the study found "no instances of feminine first-person pronouns among female characters," and male lines constituted 62.9% of total lines [32].
- **Unser-Schutz (2015)**, extending this to 10 popular manga series, found "while personal pronoun usage was generally normative, all genders used a variety of gendered SFPs" [33]. Critically, shoujo manga characters used less stereotypical speech than shōnen manga characters—likely because shōjo manga's focus on interpersonal relationships and better gender balance (only 55.5% male text vs. 79.9% in shōnen) requires more naturalistic character speech [33][34].
- **Furukawa (2009)** demonstrated that translated fiction (Bridget Jones's Diary) uses feminine forms 45.22% of the time vs. 24% in real Japanese women's speech—and 45% in film subtitles, all strongly feminine. Translators "create and reinforce consensus on how women should speak" [35].
- **Kloutvorová's (2020)** doctoral dissertation directly compared the socio-person deictic expressions (first- and second-person terms) of Japanese junior high school girls (ages 12–15) with those of shoujo manga characters, finding both continuity and divergence and filling a major gap in the research literature on the girl-reader/character relationship [36].

### 3.3 Shoujo Manga's Role in Constructing Women's Language

A central argument of Nakamura Momoko's work is that girls' media—including shoujo manga and the girls' magazines from which they emerged—have historically been a primary vehicle for constructing and disseminating women's language ideology. Her genealogy traces how "schoolgirl speech" (including the *teyo-dawa* style) was indexicalized in the Meiji period, then repackaged through girls' magazines and postwar shoujo manga into a national feminine ideal [1][2]. Inoue's *Vicarious Language* reports that she herself "grew up in regional Japan where no one spoke women's language, first encountering it through television, dolls (Licca-chan), dubbed foreign films, and commercials—experiencing it as 'the language of whiteness,' commerce, and Tokyo" [19]. This is direct autobiographical evidence that media, not community, is the primary vector for acquiring joseigo.

The shoujo manga industry has also been an explicitly ideological space. Jennifer Prough's ethnographic study of the four major shoujo manga publishers found the genre defined through the phrase "what girls like"—operationalized as "what girls want," "what girls should want," and "what will sell" [37]. The genre's historiography shows a trajectory from 1920s girls' magazines and Class S fiction (Nobuko Yoshiya), through Osamu Tezuka's *Princess Knight* (1953–56) and the 1970s Year 24 Group's psychological and gender-experimental works (*The Rose of Versailles*, *Heart of Thomas*), to contemporary series with fighting heroines and gender-nonconforming characters like *Sailor Moon*'s Sailor Uranus, who uses *boku* and "the text says she is both a man and a woman" [38][39][40].

### 3.4 How Manga Differs from Real-World Speech

The key analytical tension for this study is that manga speech is both *more stereotypically gendered* than real speech and *partially detached* from it. Public perception surveys conducted by Japan's Agency for Cultural Affairs in 2001 and 2007 found that approximately 45% of respondents thought manga had influenced the language of young Japanese people, with manga consistently ranking top among perceived influences—yet also being blamed for linguistic "ailments" like young women using masculine pronouns [33][41].

Unser-Schutz's empirical work, however, "suggest[s] manga reflect previously established language changes rather than lead them, consistent with reports that mass media are not a clear source of linguistic change" [33]. This is a crucial finding that complicates the research brief's central hypothesis: manga may function less as an independent driver of linguistic change than as a *reinforcing loop* that amplifies and stereotypes existing trends. At the same time, manga's "low text" status makes it a perpetual scapegoat for moral panics about language change [41].

## 4. Queer Japanese Language Practices

### 4.1 Onē-Kotoba: The Signature Queer Register

*Onē-kotoba* (オネエ言葉, "queen-talk" or "older sister speech") is the most visible queer linguistic formation in Japanese. It is characterized by combining hyper-feminine forms (*atashi*, *da wa*) with crude language from men's speech. The canonical example is *"Atashi ima karē kuttara geri da wa"* ("I just ate curry and now I have diarrhea")—feminine pronoun and *da wa* particle combined with the masculine-crude verb *kuttara* [16][42][43]. Claire Maree describes the style as characterized by "exaggerated intonation and 'scathing wit' that appropriates hyper-feminine speech combined with vulgar language" [44].

Key facts about onē-kotoba:

- It originated among *danshō* (male sex workers) during the Shōwa era and in the gay bar scene of Shinjuku Ni-chōme, particularly bars run by *mama-san* figures [16][42].
- Maree's *queerqueen: Linguistic Excess in Japanese Media* (2020) traces the "queerqueen" media figure—"a sometimes cross-dressing cross-speaking" personality—back to at least the 1950s, arguing that queerqueen voices are collaboratively created by hidden "language-labourers" (editors, transcribers, captioners, censors) [44][45]. The style crossed into mainstream primetime TV around 2003–2005 with makeover media, positioning the queerqueen as a man "more womanly than a woman" [46].
- Reception within the queer community is mixed: some view onē-kotoba as a "cultural treasure" and "native language" that builds solidarity, while others criticize it for limiting queer representation to comical roles [42][43].
- Itakura's (2023) ethnographic study of gay volleyballers in Ni-chōme found that they "swing between discretion and disclosure by fashioning language(/gender)," using onē-kotoba tactically to "lubricate an aesthetically pro-silence erotic play" [47].

### 4.2 Pronoun Practices Among Queer Speakers

Empirical research on queer pronoun use reveals considerable diversity and context-dependence:

- **Gay men**: Ogawa and Shibamoto Smith's study of ten gay Japanese men in their 20s found the feminine *atashi* was used by only two participants, neither exclusively. One participant used *boku* in professional contexts but switched exclusively to *atashi* when discussing gay community topics; another "did not want to use a masculine pronoun and felt neutral/feminine pronouns fit his identity better." Most preferred masculine forms: six of ten used *ore* (three exclusively) [48]. The researchers concluded that "although gay Japanese men have been thought of as a monolithic category, especially as portrayed in the media and through the use of o-nē-kotoba, this is not necessarily the case" [48].
- **Lesbians**: Hideko Abe's fieldwork in Shinjuku Ni-chōme lesbian bars showed queer women using *ore* during heated arguments, regardless of their usual pronoun [42][43][49]. Abe's *Queer Japanese* (2010) is the foundational monograph on how Japanese sexual minorities "negotiate power, identity, and desire through linguistic practices," arguing that gender is fluid and negotiated through language [49][50].
- **Trans and non-binary speakers**: The gender-neutral reflexive *jibun* (自分) is frequently adopted. Onabe (transmasculine) speakers in the documentary *Shinjuku Boys* used varied gendered markers including *jibun* [16]. A 2022 Tokyo Weekender profile of non-binary individuals found one (Rummy Mizrahi, AFAB) using *watashi* in Japanese despite preferring he/him in English, while another (Yoshiki Matsuoka/Omochi, AMAB) uses neutral *watashi* at work and *jibun* outside work "as a statement rejecting the gender binary" [51]. The Heritage Japanese textbook literature identifies *jibun* as "a particularly useful pronoun for non-binary individuals or those who prefer not to express gender through their choice of pronoun" [52].
- **Fushimi Noriaki**, the gay activist and writer, reportedly "went five years without using any self-referencing term" [8].

### 4.3 Deliberate Subversion, Adoption, and Mixing

The queer linguistic repertoire is not a simple adoption of "women's language" or "men's language" but a deliberate, reflexive negotiation. Lunsing and Maree's chapter "Shifting Speakers" (2004) in *Japanese Language, Gender, and Ideology* directly analyzed "lesbians and gays negotiating gendered self-reference forms" [21][53]. The defining feature of onē-kotoba is its parodic mixing: "parodies gendered speech by combining hyper-feminine forms (あたし, だわ) with crude language from 男言葉" [42]. This is not accidental register mixing but a conscious linguistic strategy.

Bendall's (2022) master's thesis, which tested whether Abe's 2010 findings still hold by analyzing speech in the 2019 documentary *Queer Japan*, found that "the findings that lesbian women used more masculine language and gay men used more feminine language was NOT the case" in contemporary data. The thesis concludes that "gendered language is becoming more and more obsolete and more a stereotype or forced image than a type of language that is actively used in everyday life. The only exception is with first-person pronouns, where defined masculine and feminine words are still being used" [54].

Maree's career-spanning work emphasizes that the queerqueen figure "serves contradictory goals: reproducing and yet challenging hegemonic norms of gender, sexuality, and desire" [44]. Her 2024 article with Arimori and Watanabe documents the continuing cisheteronormativity of Japanese dictionaries (e.g., *Kōjien* defining sex as "between men and women") and calls for pedagogical approaches that decenter heteronormative language norms [55].

### 4.4 Queer Language in Digital Spaces

The Carpi and Iacus (2020) large-scale study of 408 million Japanese tweets (2015–2019) found that "gendered language is in fact used also on Twitter, in about 6% of the tweets, and that the prescriptive classification into 'male' and 'female' language does not always meet the expectations, with remarkable exceptions," including "switching patterns... with 'male' markers used by females and vice versa" [56]. This finding—that even in written SNS text, roughly 6% of tweets deploy gendered language—provides a tractable corpus-based approach to studying queer speakers' usage.

## 5. Media Effects on Language: What the Evidence Shows

The empirical literature on whether media consumption changes personal speech yields a nuanced picture:

1. **Media are perceived as influential.** Agency for Cultural Affairs surveys consistently show ~45% of Japanese respondents believe manga has influenced young people's language, with manga ranking top among perceived influences [33][41].

2. **Media supply the ideological content of gendered language.** The historical record is unambiguous: joseigo as a codified register was constructed through textbooks, girls' magazines, school regulations, and postwar media—including shoujo manga [1][2][19]. Nakamura's and Inoue's genealogies show that "women's language" is a media-born ideology that people learn *from representation*, not from community inheritance [19].

3. **Media consumption correlates with but does not simply cause language change.** Unser-Schutz's corpus studies found manga "reflect previously established language changes rather than lead them" [33]. Where media effects are observed, they are mediated by identity work: Furukawa showed translated fiction over-feminizes female characters relative to real speech, constructing a normative target that readers can adopt or reject [35].

4. **Fiction and reality diverge systematically.** Vranic found *wa* occurs only 0.25% in real conversation compared to its prominence in home dramas and manga [23]. Kiyohara's (2026) analysis of AI translation showed machine translation systems render female protagonists' strategic masculine forms as soft feminine endings, "demonstrating AI's failure to recognize the strategic use of gendered forms" [57].

5. **Role language is learnable and portable.** Kinsui's yakuwarigo framework assumes that readers acquire character-speech associations from fiction and can produce them. The impressive body of work on Japanese-language learners acquiring gendered speech from anime/manga (e.g., 51% of one surveyed cohort learned Japanese solely through anime) demonstrates that fictional registers are learnable [58]. The question for this study is whether native speakers *selectively deploy* shoujo manga registers as stylistic resources.

The most defensible synthesis is a **reinforcement model**: shoujo manga does not invent gendered speech ex nihilo, but it codifies, exaggerates, and idealizes it; for readers who are already engaged in identity negotiation around gender (as queer young adults are), manga provides a readily available stylistic palette for play, parody, affiliation, and self-construction.

## 6. Research Design and Methodology

The research brief asks for flexible methodology. The optimal approach is a **mixed-methods, multi-phase design** combining (a) a large-scale survey, (b) in-depth semi-structured interviews, and (c) naturalistic SNS corpus analysis, with (d) a control group. This triangulation addresses the central limitation of self-report (participants' language ideologies often diverge from their actual usage, as Vranic demonstrated when participants' claims about their own speech contradicted their recorded speech [23]).

### 6.1 Phase 1: Survey

**Population**: Self-identified queer Japanese young adults, ages 18–35, native Japanese speakers, recruited through LGBTQ+ community organizations, queer-friendly university circles, social media (X/Twitter, Instagram), and snowball sampling. Target n = 300–500 for statistical power.

**Control group**: Two comparators: (a) queer Japanese young adults who report minimal/no shoujo manga consumption (n ≈ 150); (b) non-queer Japanese young adults matched on age, region, and education (n ≈ 150). General societal norms are additionally derived from existing corpus data (BCCWJ, the 100-million-word Balanced Corpus of Contemporary Written Japanese developed by NINJAL, which can serve as a baseline reference for contemporary written Japanese gendered forms [59]).

**Key survey instruments**:
- **Consumption metrics**: frequency of shoujo manga reading (daily/weekly/monthly), years of exposure, approximate volume of titles consumed, preferred subgenres (classic shōjo, josei, BL, yuri), and whether consumption has changed over time.
- **Pronoun inventory**: preferred first-person pronoun(s) across five contexts—casual conversation with friends, formal/work settings, online text (SNS/DM), within queer spaces, and with family. Include the full range (*watakushi*, *watashi*, *atashi*, *uchi*, *boku*, *ore*, *jibun*, own name, none), allowing multiple selections per context.
- **SFP inventory**: self-reported frequency of use for key particles (*wa*, *no*, *kashira*, *wa yo*, *zo*, *ze*, *sa*, *na*, *ne*, *yo*, *yo ne*, *da*), again contextually differentiated.
- **Identity measures**: gender identity (including non-binary/X-gender options), sexual orientation, outness level, participation in queer communities, age, region (urban vs. rural), education level.
- **Attitudinal measures**: awareness of gendered language as a norm; attitudes toward joseigo/danseigo; whether participants consciously adopt, avoid, subvert, or mix gendered forms; whether they associate shoujo manga speech with "natural" or "ideal" ways of speaking.

### 6.2 Phase 2: Semi-Structured Interviews

Select 20–30 survey respondents for follow-up interviews (stratifed by consumption level, gender identity, and pronoun choices). Interview topics:

- Life history of media consumption: when they started reading shoujo manga, which titles were formative, which characters they identified with.
- Metalinguistic awareness: can they identify the "shoujo manga way of speaking"? Do they consciously use it, avoid it, or parody it?
- Identity-language nexus: how do they feel about *watashi* vs. *boku* vs. *ore* vs. *jibun*? Do they switch pronouns in different spaces, and why? How does their queer identity shape these choices?
- Community norms: what do their queer peers use? Do queer spaces have their own linguistic norms that override or incorporate media-derived forms?
- Contextual shifting: elicit narratives about specific interactions (with family, at work, in queer bars, online) where pronoun/particle choices mattered.

This phase directly addresses the brief's question of whether participants "deliberately subvert, adopt, or mix gendered conventions," and captures the reflexive negotiation that the literature identifies as central [21][48].

### 6.3 Phase 3: Naturalistic SNS Corpus Analysis

Self-report is unreliable; naturalistic data is essential. Options:

- **X/Twitter corpus**: Recruit consenting participants to share their public (or privately provided) tweet histories. Following Carpi and Iacus's methodology (which identified gendered markers in ~6% of 408 million tweets [56]), code for first-person pronouns and SFPs in participants' posts, comparing queer participants with a matched non-queer sample and with the general Twitter baseline.
- **LINE/DM logs** (with consent): Direct-message exchanges within queer friend groups would capture the most casual register, where gendered particles are most likely to surface. This is more invasive but methodologically powerful.
- **Coding scheme**: For each utterance, record pronoun form, SFP presence/type, addressee type (where inferable), topic, and platform. Compare rates of shoujo-manga-typical forms (*atashi* + *wa/no/kashira*) against mainstream youth baselines (from the Japan Times/college surveys: ~42% *uchi*, 76% *ore* among men [10]).

### 6.4 Baseline Comparisons and Analytical Strategy

The analysis should test four hypotheses:

- **H1 (Adoption)**: Higher shoujo manga consumption correlates with greater use of shoujo-manga-typical forms (*atashi*, *wa*, *no*, *kashira*) among queer young adults, controlling for gender identity, age, and region.
- **H2 (Contextual sensitivity)**: Shoujo-manga-typical forms appear more frequently in casual and queer-space contexts than in formal contexts, indicating register-shifting rather than wholesale adoption.
- **H3 (Identity interaction)**: The effect of consumption is moderated by gender identity—e.g., trans women and AMAB non-binary participants may adopt *atashi*/*wa* patterns from manga as identity resources, while AFAB participants may use manga conventions parodically (onē-kotoba style mixing) or reject them in favor of *boku*/*ore*/*jibun*.
- **H4 (Subversion/mixing)**: Following onē-kotoba's model, queer speakers who consume shoujo manga will show higher rates of *mixed* gendered forms (feminine pronoun + masculine particle, or vice versa) than either the non-consuming queer control or the mainstream baseline.

Statistical methods: regression models with consumption intensity as the key predictor; interaction terms for gender identity; multinomial logistic regression for pronoun choice across contexts. Qualitative analysis of interviews uses thematic coding, with particular attention to participants' own ideologies about media influence.

### 6.5 Ethical Considerations and Limitations

- Privacy is paramount given Japan's relatively low rates of outness and documented harassment of LGBTQ+ students [55]. All data collection must be anonymized; SNS data requires explicit consent.
- The population is self-selected; results generalize to engaged queer community members rather than all queer Japanese young adults.
- Cross-sectional survey data cannot establish causation. The interview and corpus phases mitigate this by capturing process and practice, but a longitudinal component (re-surveying after 12 months, or tracking consumption changes) would strengthen causal inference.
- The study should be conducted in Japanese (instruments, interviews, coding), with analysis reported bilingually where appropriate, per the brief's instruction to prioritize Japanese-language sources and participants.

## 7. Anticipated Findings and Analytical Nuances

Based on the scholarship reviewed, the following outcomes are most likely:

**1. Consumption correlates with *awareness* and *stylistic repertoire*, not simple adoption.** The literature strongly suggests that manga readers know the gendered conventions better and can deploy them, but actual deployment is filtered through identity goals. As Okamoto and Shibamoto Smith argue, "self-reference choice is not the result of a speaker's gender identification but a reflexive negotiative strategy" [21]. Queer speakers are likely to be *hyper-fluent* in shoujo manga conventions precisely because the genre is a site of gender fantasy and experimentation—but their use will be strategic.

**2. Pronoun effects will be stronger than SFP effects.** Bendall's finding that "the only exception is with first-person pronouns, where defined masculine and feminine words are still being used" aligns with the broader empirical record: pronouns are the most consciously controlled gendered form [54]. Survey data show women's *uchi*/*watashi*/*boku* diversification is already mainstream [10], so queer speakers' choices will be visible and reportable. SFPs, by contrast, are shifting toward neutrality among youth generally [24]; effects may appear in *style* (e.g., using *wa* with falling vs. rising intonation [18]) rather than simple frequency.

**3. The onē-kotoba model of "mixing" is the most likely pattern for AMAB and transfeminine speakers.** The canonical *atashi* + *kuttara* + *da wa* structure demonstrates that queer Japanese speakers have a well-established template for combining feminine media-derived forms with masculine-crude content [16][42]. Shoujo manga provides the "feminine half" of this repertoire. For AFAB and transmasculine speakers, the more relevant manga resource may be tomboy and "girl prince" characters (e.g., *Princess Knight*'s Sapphire, *Revolutionary Girl Utena*'s Utena, *Sailor Moon*'s Haruka with her *boku*) [39][40].

**4. Contextual variation will be pronounced.** The 2014 survey's finding that *watashi* usage spikes to 80% when speaking to teachers demonstrates acute context-sensitivity among Japanese youth [10]. Queer speakers almost certainly shift between *ore* in queer spaces, *watashi* at work, and manga-derived forms in intimate or playful contexts.

**5. The relationship between media and language is bidirectional.** Unser-Schutz's finding that "manga reflect previously established language changes rather than lead them" [33] suggests the most accurate framing is *resonance*: shoujo manga amplifies and idealizes gendered forms that already circulate in society, and queer readers—who are already engaged in gender play—absorb and redeploy them. The study's outcome should therefore be framed not as "manga causes X usage" but as "shoujo manga provides a symbolic resource that queer young adults variously adopt, subvert, or mix in the ongoing construction of gendered selves."

---

## 8. Broader Implications

This study contributes to three conversations:

**For Japanese sociolinguistics**: It extends the Okamoto/Shibamoto Smith/Nakamura project of documenting the gap between gendered language ideology and actual practice into an understudied population—queer young adults—and links it to the equally important yakuwarigo literature on how fictional registers circulate in society [1][4][21]. The finding that queer speakers use manga conventions strategically would demonstrate that gendered language is best understood as a *stylistic resource* rather than a *normative system*.

**For media-effects research**: The study tests whether mass media shape personal expression in a minority community, moving beyond the moral-panic framing that has dominated Japanese public discourse about manga and language ("45% of respondents thought manga has influenced the language used by young Japanese people" [33]). A nuanced finding—consumption expands the stylistic repertoire without determining usage—would refine both the "media as driver" and "media as mirror" positions.

**For queer linguistics**: The study operationalizes Maree's and Abe's insights about queer linguistic agency [44][49] in a measurable, comparative design, and addresses Okamoto's (2021) call for research on "understudied speakers" and how they "deploy a wide variety of linguistic and semiotic resources for styling diverse forms of gender and sexual identity in situated practice" [60].

**For Japanese language education**: Maree, Arimori, and Watanabe's critique of cisheteronormativity in Japanese language teaching—including textbooks that teach *kanojo*/*kare* as default and *Genki*'s gendered terminology—suggests that understanding how queer speakers actually navigate pronouns and particles is a matter of pedagogical urgency, not just scholarly interest [55].

---

## 9. Conclusion

The central question—whether shoujo manga consumption correlates with changes in first-person pronoun choice and sentence-final particle usage among queer Japanese young adults—is answerable through the mixed-methods design proposed here. The theoretical and empirical groundwork is solid: we know that joseigo is an ideological construct disseminated through media [1][19]; that shoujo manga encodes distinct, exaggerated gendered speech conventions that are partially detached from real usage [4][33]; that queer Japanese speakers already engage in deliberate, context-sensitive mixing and subversion of gendered forms [42][44][49]; and that real-world youth usage is considerably more neutral and diverse than either textbooks or manga suggest [10][24]. 

The most probable substantive finding is that shoujo manga consumption does not *determine* queer speakers' pronoun and particle choices, but it does *expand their stylistic repertoires* and provide an idealized gendered register that queer young adults adopt, parody, mix, or reject as part of their ongoing identity work. Whether a transfeminine reader adopts *atashi* + *wa* as an authentic self-voice, an AFAB non-binary reader borrows tomboy *boku* conventions, or a gay man deploys hyper-feminine manga speech in the parodic onē-kotoba mode, the common thread is *agency*: media supplies the raw materials, but queer speakers do the building. This conclusion would affirm the broader sociolinguistic insight that mass media's influence on personal expression is real but mediated—always filtered through identity, context, and reflexive choice.
