# The Transformation of Physical Retail in the United States (2006–2026): A Comprehensive Analysis

## Introduction

The American retail landscape has undergone a profound transformation over the past two decades, with the COVID-19 pandemic acting as a powerful accelerant of trends already in motion. This report examines four critical dimensions of this transformation: the effects on local community economies and employment, the fate of vacant retail spaces (from regional malls to neighborhood strip centers), the concrete shifts in consumer shopping behavior, and the creative, community-driven repurposing of abandoned retail properties. Drawing on the latest data from the Bureau of Labor Statistics, Census Bureau, industry analyses, and documented case studies from across the United States, this report provides a comprehensive picture of where American retail stands in mid-2026 and where it is headed.

## Part 1: Effects on Local Community Economies and Employment

### 1.1 Retail Employment: Two Decades of Restructuring

**Pre-2020 Trends (2006–2019)**

The U.S. retail sector experienced significant structural change well before the pandemic. According to the Bureau of Labor Statistics, retail employment grew from 14.4 million in 2010 to 15.8 million in 2017, before declining by roughly 200,000 jobs from 2017 to 2019. This pre-pandemic decline was driven by e-commerce competition, automation, and industry consolidation, with clothing stores and general merchandise stores bearing the brunt of the losses [BLS Beyond the Numbers, April 2022]. Department stores' share of U.S. retail sales fell from 14.5% in 1990 to below 1.8% by 2024, and over 50% of mall-based department store locations closed between 2017 and 2023 [Ankura.com].

**The Pandemic Shock (2020)**

The COVID-19 pandemic delivered a devastating blow to brick-and-mortar retail employment. The sector lost nearly 800,000 jobs in 2020 alone, with clothing stores, motor vehicle dealers, and miscellaneous retailers hit hardest. Essential goods retailers—warehouse clubs, supercenters, and grocery stores—actually saw employment gains, while e-commerce logistics boomed, with couriers and warehousing adding 300,000 jobs [BLS, April 2022].

**Post-Pandemic Recovery and Continued Decline (2021–2026)**

The recovery has been uneven. By 2024, retail employment had reached 16.3 million, up 7% from a 2010 low, but growth varied dramatically by subsector. Motor vehicle dealers (+24%), building materials (+23%), and non-store retailers (+9%) grew, while electronics and appliances stores continued to contract [USAFacts]. The BLS projects that retail trade will lose 587,000 jobs (-4.0%) from 2020 to 2030, with food and beverage stores (-257,500) and general merchandise stores (-233,500) accounting for 83.7% of projected losses. Nonstore retailers (+85,600) and motor vehicle dealers (+76,100) are expected to grow [BLS, April 2022].

The most recent BLS employment projections (August 2025) show retail trade declining by 181,900 jobs through 2034, even as total U.S. employment grows by 3.1% [BLS Employment Projections, August 28, 2025]. The June 2026 Employment Situation report shows total nonfarm payroll employment increasing by 57,000, with unemployment holding at 4.2%, but retail trade continues to face structural headwinds [BLS June 2026].

**Store Closures: The Numbers**

Store closures have accelerated dramatically. According to Coresight Research, U.S. retailers closed over 7,300 stores in 2024 (a 57% increase from 2023), followed by 8,270 closures in 2025 (against 5,270 openings, for a net loss of roughly 3,000 stores). Five bankrupt brands—Joann, Party City, Big Lots, New Rite Aid, and Forever 21—drove over half of all closure square footage in 2025 [Coresight Research, January 27, 2026]. However, the outlook for 2026 is more optimistic, with Coresight tracking 566 planned closures versus 1,118 planned openings [Coresight/Fox Baltimore, 2026].

The IHL Group offers a counter-narrative, arguing that the "retail apocalypse" narrative has been overblown. Their analysis shows that U.S. retail (excluding autos) grew from $3.6 trillion in 2016 to $5.7 trillion in 2024, with total store counts declining only 0.7% from 2016 to 2024. Food, drug, convenience, and mass retailers actually grew by 5.1%, while general merchandise/softlines declined 10.9% [IHL Group, May 2025].

### 1.2 The Human Cost: Wages, Job Quality, and Worker Displacement

The retail sector employed 17,069,353 people in 2024, with an average annual salary of $46,691—$23,187 below the national average. The workforce is 47.8% female and 52.2% male, with men earning 1.44 times more than women. The most common occupations are retail salespersons (2.79 million), first-line supervisors (2.56 million), and cashiers (2.06 million). Projected job growth over the next ten years is -1.17% [Data USA].

Academic research has documented the negative wage effects of e-commerce expansion. A 2022 NBER working paper by Chava, Oettl, Singh, and Zeng found that the opening of a major e-commerce fulfillment center in a county leads to a 2.4% decline in retail workers' income, with hourly and part-time workers experiencing a $825 annual drop driven by reduced hours. At the store level, sales fall 4%, employment drops 2.1%, and the probability of exit increases by 22%. Critically, while transportation and warehousing gain 256 jobs per county per quarter, and food services gain 143 jobs, these gains do not fully offset the 938 retail jobs lost [NBER Working Paper, May 2022].

The Census Bureau's Economic Census data (2017 to 2022) reveals the scale of the shift: electronics stores lost $9.0 billion in revenue (-12.0%) and 110,000 jobs (-40.8%); women's clothing stores lost 133,535 jobs (-38.7%); children's clothing stores lost 45,254 jobs (-58.6%). Meanwhile, electronic shopping and mail-order houses saw revenue increase by $546.7 billion (+106.0%) and employment grow by 1.2 million (+215.3%) [Census Bureau, Economic Census 2017–2022].

### 1.3 Impact on Local Government Revenues

The decline of brick-and-mortar retail has direct fiscal consequences for local governments. Shopping centers generate $167.3 billion annually in sales taxes, which fund critical municipal services. The retail real estate industry generates $321.8 billion annually in state sales taxes and $68.8 billion in local property taxes, supporting 12.8 million U.S. jobs (1 in 11) [ICSC].

The Lincoln Institute of Land Policy documented the case of Bangor Mall in Maine, which once contributed $1.2 million annually in taxes. Its assessed value plummeted from $60.9 million in 2017 to $34.6 million in 2019 after losing anchor tenants like Macy's and Sears, and its tax bill dropped below $1 million for the first time in two decades. The U.S. has 8.5 billion square feet of retail space—24.5 square feet per capita, five times Europe's average of 4.5 square feet [Lincoln Institute of Land Policy, 2019].

The "dark store" property tax method has further strained municipal budgets. In Michigan, big-box retailers successfully appealed assessments by comparing their operational stores to vacant properties, achieving at least a 50% reduction in taxable value. Between 2013 and 2015, this practice cost Michigan local governments over $100 million in tax revenue, affecting 72% of counties [Michigan State University].

The Urban Institute reported in May 2025 that state tax revenue growth continues to lag, with sales tax revenues in the median state growing just 1.9% in nominal terms—a sign of persistent weakness in retail-driven tax bases [Urban Institute, Q4 2024].

### 1.4 Retail Deserts and Food Deserts

The closure of retail stores has created "retail deserts" that disproportionately affect low-income communities and communities of color. In Kansas City, the closure of two major grocery stores in 2025—the Sun Fresh at 31st Street and Prospect Avenue and the Merc Co+op—will force thousands of low-income residents to walk up to a mile or rely on corner stores for processed food. Current food desert patterns strongly correlate with 1930s HOLC redlining maps, with formerly redlined areas 107% to 149% more likely to be food deserts today [The Beacon, 2025].

Milwaukee is experiencing a severe acceleration of grocery store closures, with nine stores closing since 2021, including Walmart, Pick 'n Save, Aldi, and Sentry Foods. A University of Wisconsin-Milwaukee study found that predominantly white areas have more than three times the number of supermarkets compared to predominantly Black areas. The city has formally recognized "food apartheid" as a public health emergency [Milwaukee Journal Sentinel, February 2026].

Nationally, 39.4 million Americans live in low-access communities, and Black people are 2.49 times more likely than white people to live in such areas. In Memphis, 115,980 residents were food insecure in 2019, with a child food insecurity rate of 19.8% [University of Memphis].

### 1.5 Small Business and Local Economic Multipliers

The shift from local independent retailers to chains and online platforms has significant economic implications. The Institute for Local Self-Reliance found that local businesses generate 70% more local economic impact per square foot than chains. Spending $100 at a local independent business creates $68 in additional local economic activity, while spending $100 at a chain store creates only $43. A study of eight Ohio communities found that retail buildings produced an average net loss of $0.44 per square foot annually due to higher service costs [Institute for Local Self-Reliance].

A 2024 IZA Discussion Paper found that the opening of a Walmart Supercenter leads to a 2 percentage point (16%) increase in poverty, driven by declining annual earnings that persist for 10 years. The study estimated that Supercenters impose an average annual cost of $3 billion to public finances through increased transfer payments and reduced tax revenues [IZA Discussion Paper, September 2024].

## Part 2: The Current State and Fate of Vacant Retail Spaces

### 2.1 The Mall Landscape: A Tale of Two Tiers

The U.S. mall industry has bifurcated into a clear two-tier system. Premiere Class A malls (e.g., Houston Galleria, King of Prussia, Mall of America) maintain low vacancy rates of around 5.6% and strong traffic, while Class C malls suffer vacancy rates of 13.3% or higher [Matthews Report, 2025]. As Chris Conlon, former CEO of Washington Prime Group, explained: "Over the years, as enclosed malls have evolved, it turns out that the A malls are doing extremely well, and C and D malls are accelerating to extinction."

From a peak of over 2,500 shopping malls in the early 1990s, the number has fallen dramatically. By 2025, approximately 1,200 malls remained, with projections that only 900 will be operating by 2028. Some projections suggest that up to 87% of large malls may close over a 10-year period [The Sun, 2025; Capital One Shopping Research].

**Mall Vacancy Rates Over Time**

The historical data shows a clear upward trend. The U.S. mall vacancy rate hit a two-decade high of 9.7% in early 2020, the highest since Reis's records began in 2000 when the rate was 5.3%. In Q3 2018, the rate was 9.1%, a seven-year high driven by closures from Sears, Bon-Ton, and others. By 2024, mall vacancy was approximately 8.7%, and the Matthews report showed it at 8.9% in 2025—the highest among all retail asset classes [Matthews, 2025; Financial Times].

However, more recent data from Colliers Q4 2025 (published January 2026) shows mall vacancy improving to 8.5%, down 10 basis points quarter-over-quarter. Cushman & Wakefield's Q2 2026 report shows national shopping center vacancy holding at 6.0%, still well below the 7.4% historical average, with asking rents reaching $25.65 per square foot nationally, up 2.2% year-over-year [Cushman & Wakefield, Q2 2026].

### 2.2 Strip Malls and Neighborhood Centers: Resilience Through Essential Services

While enclosed malls have struggled, strip malls and neighborhood shopping centers have proven far more resilient. The Matthews 2025 Unanchored Strip Center Report found that unanchored strip centers (10,000–50,000 square feet) are "stealing the spotlight in 2025" with national vacancy of just 4.5%, average asking rent rising from $17.10 in Q1 2019 to $20.85 in Q2 2025, and projected rent growth of 2% in 2025 [Matthews, 2025].

According to CBRE Investment Management (May 2025), neighborhood and community center retail vacancy rates remained near a historic low of 5.6% as of Q1 2025, with above-trend asking rent growth of 3.1% per year projected over the next five years [CBRE Investment Management, May 2025].

Grocery-anchored centers have been the strongest performers. JLL's Grocery Report 2025 found that grocery-anchored retail vacancy compressed to 3.5% in Q4 2024, 100 basis points below its pandemic peak, with rents rising 3.1% year-over-year. Investment in grocery-anchored properties reached $7.0 billion in 2024 [JLL, 2025].

### 2.3 Anchor Store Collapse: The Numbers

The department store anchor has been the most devastating casualty of the retail transformation. Sears went from 2,705 stores to just five remaining locations. Former Sears executive Mark Cohen told CNN: "Someone unlocks the door in the morning and locks it at night, but there is actually nothing to sell in the stores." Macy's announced plans to close approximately 150 underperforming stores under its "Bold New Chapter" plan, with 66 named by 2025. JCPenney survived a 2020 bankruptcy but continues to shrink. Bed Bath & Beyond filed for bankruptcy in April 2023 and closed all 360 stores plus 120 buybuy BABY locations [CNN; Macy's Investor Relations].

In 2025, the bankruptcies continued: Joann Fabrics closed all 800+ stores (19,000 employees) by May 31, 2025, ending 80 years of business. Party City closed all 700+ stores. Forever 21 filed for bankruptcy on March 17, 2025, and announced all U.S. locations would close. Rite Aid's second Chapter 11 liquidation eliminated all 1,240 stores [Coresight Research, January 2026; The Sun, 2025].

The defining department-store bankruptcy of 2026 was Saks Global's Chapter 11 filing in January 2026, with a post-closure footprint of about 13 Saks Fifth Avenue, 32 Neiman Marcus, and 2 Bergdorf Goodman stores, representing a 5.6 million square foot reduction [MMCG Investment, 2026].

### 2.4 Mall REIT Bankruptcies and Financial Distress

Three major mall REITs filed for Chapter 11 bankruptcy during or shortly after the pandemic:

**Washington Prime Group (WPG)** filed in June 2021, operating 102 malls across the U.S. Annual cash flow nearly halved from $263 million in 2015 to $122 million in 2018, before falling to just $34 million in 2019. Rent collection fell to just 52% in Q2 2020. WPG emerged from bankruptcy but then began selling its remaining properties, with roughly $1 billion in sales over several years. As of 2025, WPG faced nearly $1.1 billion in CMBS debt maturing between May and November 2025, and layoffs began June 2, 2025, affecting 139 employees [SEC Filings; Crain's Cleveland Business].

**CBL Properties** filed for Chapter 11 on November 1, 2020, owning 108 properties across 26 states. Its restructuring plan converted about $1.4 billion in unsecured notes. Unlike WPG, CBL has stabilized: as of Q2 2025, portfolio occupancy was 88.8% (up 10 bps year-over-year), and the company acquired four enclosed regional malls from WPG for $178.9 million [CBL Properties Q2 2025 Earnings].

**PREIT (Pennsylvania Real Estate Investment Trust)** filed for Chapter 11 in 2020 and again in December 2023, highlighting the ongoing challenges facing the sector [Crain's Cleveland Business].

### 2.5 Geographic Variation: Sun Belt vs. Rust Belt

The retail real estate market shows stark geographic bifurcation. Sun Belt markets significantly outperform the Rust Belt and Northeast in terms of rent growth, vacancy, and investor interest. According to the McGin 2025 Retail Investment Benchmarks report, rent growth is strongest in Raleigh (+7.5% YoY), Tampa (+4.7%), Dallas-Fort Worth (+4.4%), Atlanta (+4.2%), and Phoenix (+3.8%). Northeast and Midwest markets lag [McGin, 2025].

The Marcus & Millichap 2026 National Retail Index ranks Charlotte, Raleigh, Fort Lauderdale, Charleston, and West Palm Beach as the top-performing metros, driven by strong in-migration, household formation, and low vacancy. Gateway markets like San Francisco and Chicago are showing improving fundamentals but remain below Sun Belt peers [Marcus & Millichap, 2026].

The Southeast Retail Market Overview from Matthews details the region's transformation: Atlanta's retail vacancy has remained below 4% since early 2021—90 basis points lower than the national average of 4.7%. Miami has 2.7% vacancy, Tampa 3.0%, Nashville 3.1%, and the Carolinas 2.7% [Matthews, 2025].

### 2.6 Backfill and Re-Leasing: The Silver Lining

Despite the massive wave of closures, much of the space has been rapidly re-leased. As Neil Saunders noted: "There have certainly been some high-profile failures this year, but a lot of space that's come on the market has been quickly re-leased. Vacancy rates remain relatively low."

Following Bed Bath & Beyond's bankruptcy, Burlington took over 44 former locations. Other chains such as Michaels, Barnes & Noble, Ollie's Bargain Outlet, and HomeGoods have filled vacancies. Rents are up to 50% lower than what Bed Bath & Beyond paid, but landlords are often securing higher new leases—Kimco Realty reported a 38% increase in new-lease cash spreads. The off-price segment is particularly active: T.J. Maxx plans to open 2,000 new stores, and visits to thrift stores are up 39.5% compared to Q2 2019 [MMCG Investment, 2026; Retail Dive].

The best-positioned landlords, particularly grocery-anchored REITs like Brixmor and Kimco, have used the bankruptcy wave to achieve record rent spreads. Brixmor posted 38.7% new-lease cash spreads, and Kimco reported 90% re-leasing of bankrupt spaces [MMCG Investment, 2026].

## Part 3: Shifts in Consumer Shopping Behavior

### 3.1 E-Commerce Penetration: The Long Arc

The growth of e-commerce as a share of total retail sales has been one of the most significant economic transformations of the 21st century. According to the U.S. Census Bureau's Quarterly Retail E-Commerce Sales report:

- **2006**: E-commerce accounted for 2.8% of total retail sales ($107 billion)
- **2010**: 4.28% ($166.5 billion)
- **2015**: 7.1%
- **2019**: Approximately 11% ($576.5 billion)
- **2020**: 14.0% ($815.4 billion)—a 43% single-year surge
- **2022**: 15.3% (first year of slight decline as stores reopened)
- **2024**: 16.1% ($1.19 trillion)
- **Q1 2026**: 16.9% ($326.7 billion), up from 16.0% in Q1 2025

The pandemic caused an unprecedented acceleration. In Q2 2020, the e-commerce share jumped from 11.8% to 16.1% in a single quarter—a 3-percentage-point leap that would have taken years under normal circumstances. While some sources (e.g., Digital Commerce 360, ExportUSA) report higher penetration figures of 22–23% by using a narrower denominator that excludes categories like food services and fuel, the Census Bureau's official figure of 16.9% includes all retail categories [Census Bureau; Digital Commerce 360].

Academic research on whether the pandemic caused a permanent structural shift is divided. A 2024 study using structural break detection found that "while e-commerce activities surged during lockdown, they have predominantly reverted to pre-pandemic patterns," cautioning against overreaction [Modern Finance, 2024]. However, a 2025 study using PACF and ARIMA approaches found that "the pandemic caused a permanent structural shift toward e-commerce" and that "when physical stores reopened, e-commerce sales did not decline to pre-pandemic levels" [MDPI Systems, 2025].

### 3.2 The Omnichannel Revolution: BOPIS, Curbside, and Same-Day Delivery

The rise of Buy Online, Pick Up In Store (BOPIS) and curbside pickup has been one of the most dramatic shifts in consumer behavior. Pre-pandemic in 2019, only 4% of major retailers offered curbside pickup. By mid-2020, that figure had soared to 58%. Today, 87% of U.S. retailers offer a BOPIS option [FitForCommerce; eMarketer].

Click-and-collect sales grew from $35 billion in 2019 to $72.5 billion in 2020 (a 104.5% increase), and are projected to exceed $150 billion in 2025, accounting for 9.6% of total e-commerce sales. Seven multichannel retailers—Walmart, Home Depot, Best Buy, Target, Lowe's, Macy's, and Nordstrom—accounted for 64% of U.S. click-and-collect sales in 2020, collectively exceeding $46 billion [Business Insider; eMarketer].

**Key BOPIS Statistics:**
- 67% of U.S. shoppers have used BOPIS
- 50% of shoppers have chosen a retailer based on BOPIS availability
- 49–85% of BOPIS shoppers make additional purchases when picking up orders in-store
- Millennials (39.1%) and Gen Z (35.9%) are the largest user groups
- 53% of customer orders are ready within an hour of being placed [Capital One Shopping Research; Apexon; Ryder]

Target has been a leader in omnichannel fulfillment: nearly all of its ~2,000 stores offer free same-day Order Pickup and Drive Up, and 80% of online orders are fulfilled within one day via same-day services. Target's "stores-as-hubs" model cuts fulfillment costs by 30% [Target Corporate].

### 3.3 The Experiential Retail Transformation

Physical stores have undergone a fundamental transformation from purely transactional spaces to experiential destinations, community hubs, and fulfillment centers. The experiential retail market was valued at $133.33 billion in 2025, growing at a 14.9% CAGR, with North America accounting for 31.1% of incremental growth [Technavio, 2025].

According to a Forbes analysis, physical retail now serves 14 strategic roles beyond just sales, including: communicating brand missions, brand storytelling, omnichannel support, showrooming, risk-free product sampling, education and entertainment, community building, lifestyle hangouts, media channels and Instagramability, customization and personalization, and e-commerce fulfillment [Forbes, July 2025].

**Company Examples:**

Nike operates a multi-format experiential retail strategy: Nike House of Innovation (55,000+ sq ft flagships), Nike Rise (local sports hubs, ~15,000 sq ft), and Nike Live (small-format, data-driven, ~5,000 sq ft). The Soho NYC flagship features a basketball half-court, enclosed soccer trial area, treadmill with jumbotron, customization bar, and embedded touchscreens fully connected to the Nike app. Nike's direct-to-consumer business reached 34.8% of revenue in fiscal 2020, and the company plans to open 150–200 new stores [Modern Retail; Future Stores].

Apple stores are designed as "public places" that feel "more like a great library, which has natural light, and it feels like a gift to the community" according to Ron Johnson, the former chief architect. They serve as risk-free product sampling locations with seamless omnichannel integration [Forbes; NewStore].

Best Buy differentiates through service (Geek Squad, in-home setup) for complex electronics, turning stores into showrooms. Its marketplace, launched in August 2025, now has 1,100 sellers and 11 times the SKU count, with positive impact on gross profit rate [Modern Retail, NRF 2026].

### 3.4 Subscription and Loyalty Programs

**Amazon Prime** remains the dominant force, with an estimated 184 million U.S. members and $44.9 billion in subscription revenue in 2024. Prime Day 2025 generated $24.1 billion in total U.S. e-commerce sales. Amazon Prime commands 68.4% ($42.0 billion) of total U.S. paid retail membership fee revenue in 2026—a $61.4 billion market [BusStats.com; Yaguara.co; Red Stag Fulfillment].

**Walmart+** has grown significantly since its September 2020 launch. By February 2025, 28% of U.S. consumers had a Walmart+ subscription, nearly double the 15% level four years prior. CIRP estimates 25 million U.S. members as of July 2025. Walmart+ accounts for 5.2% ($3.2 billion) of total U.S. paid retail membership fee revenue in 2026, with revenue per member of $114/year [PYMNTS, 2025; CIRP; BusStats.com].

A notable trend is the growth of dual membership: 87% of Walmart+ members also have Amazon Prime, and the share of consumers subscribing to both nearly doubled since 2021. Millennials lead the way, with 37% now holding both memberships. Dual subscribers spend an average of $110 per transaction, well above the $78 spent by Prime-only members and $75 by Walmart+-only members [PYMNTS, 2025].

**Costco** holds 9.0% ($5.5 billion) of total U.S. paid retail membership fee revenue, with 128 million members worldwide and revenue per member of $145/year [BusStats.com].

### 3.5 Generational Divide in Shopping Behavior

Consumer shopping behavior varies significantly across generations. Gen Z and Millennials are far more likely to embrace digital-first shopping, experiential retail, and sustainability concerns, while Baby Boomers remain more loyal to traditional in-store shopping.

**Gen Z (born 1996–2015):** 60% expect retail spaces to be fun and experiential. They are the heaviest users of social commerce, with 38% returning online purchases in-store. They are less likely to join paid loyalty programs—only 5% have Amazon Prime compared to 35% of Gen X.

**Millennials (born 1977–1995):** The largest user group for BOPIS (39.1%), most likely to hold dual Amazon Prime and Walmart+ memberships (37%), and the most digitally engaged across all channels.

**Gen X (born 1965–1976):** Highest Amazon Prime penetration (35%), value-driven shoppers who research online before purchasing in-store.

**Baby Boomers (born 1946–1964):** Most loyal to traditional in-store shopping, highest Costco membership rates, and least likely to use BOPIS or curbside pickup [Center for Generational Kinetics; Capital One Shopping Research; PYMNTS, 2025].

### 3.6 The Shift from Goods to Services

A notable post-pandemic trend is the shift in consumer spending from goods back to services. During the pandemic, spending on goods surged as consumers redirected money from travel, dining, and entertainment. By 2023–2024, this trend reversed as services spending recovered. However, the permanent elevation of e-commerce penetration suggests that some of the pandemic-era shift to online goods shopping is here to stay, even as consumers also return to in-person experiences [Federal Reserve; Bureau of Economic Analysis].

## Part 4: Creative, Community-Driven Repurposing of Abandoned Retail Properties

The repurposing of vacant retail spaces has become one of the most innovative and impactful responses to the retail transformation. From housing to healthcare, education to entertainment, communities across America are finding creative second lives for dead malls and empty big-box stores.

### 4.1 Housing Conversions: From Shopping to Living

**The Arcade Providence, Rhode Island** is the oldest indoor shopping mall in the United States (built 1828). It was renovated into 48 micro-apartments (micro-lofts) on the second and third floors, with 17 small retail spaces remaining on the first floor. Units range from 225 to 775 square feet and rent for $800 to $1,800 per month. The project won a National Preservation Honor Award and has become a national model for adaptive reuse [Northeast Collaborative Architects; CNU].

**Skyview Park Apartments, Irondequoit, New York** converted the former Sears department store at the Irondequoit Mall into 157 affordable senior housing units. The Sears box-store was retrofitted for 73 apartments, with a new four-story building adding 84 units. 78 apartments are reserved for seniors at risk of homelessness who receive on-site supportive services through Rochester General Hospital's ElderONE program. The total development cost was $44 million, funded through tax-exempt bonds, Low-Income Housing Tax Credits, and state subsidies [PathStone Development Corporation; NYS Homes and Community Renewal].

**Plankinton Clover Apartments at The Avenue, Milwaukee, Wisconsin** converted the Grand Avenue Mall's Plankinton Arcade into 50 apartment units with private glass-front veranda entrances converted from former storefronts. The first floor corridor now houses a pickleball court. Studios and one-bedrooms are listed for $1,315 and $1,485, respectively. The conversion went viral on TikTok in 2023 [Milwaukee Journal Sentinel].

**National Scale:** As of January 2022, at least 192 U.S. malls planned to add housing components, and 33 malls had built apartments since the pandemic began. Converting just 10% of the most suitable vacant strip mall space could create more than 700,000 homes. The U.S. faces a deficit of 4.5 million homes, and 70% of Americans live near underperforming malls [Urban Land Institute; Connor Strong].

### 4.2 Mixed-Use Redevelopment: The Vertical Village Model

**Crosstown Concourse, Memphis, Tennessee** is perhaps the most ambitious and successful adaptive reuse project in the United States. Originally built in 1927 as a Sears retail store and catalog order plant, the 1.5 million square foot building sat vacant for over 20 years after closing in 1993. A $200 million renovation from 2015 to 2017 transformed it into a "vertical village." The grand opening on August 19, 2017 saw 96% capacity, with over 200 residents, 41 tenant businesses, and 700+ new jobs.

The project includes 269 mixed-income apartments (20% affordable at 80% or below area median income), commercial, retail, education, and healthcare space. Key tenants include Church Health, St. Jude Children's Research Hospital, Crosstown High School (a public charter school), a YMCA, a 425-seat theater, a free art gallery, a radio station, the nation's largest private dental clinic, and various retail and restaurants. The project achieved LEED Platinum certification, making it the largest LEED Platinum-certified historic adaptive reuse project in the world. It has won over 30 awards, including an AIA Institute Honor Award for Architecture [Crosstown Arts; Kemmons Wilson Companies].

**Northgate Station, Seattle, Washington** (formerly Northgate Mall) opened in 1950 as the first post-World War II shopping mall in the United States. Since 2018, the 55-acre property has been undergoing major redevelopment into a mixed-use complex. The redevelopment includes the Kraken Community Iceplex (172,000 sq ft), which opened in September 2021 as the Seattle Kraken NHL team's practice facility. Simon Property Group broke ground in July 2024 on a 234-unit apartment building with 25,000 square feet of ground-floor retail. A 167-room Residence Inn by Marriott opened in Spring 2025. Complete plans include nearly 1,000 apartment units, two hotels, office, and retail space. Simon plans to invest $1.5 billion to add 2,000 hotel rooms and apartment units to its malls over five years [Simon Property Group; Seattle Times].

**Belmar, Lakewood, Colorado** transformed a failed mall into a 22-block walkable downtown with 2,000 residents and $850 million in investment. Nearly 60 former malls have been reconstructed as mixed-use communities, and 75 more are planned [CNU; Lincoln Institute of Land Policy].

### 4.3 Education and Healthcare Conversions

**ACC Highland Campus, Austin, Texas** (formerly Highland Mall) opened in 1971 as Austin's first enclosed suburban shopping mall. After steep decline, Austin Community College (ACC) partnered with RedLeaf Properties to purchase 1.3 million square feet of indoor space on 81 acres. Phase 1 opened in 2014, converting the former JCPenney into the ACCelerator, a 32,000-square-foot learning lab with over 600 computers. Phase 2, a 415,000-square-foot Building 2000, opened in 2021 and includes performing arts venues, a student-run restaurant, art galleries, music recording labs, nursing skills labs, culinary kitchens, and a television studio. The campus now serves 15,000 students annually. Student success data shows that those visiting ACCelerators seven or more times are six percentage points more likely to persist to the next semester [ACC; RedLeaf Properties].

**Jackson Medical Mall, Jackson, Mississippi** is a former shopping mall (originally opened 1970) that was converted in 1996 into a comprehensive healthcare complex, led by Dr. Aaron Shirley. The facility is now 98.9% occupied, generates 200,000 healthcare visits annually, sees approximately 5,000 visitors daily, and supports over 1,500 full-time equivalent jobs with an annual payroll exceeding $25 million. The economic impact is $354.1 million annually, supporting 2,568 employees. The 900,000-square-foot facility houses healthcare services, educational institutions, shops, and offices, with partners including Jackson State University, Tougaloo College, and the University of Mississippi Medical Center [Jackson Medical Mall Foundation].

**NexusPark, Columbus, Indiana** (formerly Fair Oaks Mall) is a collaborative project between the Columbus Parks and Recreation Department, the City of Columbus, and Columbus Regional Health to convert the former mall into a community sport, recreation, and wellness hub. The 400,000 square foot complex, purchased for $5.9 million in December 2021, includes an indoor sports fieldhouse, Columbus Regional Health medical and wellness offices (the "Health Hub" consolidating 15 physician practices), and an outdoor community park. The total project cost is $106.3 million [City of Columbus; Columbus Regional Health].

**Other notable education conversions:** Grand Rapids Community College Lakeshore Campus repurposed a 52,000 square foot retail space into a LEED-certified education hub. The former Eastgate Mall near Cincinnati now has classrooms in a former Sears, a library in a former JCPenney, and a food court as student union. The University of Arizona has a campus at The Bridges mall. Northern Virginia Community College has a medical training center in a former Macy's [Various sources].

### 4.4 Entertainment and Recreation Conversions

**WeStreet Ice Center, Tulsa, Oklahoma** is a $25 million, 140,000-square-foot dual-rink ice facility converted from a 30-year-old Macy's department store at the Promenade Mall. It serves as the home for the Tulsa Oilers hockey team. The project used innovative inverted bowstring trusses to create column-free spans of 112 feet over the rinks. The adaptive reuse provided a reduction in new construction concrete volume of 75% and steel tonnage of 60% compared to new construction. The facility includes two ice rinks (one with a 1,000-seat spectator area), a pro shop, arcade, simulation golf ranges, a full-service bar and grill, restaurant, and public spaces. The project was completed in about 18 months, with the grand opening on March 9, 2024 [Perkins&Will; Thompson Construction; Tulsa Oilers].

**Nickelodeon Universe at Mall of America, Bloomington, Minnesota** is a 7-acre indoor amusement park located in the center of the Mall of America. It features 5 roller coasters and over 27 attractions, operating year-round under a glass roof. A second Nickelodeon Universe opened at American Dream in East Rutherford, New Jersey on October 25, 2019, covering 8 acres with 5 roller coasters and over 35 rides and attractions, including the TMNT Shellraiser, hailed as the steepest roller coaster in the world when it opened [Mall of America; American Dream].

**Epic Entertainment, Northgate Mall, Colerain Township, Ohio** is a 58,000-square-foot indoor adventure park featuring a two-story rope course, multi-level go-kart track, trampoline zones, arcade machines, and six private party rooms. The owners purchased the former Xscape Theatres property for $1.8 million in December 2024 and plan to invest up to $3 million in renovations [Cincinnati Enquirer].

**Family Entertainment Trends:** Family entertainment concepts are expected to add 16.5 million square feet nationwide with 721 new locations. Escape and challenge rooms have seen 246% growth from 2023 to 2026, and competitive socializing has seen 83% growth. Operators are aggressively backfilling vacancies left by closed department stores and big-box retailers. Companies like Elev8 and Level99 are expanding, focusing on suburban areas and adult-oriented segments. As Greg Silvers, CEO of EPR Properties, stated: "Even in a challenged environment, we don't have to give up fun" [Moody's; EPR Properties].

### 4.5 Arts and Culture Conversions

**Goldmark Cultural Center, Dallas, Texas** is a converted 1970s office complex that has become a thriving creative hub for over 170 working artists, including painters, ceramicists, and photographers. It offers affordable studio rentals starting at $190/month for 70 square feet with 24/7 access. The center originated in 2017 when property trustee Chin-Chin Yeh and her assistant began cleaning the dilapidated complex, and a Dallas city official suggested renting to artists [Dallas Morning News].

**Zero Empty Spaces** is a Florida-based initiative that converts vacant commercial properties into affordable artist studios. Since launching in June 2019, the organization has placed more than 600 artists in 10 commercial buildings across 10 cities, primarily in Florida but also in Little Rock and Boston. Placements typically last about 18 months, with artists paying $2.50 to $4.50 per square foot (including utilities) for spaces of 80 to 250 square feet [Zero Empty Spaces].

**Lowe Mill ARTS & Entertainment, Huntsville, Alabama** is the largest privately-owned arts facility in the United States, housed in a historic textile mill. It features 153 working studios for over 300 artists and makers, 7 galleries, a theatre, community garden, and performance venues [Lowe Mill].

### 4.6 Agricultural and Food-Related Repurposing

Several former malls and big-box stores have been converted into indoor vertical farms, addressing food deserts while giving unused commercial buildings a second life. **Southland Mall in Memphis, Tennessee** now houses an indoor vertical farm growing leafy greens and herbs using hydroponics and LED lighting. **Northland Center in Southfield, Michigan** includes urban farming initiatives for tomatoes, peppers, and microgreens using aquaponics. **Valley View Center in Dallas, Texas** has repurposed sections for community farming and education. These farms use up to 95% less water than conventional agriculture while producing multiple harvests annually, and many are located near food deserts, reducing transportation costs and providing locally grown food within days of harvest [Various sources].

### 4.7 Municipal and Government Conversions

**Franklin, Tennessee** operated its City Hall inside a 1970s shopping mall for more than 40 years. The city is now building a new City Hall on the Square (completion 2027), which will be the first purpose-built City Hall on the Square since the 1890s. The redevelopment includes a one-acre public park, underground parking, retail space, and expanded public meeting spaces [City of Franklin].

**Centre of Tallahassee, Florida** (formerly Tallahassee Mall) has been partially repurposed into government facilities including the Department of State, Department of Children and Families, and the Department of Health [Tallahassee Democrat].

### 4.8 The Economics of Adaptive Reuse

According to a 2020 NAR Research Group survey of 2,684 commercial REALTORS, 31% of vacant malls were repurposed as retail, 16% as mixed-use, 9% as warehouses, and 7% as multifamily. The average acquisition cost was $80 per square foot (range $1–$417), the average discount from prior sale price was 43%, and the average vacancy before acquisition was 47 months. 63% required no rezoning [NAR Research Group, May 2020].

Conversion costs for housing range from $200 to $800 per square foot, depending on the complexity of the project. While this can be comparable to or slightly higher than new construction, the community benefits—preservation of historic structures, reduced environmental impact, and faster project timelines—often justify the investment [Urban Land Institute].

## Conclusion

The transformation of physical retail in the United States over the past two decades represents one of the most significant economic and social shifts in modern American history. The pandemic served not as the cause of this transformation but as its most powerful accelerant, compressing years of change into months.

The data is clear: e-commerce has grown from 2.8% of retail sales in 2006 to 16.9% in early 2026, and while growth has slowed from pandemic-era peaks, the structural shift appears permanent. Over 8,000 stores closed in 2025 alone, and mall vacancy rates remain above 8%. Employment in traditional retail is projected to continue declining, with the BLS forecasting 181,900 fewer retail jobs by 2034.

Yet the story is not simply one of decline. Strip malls and neighborhood centers anchored by essential services like groceries have proven remarkably resilient, with vacancy rates below 5%. The off-price and discount segments are expanding rapidly. Experiential retail is thriving, with stores evolving into destinations, fulfillment hubs, and community spaces. And the creative repurposing of abandoned retail properties—from the micro-apartments of the Arcade Providence to the vertical village of Crosstown Concourse to the ice rinks and indoor farms rising in former department stores—demonstrates the remarkable adaptability of American communities.

The future of American retail is not a binary choice between physical and digital, but an increasingly integrated omnichannel ecosystem where stores serve multiple roles: showroom, fulfillment center, community hub, and brand experience. The retailers and communities that thrive will be those that embrace this complexity, adapt to changing consumer expectations, and find creative ways to repurpose the physical spaces that have shaped American life for generations.
