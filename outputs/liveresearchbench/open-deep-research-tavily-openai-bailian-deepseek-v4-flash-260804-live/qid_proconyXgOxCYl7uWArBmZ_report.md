# Performance-Enhancing Drugs and Technologies in Elite Sports: A Comprehensive Global Analysis

## Introduction

The debate over whether performance-enhancing drugs and technologies (PEDs and PEDTs) should be allowed or banned in elite-level competitive sports represents one of the most contentious and complex issues in contemporary athletics. This report examines twelve distinct arguments—six supporting the legalization or regulation of PEDs/PEDTs, and six supporting their continued prohibition—drawing on academic research, case studies, expert testimony, statistical data, and industry reports from a global perspective. Each argument is presented with a clear thesis statement followed by multiple substantiated supporting points.

---

## PRO ARGUMENT 1: Athlete Autonomy and Bodily Freedom

**Thesis:** Adult athletes possess the same rights to bodily autonomy and self-determination as any other competent adult, and the current prohibitionist framework represents an unjustified paternalistic restriction on their personal liberty.

### Supporting Points

**1. Philosophical Foundations of Autonomy in Sport**

Bioethicist Julian Savulescu of the University of Oxford, the most prominent academic advocate for doping legalization, argues that performance enhancement is a fundamental expression of human nature. In his influential Aeon essay, he writes: "To be human is to be better. Humans are not like racehorses, flogged by the whip of the jockey: they are their own masters. The choice to be better is an expression of that, and so performance enhancement embodies the spirit of humanity" [1]. Savulescu has published extensively on this topic, including in the British Medical Journal (2004) and has founded the Oxford Uehiro Centre for Practical Ethics [2].

**2. The Incoherence of Current Anti-Doping Rules**

Savulescu critiques the arbitrary nature of current prohibitions, noting that caffeine is a performance-enhancer that used to be banned and is now allowed; creatine, beetroot extract, and many other legal substances enhance performance. He argues that if the criterion is simply "performance enhancement," then countless legal substances and practices would also need to be banned. He has stated: "The rules on doping in sport are incoherent – should we change them to allow the right kind of performance enhancement?" [3]

**3. Distinction Between Physiological and Mental Doping**

Savulescu's core argument distinguishes between physiological doping (e.g., steroids, EPO, blood doping) and mental doping. He argues that physiological doping simply optimizes natural processes and still requires extreme effort, courage, and mental endurance. He points to cyclists like Tyler Hamilton who pushed through pain with blood doping, arguing this still required courage and determination. He writes: "I see no good reason to ban physiological doping... mental doping undermines courage, determination and 'toughness'– our very nature as human agents – and should be banned" [1].

**4. The Enhanced Games as a Libertarian Alternative**

The Enhanced Games, launched in 2026 in Las Vegas, explicitly frames enhancement as a matter of individual freedom. Founder Aron D'Souza stated the event would "break world records and fundamentally change the trajectory of not just sport, but humanity as a whole" [4]. The event received backing from prominent libertarian and tech investors including Peter Thiel, Christian Angermayer, Donald Trump Jr., and the Winklevoss twins. The Vanity Fair article (2026) notes that the venture is part of a larger biohacking economy, with the Games aiming to sell peptides and supplements to the public [5]. Athlete Shania Collins stated: "I always thought: If it was done properly, why be against it? … It's the lying. The sneaking around. Transparency is huge to me" [5].

**5. Academic Support for the Autonomy Position**

The Oxford Practical Ethics paper "Ethics of Performance Enhancement in Sport: Drugs and Gene Doping" (Foddy & Savulescu, 2007, cited by 124+ sources) argues that "performance enhancement is inevitable and unpoliceable, that it is not against the spirit of sport and that we should remove" bans [6]. The Britannica pro-con article on sports and drugs presents the argument that "adults should have bodily autonomy and safe, regulated use" of PEDs, noting that columnist Maeve Juday has argued: "It's time to head in the opposite direction: legalizing performance enhancing drugs (PEDs)" [7].

**6. Counterpoint to the Paternalism Critique**

The Hastings Center for Bioethics notes that the therapy-enhancement distinction is blurry, and context matters—what is problematic in sport may be acceptable elsewhere. Thomas H. Murray (President Emeritus of The Hastings Center) highlights that "The HGH molecule neither knows nor cares whether it is helping a child inch towards normality or making a hugely muscled athlete even more muscular" [8]. The Outram review article (published in the American Medical Association Journal of Ethics, 2014) notes that "Several authors have suggested that, in the athletic context, health should be thought of as the ability to achieve one's reasonable goals" [9].

## PRO ARGUMENT 2: Leveling the Playing Field (Genetic Inequality)

**Thesis:** Elite sport has never been a level playing field because genetic inheritance creates massive natural inequalities—over 200 genetic polymorphisms linked to performance traits, with heritability estimated at >70% for elite athlete status. Since the "natural lottery" of genetics is inherently unfair, allowing PEDs could actually reduce these inequities rather than increase them.

### Supporting Points

**1. The Scale of Genetic Inequality in Sport**

The Genetic and Athletic Performance review (PMC, 2022) notes that nearly 200 genetic polymorphisms have been linked to performance traits, and over 20 may influence elite athlete status, with heritability estimated at >70% for becoming an elite athlete [10]. Twin studies show heritability above 50% for traits like VO₂max (up to 93%), anaerobic power, muscle fiber distribution, and motor learning. Meta-analyses show deliberate practice explains only 18% of sports performance variance [11].

**2. The Regulating Genetic Advantage Study**

The most directly relevant evidence comes from a study at Stanford Law School / Harvard Journal of Law & Technology (Fall 2018). The authors conducted two experimental studies with 1,000 US respondents examining public attitudes toward gene doping. The findings were striking: 79% of respondents support allowing genetically modified athletes with an advantage to compete alongside those born with that advantage; 54% support allowing modified athletes to compete alongside unmodified athletes; 34% endorse creating a separate competitive category. Only a small minority supported an outright ban. The study concludes: "These results are inconsistent with the protection of biological innateness. WADA need to acknowledge that genetic modification calls into question the foundations of their prohibition decisions. Our results are consistent both with increasing acceptance of genetic modification, and with theoretical and empirical work that equality of opportunity is best achieved through minimizing the role of luck. In this view, genetic modification is a tool to reduce the inequities of the natural lottery" [12].

**3. The Caster Semenya Case**

The Caster Semenya case directly illustrates the genetic inequality argument. Semenya, a two-time Olympic gold medalist in the 800m, has naturally high testosterone levels due to a Difference of Sex Development (DSD). The Court of Arbitration for Sport ruled in 2019 that she must take medication to suppress her testosterone to compete in events from 400m to one mile. The IAAF (now World Athletics) argued that DSD athletes have testosterone levels in the normal male range (7.7-29.4 nmol/L) compared to typical female levels (0.12-1.79 nmol/L), giving them a 1.8% to 4.5% competitive advantage. Semenya's legal team argued: "Her genetic gift should be celebrated, not discriminated against." Critics note that more than 200 genetic variations have been identified that provide an advantage in elite sport, yet only hyperandrogenism is singled out for regulation. The New York Times reported that forcing Semenya to lower her testosterone could slow her by up to seven seconds in the 800m [13].

**4. The Public Perception Gap**

The Regulating Genetic Advantage study found that "whereas the public sees drug doping as rightfully prohibited, they do not see genetic modification for performance enhancement in the same way. With the unlevel playing field of inherited genetic advantage laid bare, people do not support protecting natural talent. It becomes clear this protection amounts instead to the defense of at-birth genetic advantages" [12].

**5. The Inevitability of Gene Editing**

The New America article "We Are Not Ready to Deal With Gene-Edited Athletes" warns that "children whose genomes have been modified before birth... could be born in the next few years. Some of these individuals could conceivably be of age to compete in the 2040 Olympics." The article notes that historical precedents—state-sanctioned doping programs (e.g., East Germany, China, Russia) and athletes using untested performance enhancers (e.g., blood doping in the 1980s-90s)—suggest governments and wealthy parents may take similar risks with gene editing [14].

**6. Sport New Zealand's Scenarios**

The Sport New Zealand "Unlevel Playing Field" report (2026) examines the future of elite athlete classification and identifies key drivers: gene editing, cyborg technologies, politicization of sport, public acceptance of enhanced athletes, and market dynamics. It presents four scenarios for 2025-2070: (1) Maintenance of the level playing field; (2) Expanded categories for transgender, intersex, genetically modified, and technologically enhanced athletes; (3) End of the era for natural athletes, where enhancement becomes the norm; (4) Politicized performance enhancement fractures elite competition. The report states: "Given humanity now possesses the capacity and technology to pursue human enhancement, anti-doping agencies will be required to regulate not only performance enhancement, but also human enhancement" [15].

## PRO ARGUMENT 3: Enhanced Spectator Experience and Entertainment Value

**Thesis:** Sports are ultimately entertainment products, and fans consistently demonstrate through their viewing behavior that they prioritize exciting, record-breaking performances over the "purity" of competition. Evidence from multiple studies shows that doping scandals have minimal to no impact on viewership, and that fans continue to watch and support athletes and leagues even after major doping revelations.

### Supporting Points

**1. The Tour de France Viewership Study**

The most direct evidence comes from a study by Feddersen (2017), analyzing Danish TV viewership from 1993-2015 of 447 stage broadcasts. The study found that "the doping variable (indicating an in-race doping expulsion) is not statistically significant, indicating no short-term impact of doping scandals on TV audience size." The key drivers of viewership were stage type (mountain stages, individual time trials, and prologues), patriotism (a Danish rider wearing the yellow jersey adds ~120,000 viewers), and suspense (viewership increases by ~7,500 per stage). Year fixed effects showed no long-term negative trends after major doping scandals [16].

**2. The Nordic Ski World Championships Satisfaction Study**

A study published in the International Journal of Sports Marketing and Sponsorship (Volume 26, Issue 6, January 2025) examined 896 on-site spectators at the 2019 Nordic Ski World Championships during a doping scandal. Results showed that "the scandal did not affect the spectators' satisfaction and perception of the event" and that "spectator satisfaction remained quite stable while any negative impacts, if not directly affecting the spectator, seemed to be considered as somehow 'part of the event'" [17].

**3. Historical Doping and Tour de France Popularity**

Statista data (July 2025) highlights that 44 of the last 57 Tour de France winners have been found guilty of doping at some point, many retroactively stripped of their titles, yet the Tour remains one of the most-watched sporting events in the world [18]. Outside Online (2013) notes: "Amazingly, in spite of the doping scandal, even more Americans turned on the Tour the following year. An average of 343,000 people per day" watched [19]. The HAL-SHS research chapter (Andreff, 2016, cited by 53) concludes that "Tour de France attendance and TV audience do not seem to be seriously threatened and surely not definitely affected by recurring doping scandals" [20].

**4. The Social Science Research Network Study**

The Social Science Research Network study (Cisyk, 2017, cited by 51) found "little evidence in support of the main economic rationale for regulating athletic doping that doping reduces fan interest" [21].

**5. The Enhanced Games as a Market Test**

The Enhanced Games in Las Vegas (May 24, 2026) featured 42 athletes from 24 nations. Greek swimmer Kristian Gkolomeev unofficially broke the 50m freestyle world record (20.81 seconds, 0.07s under the record). The livestream peaked at 60,000 viewers but suffered technical issues. The Bangor University analysis concluded: "The ethical question – whether the risks are worth it – has been answered not by philosophers or regulators, but by the market and the score board" [22]. The article "Is the juice worth the squeeze?" in The Conversation (2026) notes that the Enhanced Games "produced ordinary results" and "the market and scoreboard have answered the ethical question" [23].

**6. Changing Fan Demographics and Expectations**

The Deloitte 2023 Sports Fan Insights survey (3,001 US sports fans ages 14+) found that the overall state of sports fandom is strong—over 75% of fans rated their fandom 7 or higher, and 90% said their fanhood has grown or stayed the same. Gen Z fans crave a social sports experience—61% watch live events with others, and nearly half use social media while watching [24]. The Ampere Analysis research (Minal Modha, 2025) shows that 45% of 18-to-24-year-olds enjoy watching sport—identical to the general population. Since 2016, the proportion of young sport fans has grown by 23%, with a 45% increase among young women. Sports documentaries like Netflix's 'Drive to Survive' have boosted F1's young fan share by 67% since 2017 [25].

## PRO ARGUMENT 4: Medical and Therapeutic Benefits

**Thesis:** Many substances on the WADA Prohibited List are legitimate medications with proven therapeutic applications. The current system creates a Catch-22 where athletes needing essential medical treatment risk their careers, and even when Therapeutic Use Exemptions (TUEs) are available, the process is burdensome, stigmatizing, and inconsistent across jurisdictions.

### Supporting Points

**1. The WADA TUE System Acknowledges Medical Necessity**

The WADA Therapeutic Use Exemption (TUE) system is explicitly designed to "ensure that athletes can be treated for medical conditions - even if the treatment involves using a prohibited substance or method – while avoiding the risk of being sanctioned." WADA states: "Athletes have an anti-doping right to medical treatment and protection of health" [26]. The International Testing Agency (ITA) confirms: "Athletes have an anti-doping right to medical treatment and protection of health. To uphold this right, an athlete can obtain a Therapeutic Use Exemption (TUE) which allows them to use a prohibited substance or method for legitimate health reasons in the context of sport if strict conditions are met" [27].

**2. The Scope of Medical Conditions Requiring TUEs**

The major medical conditions requiring TUEs include asthma (beta-2 agonists like salbutamol, formoterol), ADHD (methylphenidate, amphetamines), diabetes (insulin), growth hormone deficiency, hypogonadism (testosterone replacement), and various other endocrine, autoimmune, and cardiovascular conditions. The WADA Prohibited List includes substances that treat these conditions, and the TUE process exists to accommodate them [28].

**3. The Complexity and Burden of the TUE Process**

The TUE application process requires meeting four strict criteria: (1) The prohibited substance or method is needed to treat a diagnosed medical condition supported by relevant clinical evidence; (2) The therapeutic use will not, on the balance of probabilities, produce any additional enhancement of performance beyond what might be anticipated by a return to the athlete's normal state of health; (3) The prohibited substance or method is an indicated treatment and there is no reasonable permitted therapeutic alternative; (4) The necessity for use is not a consequence of prior use of a prohibited substance or method without a TUE [26]. The TUE process for national-level athletes (Sport Integrity Canada) involves seven steps: confirm exemption requirement, complete the TUE form, obtain a physician's letter, assemble a medical file using condition-specific checklists, submit the complete application, follow up, and receive a decision [29].

**4. The Blurry Line Between Treatment and Enhancement**

The Hastings Center for Bioethics notes that the therapy-enhancement distinction is inherently blurry. Thomas H. Murray writes: "The HGH molecule neither knows nor cares whether it is helping a child inch towards normality or making a hugely muscled athlete even more muscular" [8]. This blurriness creates genuine ethical dilemmas for athletes and physicians navigating the TUE system.

**5. The TUE Physician Guideline for Short Stature**

WADA's TUE Physician Guideline for short stature (2023) specifies that TUEs will only be granted to athletes whose height falls below 2.25 standard deviations on the growth curve, and treatment may continue only until the athlete reaches the 5th percentile of adult height. This policy was developed with input from international experts and a panel of leading endocrinologists, demonstrating that WADA acknowledges the medical necessity of these substances while attempting to draw boundaries [30].

**6. The Missouri Medicine Guidance**

The Missouri Medicine article (PMC, 2018) provides health professionals with essential anti-doping information. It notes that a substance or method is considered for the Prohibited List if it meets any two of three criteria: performance enhancement potential, health risk, or violation of the spirit of sport. The article includes a table of common medical diagnoses and corresponding prohibited substances (e.g., ADHD – methylphenidate; asthma – albuterol). It emphasizes: "The physician should exercise caution when prescribing medications to athletes. The first responsibility of the physician is the health and safety of the patient" [31].

## PRO ARGUMENT 5: Futility and Ineffectiveness of the War on Doping

**Thesis:** The global anti-doping system, costing hundreds of millions of dollars annually, has proven fundamentally ineffective at detecting or deterring doping, with detection rates below 1% while anonymous surveys suggest actual usage rates of 14-57% among elite athletes, making the current prohibitionist approach an expensive and largely symbolic failure.

### Supporting Points

**1. The Cost of the Anti-Doping System**

Maennig (2014) estimates the global anti-doping system costs at least $228 million per year, mostly to cover the cost of performing about 270,000 doping tests [32]. WADA's 2025 budget was approved at USD 57.5 million, with the US contributing approximately $3.625 million annually [33]. WADA's core funding is sourced equally from the Olympic Movement and Governments of the world [34]. Since its 1999 inception with USD 15.5 million, WADA's operating income has grown substantially, and since 2001, more than USD 93 million has been committed to scientific research grants [35].

**2. The Massive Disparity Between Detection Rates and Actual Usage**

WADA's 2023 testing figures show 238,827 tests for Olympic-sport athletes, with 1,373 positive findings (0.57%) and 359 atypical findings (0.15%), totaling 1,732 needing follow-up (0.73%) [36]. However, anonymous surveys paint a dramatically different picture. A 2011 WADA-commissioned study found that at the World Championships in Athletics (WCA), 43.6% of 1,203 athletes admitted doping in the previous 12 months. At the Pan-Arab Games (PAG), 57.1% of 965 athletes admitted doping. This compared to under 0.5% and 4% detected by biological tests [37]. A 2022 Commonwealth Games survey of 915 athletes found that 21% admitted to doping in the previous 12 months (low estimate 15%, high estimate 27%) [38]. Research studies estimate doping prevalence within a 12-month period to be between 20% and 62% [39].

**3. The Cat-and-Mouse Dynamic**

The USADA report on Lance Armstrong (2012) documented that Armstrong evaded detection through three methods: avoiding testers, using undetectable drugs (e.g., blood doping, testosterone patches), and advanced techniques like saline infusions to dilute results [40]. The report noted that the test at the time was insufficiently sensitive and Armstrong used sophisticated masking techniques. Hermann and Henneberg (2014) demonstrate that a single random test has only a 2.9% chance of detecting doping (assuming 48-hour window, 40% sensitivity, continuous doping). With 12 tests per year, the probability of detecting continuous doping is about 33%, meaning a 66% chance of escaping detection [41]. The article concludes: "The continuing feedback of increasingly sophisticated testing approaches and increasingly clever methods of cheating results in ongoing scandals and, possibly, a culture of 'doping' as the only way to win" [42].

**4. The 1998 Festina Affair as a Case Study**

During the 1998 Tour de France, 108 doping controls were carried out, and every single one came back negative. No EPO test existed at the time. In 2004, a French laboratory retested 60 surviving urine samples from the 1998 Tour using detection methods that finally worked for EPO. 44 of them came back positive, including race winner Marco Pantani. Of the 38 tested riders, 35 (92%) were found to have used EPO [43]. The Festina scandal led directly to the creation of WADA in 1999, yet the fundamental problem of detection lagging behind doping innovation persists.

**5. The Sochi Olympics State-Sponsored Doping (Only Caught by a Whistleblower)**

The McLaren Report documented that Russia's Ministry of Sport, the FSB, and the Moscow laboratory operated a "state-directed failsafe system" using the "disappearing positive methodology" from at least 2011 to 2015 [44]. The Sochi Laboratory used a unique sample swapping methodology, coordinated by the Ministry of Sport, FSB, and CSP, to replace dirty urine samples with clean ones pre-stored in a freezer. The FSB developed a method to surreptitiously open tamper-evident bottles using small metal rods. Forensic evidence included scratches on bottle caps, DNA analysis showing male DNA in female athlete samples, and salt concentrations beyond physiological limits [45]. None of the athletes were caught doping at the time. Russia won the most medals at Sochi, easily surpassing the United States [46].

**6. Academic Studies on Anti-Doping Ineffectiveness**

The WADA Working Group Report (2013) acknowledged that despite increased testing (from ~150,000 to ~250,000 annually), the percentage of adverse analytical findings has remained below 1% and has not improved since 1985. The report noted: "The primary reason for the apparent lack of success of the testing programs does not lie with the science involved. The real problems are the human and political factors" [47]. Lauritzen and Solheim (2024) found that a 35% increase in samples from 2012 to 2019 only produced a 6% increase in Adverse Analytical Findings, with the detection ratio below 1% (0.66% for 2013-2019). They recommend "test smarter, not more" [48]. Pielke Jr. and Boye (2019) identified four areas of concern: unreliable doping prevalence estimates (e.g., a 50% prevalence study suppressed for six years), arbitrary inclusion of substances on the Prohibited List, false accusations, and procedural bias against athletes [49].

## PRO ARGUMENT 6: Harm Reduction and Safety Through Regulation

**Thesis:** The current prohibitionist approach drives PED use underground without medical supervision, creating far greater health risks than a regulated system would. A harm reduction model—similar to supervised injection sites for drug users—would allow medical oversight, quality control, and education, potentially saving lives and reducing the severe health consequences of unregulated PED use.

### Supporting Points

**1. The Scale of Unregulated PED Use**

An estimated 2.9–4 million Americans use anabolic-androgenic steroids (AAS) in their lifetime. Worldwide, the lifetime prevalence of AAS use is estimated at 1-5%. Several case series of male gym attendees found the prevalence of AAS use to be 15-30% in this population [50]. The vast majority of PED users are not elite athletes but rather nonathlete weightlifters and fitness enthusiasts, and the adverse health effects of PED use are greatly underappreciated. With at least 3 million PED users in the U.S. alone, PED use ranks ahead of type 1 diabetes and HIV infection in prevalence, yet the resources allocated to address it as a public health problem are negligible in comparison [51].

**2. Athlete Deaths from Unregulated PED Use**

The Mayo Clinic reports that erythropoietin (EPO), which increases red blood cells to improve oxygen delivery, is linked to at least 18 deaths in cyclists, with risks of stroke, heart attack, and pulmonary embolism [52]. Between 1987, shortly after athletes are believed to have begun using EPO, and 1990, 20 young Belgian and Dutch cyclists died [53]. A May 2025 study in the European Heart Journal examined mortality among 20,286 male IFBB professional bodybuilders (2005-2020). It found 121 deaths, including 73 sudden deaths and 46 sudden cardiac deaths (SCD), with 11 occurring in active competitors (mean age 34.7). SCD incidence was 32.83 per 100,000 athlete-years—far above typical young athlete rates. Professionals had a 523% higher SCD risk than amateurs [54].

**3. The Harm Reduction Model from Other Domains**

Supervised injection sites (SIS) have demonstrated measurable success in reducing harm. A literature review found that SISs reduce overdose deaths by 35% near Vancouver's site; prevent 5-6 HIV infections per year in Vancouver and 8-13 per facility per year in Toronto and Ottawa; prevent 35-55 hepatitis C infections per facility per year. SISs lead to decreases in public drug use, publicly discarded syringes, injection-related litter, and drug-related crime—a 28% reduction in public over-sedation in Boston. Cost-effectiveness analysis shows San Francisco's hypothetical SIS would save $2.33 per $1 spent (net $2.2-$3.5 million annually); Seattle's would save $4.22 per $1 ($534,453 annually); Baltimore's would save $7.8 million vs. $1.8 million cost [55].

**4. The "Systematic Doping as Harm Reduction" Argument**

The review article "Risk and enabling environments in sport: Systematic doping as harm reduction" (International Journal of Drug Policy, 2020) argues that "Restrictive anti-doping environments produce a range of risks for people who use these substances and may prevent harm reduction." The article notes that "Anti-doping policy discourages research using performance enhancing drugs (PEDs) or methods and prohibits athlete support personnel, including healthcare providers, from providing advice, assistance, or aid to an athlete or others seeking to use, or using PEDs until harm has occurred." From a public health perspective, "this is problematic since the high prevalence of uncontrolled, medically unsupervised doping practiced in amateur sports and doping-like behaviour in the general population (substance use for performance enhancement outside sport) exposes greater numbers of people to potential harm" [56].

**5. The East German Doping Program as a Warning**

The state-sponsored doping program of East Germany (GDR) from 1968 to the late 1980s demonstrates the dangers of unregulated, coerced doping. Approximately 9,000 athletes were systematically given anabolic steroids (Oral-Turinabol) to achieve international athletic success, particularly in swimming. Athletes were told the pills were "vitamins" and forbidden from discussing them. The program caused severe side effects including liver damage, infertility, birth defects, and hormonal changes. The training motto at the pool was "You eat the pills, or you die." Many athletes complied out of fear or pressure, but some resisted. Coaches and doctors later expressed regret, comparing their obedience to that of Nazi-era medical professionals [57]. This case illustrates the catastrophic consequences when doping is conducted without transparency, consent, or medical oversight.

**6. The Call for a Regulated Market**

Savulescu has argued for a regulated doping market where athletes would receive medical supervision. In a Der Spiegel interview (2012), he stated: "One molecule of a growth hormone could not harm you, neither could two molecules. Finding and administering the safe dose is precisely what would be done in an open doping market" [58]. The NPR/CBC article on Savulescu reports that he has long advocated for PEDs to be legalized and regulated, arguing it would make sport fairer and safer [59]. The New York Times Room for Debate piece (2012) by Savulescu is titled "Permit Doping So We Can Monitor It," arguing for a regulated system rather than prohibition [60].

## CON ARGUMENT 1: Health Risks and Athlete Safety

**Thesis:** Performance-enhancing drugs cause severe, long-term health damage to athletes, and banning them protects athletes from harming themselves in a competitive arms race where the pressure to win can override rational risk assessment.

### Supporting Points

**1. Anabolic-Androgenic Steroids (AAS) — Cardiovascular Damage**

The National Institute on Drug Abuse (NIDA) reports that anabolic-androgenic steroids are the best-studied class of appearance and performance-enhancing drugs (APEDs). Anabolic steroids can cause severe, long-lasting, and in some cases, irreversible damage. They can lead to early heart attacks, strokes, liver tumors, kidney failure, and psychiatric problems. Steroid use has been associated with high blood pressure; decreased function of the heart's ventricles; and cardiovascular diseases such as heart attacks, artery damage, and strokes, even in athletes younger than 30. A review of the research suggests that about 32% of people who misuse anabolic steroids become dependent [61].

**2. The Scale of the Public Health Problem**

The Endocrine Society Scientific Statement (PMC) reports that despite the high prevalence of PED use, media attention has focused almost entirely on PED use by elite athletes to illicitly gain a competitive advantage, and not on the health risks of PEDs. There is a widespread misperception that PED use is safe or that adverse effects are manageable. In reality, the vast majority of PED users are not athletes but rather nonathlete weightlifters, and the adverse health effects of PED use are greatly underappreciated. With at least 3 million PED users in the U.S. alone, PED use ranks ahead of type 1 diabetes and HIV infection in prevalence, and yet the resources allocated to address PED use as a public health problem are negligible in comparison with these diseases. Applying a random-effects model to 10 studies, the analysis yielded an estimate that 32.5% of AAS users develop AAS dependence. Applying this proportion, in the United States alone, about 1 million men have experienced AAS dependence at some time [62].

**3. Specific Organ System Damage**

The StatPearls article on Anabolic Steroid Use Disorder (last updated March 6, 2026) states that anabolic-androgenic steroid use has emerged as a significant public health concern due to the expansion of use beyond professional athletes to broader populations. Withdrawal manifestations include fatigue, depressed mood, anhedonia, insomnia, libido changes, severe depression, and suicidality. Concentric left ventricular hypertrophy is frequently observed in individuals with prolonged exposure to AAS, even after discontinuation of the inciting agent. Approximately 71% of individuals exposed to AAS exhibit impaired cardiac contractility, resulting in reduced left ventricular ejection fraction [63].

The International Journal of Impotence Research (2026) review summarizes that nonmedical AAS use is a global public health concern, with a lifetime prevalence of 3.3% (6.4% in males) and an estimated 2.9–4 million Americans aged 13–50 having used AAS, including ~1 million with dependence. AAS suppress the hypothalamic-pituitary-gonadal axis, causing hypogonadism, reduced libido (up to 31% of users), erectile dysfunction (up to 19%), gynecomastia (39.2% in a surgical cohort), and infertility (oligozoospermia/azoospermia). Recovery after cessation is variable: endocrine parameters often normalize within months, but spermatogenesis may take 6–24 months, with incomplete recovery in some prolonged/high-dose users. Beyond reproductive effects, AAS are associated with multi-system toxicity: cardiovascular (left ventricular remodeling, hypertrophy, coronary atherosclerosis, increased risk of MI, arrhythmias, and heart failure), hepatic (cholestasis, peliosis, hepatic adenomas, especially with 17α-alkylated oral agents), hematologic (erythrocytosis, prothrombotic changes), musculoskeletal (tendon rupture due to muscle-tendon mismatch), and neuropsychiatric (depression, anxiety, irritability) [64].

**4. EPO Deaths in Cycling**

The Mayo Clinic reports that erythropoietin (EPO), which increases red blood cells to improve oxygen delivery, is linked to at least 18 deaths in cyclists, with risks of stroke, heart attack, and pulmonary embolism [52]. An estimated 20 European cyclists are believed to have died as a result of blood doping over the past 25 years [65]. Between 1987, shortly after athletes are believed to have begun using EPO, and 1990, 20 young Belgian and Dutch cyclists died [53]. The McGill University article notes that "a rare genetic mutation, as seen in Olympic skier Eero Manytrata, can cause constantly active EPO signaling, boosting oxygen-carrying capacity by 25-50% and providing a significant athletic advantage" [66].

**5. The East German Doping Catastrophe**

The East German state-sponsored doping program from 1968 to the late 1980s systematically administered anabolic steroids (Oral-Turinabol) to approximately 9,000 athletes. Athletes were told the pills were "vitamins" and forbidden from discussing them. The program caused severe side effects including liver damage, infertility, birth defects, and hormonal changes. The training motto at the pool was "You eat the pills, or you die." Many athletes complied out of fear or pressure. Coaches and doctors later expressed regret, comparing their obedience to that of Nazi-era medical professionals. One doctor stated: "In Nazi Germany we all did what we were told to do. The GDR doping machine was no different; we were just carrying out our medical orders, never questioning the system that was good to us, just doing our jobs. Have we not learned anything?" [57]

**6. The Goldman Dilemma and Athlete Risk-Taking**

The World Psychiatry review (2007) reports that a study found that 98% of athletes responded "Yes" to the question: "If you were given a performance enhancing substance and you would not be caught and win, would you take it?" More than 50% said "Yes" to the question: "If you were given a performance enhancing substance... then die, would you take it?" This is known as the "Goldman dilemma" [67]. This finding demonstrates that athletes, under competitive pressure, may make decisions that are not in their long-term health interests, supporting the paternalistic argument for protective regulation.

## CON ARGUMENT 2: Integrity of Sport and Fair Play

**Thesis:** Performance-enhancing drugs fundamentally undermine the integrity of competition by creating an uneven playing field and rewarding those willing to cheat, rather than those who train hardest, thereby destroying the fundamental premise of sport as a test of human excellence.

### Supporting Points

**1. The WADA Code and the Principle of Fair Play**

The World Anti-Doping Code (2021) states that its purposes are: "To protect the Athletes' fundamental right to participate in doping-free sport and thus promote health, fairness and equality for Athletes worldwide, and To ensure harmonized, coordinated and effective anti-doping programs at the international and national level with regard to the prevention of doping." The Code establishes that the "spirit of sport is the celebration of the human spirit, body and mind. It is the essence of Olympism and is reflected in the values we find in and through sport, including: Health, Ethics, fair play and honesty, Athletes' rights... Doping is fundamentally contrary to the spirit of sport" [68]. The Code applies strict liability: athletes are automatically responsible for any prohibited substance found in their sample, regardless of intent.

**2. The Fairness Argument in Practice**

The Britannica Sports and Drugs debate notes that the thing that makes sports fantastic is watching competition on a level playing field. As Olympic shot-putter Adam Nelson stated: "Throw PEDs in the mix, and it becomes not about how good you are, but how good your doctor is" [69]. The Markkula Center for Applied Ethics (Santa Clara University, March 6, 2025) argues that fair play promotes competition by creating an environment where winning is earned through genuine effort and adherence to the rules. If athletes' abilities are the result of genetic modification, their accomplishments lose their meaning, as philosopher Michael Sandel has argued [70].

**3. The Russian State-Sponsored Doping Scandal**

The Russian state-sponsored doping program is the most egregious example of how doping undermines fair play. WADA has described doping among Russian competitors as state-sponsored and systematic, with the Russian state found to have supplied steroids and other drugs to athletes. Russia has the most competitors who have been caught doping at the Olympic Games in the world, with more than 150 athletes. 51 Olympic medals have been stripped from Russia (and Russian associated teams), four times the number of the next highest, and more than 30% of the global total [71]. The McLaren Report concluded that Russia's Ministry of Sport, FSB, and Moscow laboratory operated a "state-directed failsafe system" using the "disappearing positive methodology" from at least 2011 to 2015 [44].

**4. The Sochi Olympics Cover-Up**

The New York Times (2016) revealed that the 2014 Sochi Winter Olympics featured a state-run doping program orchestrated by Russia. Grigory Rodchenkov, then director of Russia's antidoping laboratory, confessed to developing a three-drug cocktail of banned substances mixed with liquor for dozens of Russian athletes, including at least 15 medal winners. To avoid detection, Russian intelligence and antidoping experts secretly swapped tainted urine samples with clean ones collected months earlier, breaking into tamper-proof bottles in a shadow laboratory. Rodchenkov estimated that up to 100 dirty samples were replaced. None of the athletes were caught doping. Russia won the most medals of the Games, easily surpassing its main rival, the United States, and undermining the integrity of one of the world's most prestigious sporting events [46].

**5. The Philosophical Grounding of Fair Play**

The WADA Ethics Panel states that anti-doping programs seek to preserve what is intrinsically valuable about sport, often referred to as "the spirit of sport." It is the essence of Olympism, the pursuit of human excellence through the dedicated perfection of each person's talents. The spirit of sport is the celebration of the human spirit, body and mind, and is reflected in values we find in and through sport, including Ethics, fairplay and honesty; health; excellence in performance; character and education; fun and joy; teamwork; dedication and commitment; respect for rules and laws; respect for self and other Participants; courage; community and solidarity [72].

**6. The Strict Liability Principle**

The WADA Code establishes strict liability for presence of prohibited substances, with proof standards of "comfortable satisfaction" for the Anti-Doping Organization and "balance of probability" for the athlete. The Lex Lumen Research Journal explains that under WADA Article 2.1, if a prohibited substance is found in an athlete's sample, it will be considered a violation and there is no need to prove fault. The case of Shelby Houlihan (contaminated food) is cited as an example of the sometimes harsh application of this principle [73]. The Marquette Law School Faculty Blog (October 2010) argues that doping threatens athletes' health and undermines the integrity of sport, referencing the Olympic spirit, WADA, UNESCO, and the NFL's own steroid policy, which emphasize fairness, respect, and equal competition. The author uses a slippery slope argument: allowing medically supervised steroid use would blur the line between legal and illegal doping, making regulation unworkable [74].

## CON ARGUMENT 3: Coercion and the Doping Arms Race

**Thesis:** Even if some athletes want to use PEDs voluntarily, their use creates a coercive environment where clean athletes must dope to compete, eliminating true choice and creating a dangerous "arms race" that pressures athletes into ever-riskier drug use.

### Supporting Points

**1. The Coercion Argument — Theoretical Framework**

The Hastings Center briefing by Thomas H. Murray directly addresses the coercion argument: "The athletes I know all crave a level playing field. If my competition is gaining an edge by using a drug that tilts the field in their favor, then their choice pressures me to do the same." Murray argues that the dangerous "arms race" dynamic pressures athletes into ever-riskier drug use [75]. The ResearchGate paper "Doping and coercion" (2020) states: "If doping is legalised, you might fear that athletes who do not want to use doping will experience a morally problematic pressure to use doping" [76]. The Human Kinetics blog notes that athletes may feel trapped into using drugs in order to stay competitive. According to this argument, the user of performance-enhancing drugs is harming themselves [77].

**2. Katherine Hamilton's Story**

Katherine Hamilton, a former heptathlete, walked away from a full-ride athletic scholarship at UC Berkeley in 1981 at age 19 because of the prevalence of performance-enhancing drugs in track and field. Hamilton had been a junior national high-jump champion in 1979 and was the first woman to receive a full track scholarship to Cal. She chose not to dope, but watching drug-using competitors advance year after year led her to quit the sport entirely. She later became a nurse at San Quentin prison. She stated: "There is an untold story about all the thousands ... who make a conscious decision, that are really great athletes doing the right thing, working really hard -- and they just drop out because they're just not willing to do the things to your body and to go down that road." She also said: "I was not willing at that point to settle for being 20th and 30th, working my a-- off, with no steroids, and watching everybody else go forward, year after year after year" [78].

**3. The East German Experience of Coercion**

The East German experience demonstrates the ultimate coercion: the training motto at the pool was "You eat the pills, or you die." It was forbidden to refuse. One athlete stated: "I just woke one day and said this feels like sh*t. It's wrong, it's cheating, and I feel horrible. When I said no, no more pills, no more needles, the coaches freaked out" [57]. This case illustrates how a doping culture, once established, can become coercive and inescapable for athletes.

**4. The Doping Arms Race in Cycling**

The Point Magazine article "Descent" describes the pressure in professional cycling. Riders like Tyler Hamilton and David Millar describe the pressure to dope to remain competitive, the psychological toll, and the sport's omertà (code of silence). Tyler Hamilton stated: "I could hear the pain, but I didn't have to listen to it" [79]. The CBC Radio segment "The athlete's dilemma: to dope or not to dope" notes that some athletes succumb to the pressure to dope. Thomas Murray says in certain eras of professional bicycle road-racing and weight-lifting, "to be competitive you had to dope" [80].

**5. The Olympic Channel Podcast Revelation**

The Olympic Channel podcast (Bernice Wilson) reveals the story of British sprinter Bernice Wilson, whose coach convinced her to cheat and then continued to administer drugs without her knowledge, illustrating how athletes can be manipulated into doping [81].

**6. The Guardian Report on Pressure**

The Guardian (2016) reports that an adviser to a new play on cheating among athletes warned that British sports stars are under pressure to use performance-enhancing drugs [82]. The New York Times (1988) investigation revealed that many athletes still use drugs because of peer pressure or a belief that opponents are using [83]. The Circle Voice reports that lack of rest, stress, and anxiety make an athlete's decision to dope more of a necessity than a choice. The modern rhetoric of sports doping is that athletes are pressured into doping [84].

## CON ARGUMENT 4: Damage to Sports' Reputation and Public Trust

**Thesis:** Doping scandals erode public trust in sports, damage viewership, harm the commercial interests that support sports, and tarnish the reputation of entire sports and nations, with the Lance Armstrong case alone demonstrating over $150 million in losses.

### Supporting Points

**1. The Lance Armstrong Case — The Most Comprehensive Case Study**

The Lance Armstrong doping case was a major investigation by USADA that led to Armstrong being stripped of his seven Tour de France titles (1999–2005) and an Olympic bronze medal, and receiving a lifetime ban from cycling. USADA portrayed Armstrong as the ringleader of what it called "the most sophisticated, professionalized and successful doping program that sport has ever seen" [85]. The Wall Street Journal reported that Armstrong's estimated total losses exceeded $150 million in earnings, sponsorship contracts, and legal costs [86]. In October 2012, Armstrong lost all major sponsors. Nike stated: "Due to the seemingly insurmountable evidence that Armstrong participated in doping... we have terminated his contract" [87]. Other sponsors included Trek, Anheuser-Busch, Oakley, 24 Hour Fitness, and Honey Stinger. Armstrong was out approximately $15 million a year in sponsorship income [88].

**2. The U.S. Postal Service Sponsorship Fallout**

The Justice Department filed a False Claims Act lawsuit to recover $31 million paid by the U.S. Postal Service to sponsor Armstrong's cycling team from 1996–2004. USADA CEO Travis Tygart stated: "The evidence shows beyond any doubt that the U.S. Postal Service Pro Cycling Team ran the most sophisticated, professionalized, and successful doping program that sport has ever seen" [89].

**3. The ESSEC Knowledge Analysis**

The ESSEC Knowledge article (November 2012) analyzes the implications for corporate sponsorship. Professor Thierry Lardinoit argues that sponsors have a responsibility to protect sport's credibility, as sponsorship depends on the perceived fairness of competition. He criticizes sponsors for prioritizing short-term profits over long-term risks. A study cited during the London Olympics found that heavy viewership inspired greater physical activity, but doping doubts would undermine that inspiration. Lardinoit states: "If sport loses its credibility, then sponsorship too loses its reason for being. Cheating is a direct attack on the very essence of sport" [90].

**4. The Russian State-Sponsored Doping Scandal**

WADA's findings describe doping among Russian competitors as state-sponsored and systematic, with the Russian state found to have supplied steroids and other drugs to athletes. Russia has the most competitors who have been caught doping at the Olympic Games in the world, with more than 150 athletes. 51 Olympic medals have been stripped from Russia (and Russian associated teams), four times the number of the next highest, and more than 30% of the global total [71]. The ProPublica investigation (2016) revealed that Jack Robertson, the former chief investigator of WADA, said his efforts to investigate state-sponsored doping in Russia were repeatedly thwarted by WADA's own president. Robertson stated: "For Craig Reedie to say he assumes that a large percentage of Russian athletes who will be in Rio are clean, he's talking out of his rear end." He also stated: "We can't just keep going from scandal to scandal. And if this scandal isn't enough to bring about change, then nothing ever will be" [91].

**5. The Impact on Athlete Image Repair**

The Sport Journal article (2017) examines Armstrong's image repair strategies. Before the Oprah interview, 45% of Americans believed Armstrong should retain his titles; afterward, 51% said he should not. Only 12% believed his confession was remorseful, while 48% saw it as forced by evidence. Armstrong's prolonged denial over more than a decade undermined his mortification strategy, turning a once-popular athlete (79% favorable in 2005) into a disgraced figure whose legacy is now defined by the doping scandal [92].

**6. The Broader Erosion of Trust**

The European Olympic Committees (EOC) states that being associated with doping or a doping offence will have an impact on the person's reputation and social relations. A ban resulting from an Anti-Doping Rule Violation (ADRV) will have a significant financial impact on the individual [93]. Sport Integrity Australia warns that taking banned substances not only puts athletes at risk of a ban, it has some serious health effects. Some athletes believe that since many substances on the WADA Prohibited List are legitimate medications, they must be safe to use. This is a dangerous misconception [94]. The International Wheelchair Basketball Federation (IWBF) notes that doping can lead to serious health risks, including heart and liver damage, as well as acne, hair loss, and infertility [95].

## CON ARGUMENT 5: Corruption of the Spirit of Sport

**Thesis:** Performance-enhancing drugs fundamentally corrupt the nature of athletic competition by transforming it from a test of human excellence, dedication, and natural talent into a contest of pharmacological innovation, thereby destroying what makes sport meaningful and valuable to athletes and society.

### Supporting Points

**1. The WADA Code Definition of the "Spirit of Sport"**

The World Anti-Doping Code 2021 defines the spirit of sport as "the celebration of the human spirit, body and mind. It is the essence of Olympism and is reflected in the values we find in and through sport, including: Health, Ethics, fair play and honesty, Athletes' rights... Doping is fundamentally contrary to the spirit of sport" [68]. The Code establishes that anti-doping programs are founded on the intrinsic value of sport, which is "the ethical pursuit of human excellence through the dedicated perfection of each Athlete's natural talents" [96].

**2. Michael Sandel's "The Case Against Perfection"**

Harvard bioethicist Michael J. Sandel, in his influential book *The Case Against Perfection* (2007), argues that the moral questions about genetic engineering and enhancement require spiritual discussions often neglected by modern philosophers. The familiar moral language of autonomy, fairness, and individual rights is insufficient to address these issues. The deeper danger of genetic enhancement is a "Promethean aspiration to remake nature" that threatens to undermine an appreciation of the "giftedness of life"—the recognition that our talents and powers are not wholly our own doing [97].

On performance-enhancing drugs in sport, Sandel argues that they shift appreciation from the athlete to the pharmacist and undermine natural giftedness. He writes: "But it illustrates how performance-enhancing technologies, genetic or otherwise, can erode the part of athletic and artistic performance that celebrates natural talent" [98]. He further argues: "Even if genetic enhancements were completely safe and offered freely to all, they would still be ethically questionable. There is something appealing, even intoxicating, about a vision of human freedom unfettered by the given... But that vision of freedom is flawed. It threatens to banish our appreciation of life as a gift, and to leave us with nothing to affirm or behold outside our own will" [99].

**3. Robert L. Simon's "Mutual Quest for Excellence"**

Robert L. Simon, Professor of Philosophy at Hamilton College, proposes in his 1985 article "Good Competition and Drug-Enhanced Performance" that athletic competition is best thought of as "a mutual quest for excellence through challenge" where participants respect each other as persons. Drug use undermines this by making outcomes depend on physiological reactions to substances rather than on personal qualities like dedication, courage, and skill. Thus, prohibition is justified in the name of respect for persons, even though athletes may freely choose to use drugs. Simon states: "The use of performance-enhancing drugs in sports restricts the area in which we can be respected as persons" [100].

**4. Warren P. Fraleigh's "Mechanized Bodies"**

Fraleigh examines the ethical issue of performance-enhancing drugs in sport and concludes that "drug use is morally wrong because it reduces athletic competition to contests between mechanized bodies rather than total thinking, feeling, willing, and acting persons." Fraleigh argues: "The use of performance-enhancing drugs by the individual is morally wrong because it changes the nature of the contest without agreement and exerts coercion on others to inflict self-harm." He further states: "To have a fair contest requires that the contestants must be attempting to do the same thing better than one another" [101].

**5. Sigmund Loland on Athletic Excellence**

Sigmund Loland's article examines whether PEDs should be banned in sport, arguing that the answer depends on interpretations of sport's meaning and value. He develops a normative structure of sport based on self-imposed constraints at three levels: (1) constitutive rules, where games are defined by voluntarily accepting unnecessary obstacles; (2) fair play norms, including ethos adherence and obligations of honesty and effort; and (3) athletic excellence as a form of human excellence, grounded in the fair equality of opportunity (FEO) ideal. At the third level, PED use undermines athletic excellence by reducing athlete responsibility and increasing reliance on external system strength. Loland concludes: "Sport, in particular in its elite form, is contested terrain. The use of performance-enhancing drugs (PED) is considered by many to be the most fundamental challenge to sport's meaning and value" [102].

**6. Thomas H. Murray on "Biochemical Shortcuts"**

Thomas H. Murray, Ph.D. (former Hastings Center), argues in the American Journal of Bioethics (June 2018) that the debate over PEDs is fundamentally about what sport should celebrate: either superhuman performance at any cost, or the admirable development of natural talents through virtuous effort. He draws on Loland's argument that a serviceable concept of "natural" can be grounded in the science of human adaptation to training, framing PEDs as "biochemical shortcuts—they modify bodies directly rather than relying on the normal, natural processes of adaptation to training." Murray argues that natural talents are unearned but morally significant when perfected through work, and calls for accountability across the entire "doping ecosystem"—not just athletes—to preserve fairness and meaning in sport [103].

## CON ARGUMENT 6: Unfair Advantage for Wealthy Nations and Athletes

**Thesis:** Legalizing PEDs would disproportionately benefit wealthy nations and athletes with access to sophisticated medical support, advanced pharmaceutical technologies, and the financial resources to afford the safest and most effective doping regimens, thereby exacerbating existing global inequalities in sport.

### Supporting Points

**1. The Current Disparity in Anti-Doping Capacity**

The global anti-doping system already reflects significant inequalities. Only 25 of 54 African countries have operational National Anti-Doping Organizations (NADOs), despite 52 ratifying the UNESCO Convention. Competing priorities—such as addressing poverty, education, and health—limit government funding for sport. The report notes that Africa contributed 64% (12) and 44% (13) of their share to WADA in 2021 and 2022, respectively [104]. Europe contributes 45% of the government share of WADA's budget, making it the largest governmental contributor [105]. The United States contributes approximately $3.4 million to WADA annually, or about 7% of WADA's total budget [106].

**2. The Cost of Advanced Doping**

If PEDs were legalized, the cost of accessing the safest and most effective regimens would be prohibitive for many athletes and nations. The cost of medical supervision, advanced pharmaceutical preparations, and cutting-edge technologies would create a two-tiered system where wealthy athletes from developed nations could afford state-of-the-art enhancement while athletes from developing nations would be left with either inferior or more dangerous options. As Hermann and Henneberg (2014) calculate, to achieve 100% detection of doping in one year, 16-50 tests per athlete must be done costing approximately $25,000 per athlete—deemed economically unviable [41]. The cost to significantly improve detection is estimated at A$30,000-$40,000 per athlete per year [42].

**3. The Enhanced Games as a Model of Inequality**

The Enhanced Games in Las Vegas (May 24, 2026) featured 42 athletes from 24 nations, but the event's backing from prominent libertarian and tech investors including Peter Thiel, Christian Angermayer, Donald Trump Jr., and the Winklevoss twins demonstrates the financial resources required to participate in such ventures [5]. The Enhanced Games investor Christian Angermayer stated: "I'm doing 250 milligrams of testosterone per week. I do tesamorelin, I do SS-31, I do a lot of red-light therapy. Obviously I do gym—because by the way, you need to work" [5]. This level of sophisticated, multi-substance enhancement is simply not accessible to athletes from developing nations.

**4. The Historical Pattern of Wealthy Nations Doping**

The state-sponsored doping programs of East Germany, Russia, and China demonstrate that the most sophisticated doping programs have historically been run by wealthy or state-funded operations. The East German program cost millions and involved a dedicated scientific infrastructure. The Russian program under Putin involved the Ministry of Sport, the FSB, and the Moscow laboratory [44]. These cases illustrate that when doping becomes systematic, it is the wealthy and powerful nations that benefit most.

**5. The Rodchenkov Act and U.S. Dominance**

The United States passed the Rodchenkov Act to assert criminal jurisdiction over doping, demonstrating how wealthy nations can shape the global anti-doping landscape to their advantage. The US contributes approximately $3.4 million to WADA annually, or about 7% of WADA's total budget [106]. In 2024, the US government withheld its contribution of over $3.6 million to pressure WADA for reforms, demonstrating the leverage that wealthy nations have over the global anti-doping system [107].

**6. The Impact on Developing Nation Athletes**

The Sport New Zealand "Unlevel Playing Field" report (2026) examines the future of elite athlete classification and identifies key drivers including gene editing, cyborg technologies, and market dynamics. The report presents four scenarios including one where "Expanded categories for transgender, intersex, genetically modified, and technologically enhanced athletes" could create a tiered system that further marginalizes athletes from developing nations [15]. The UNESCO Anti-Doping Convention has invested over USD 5 million for over 200 projects in more than 120 countries since 2008, but this represents a fraction of what would be needed to level the playing field if PEDs were legalized [108].

---

## Conclusion

The debate over performance-enhancing drugs and technologies in elite sports presents a fundamental tension between individual liberty and collective good, between the pursuit of enhanced performance and the preservation of meaningful competition. The six pro arguments highlight the philosophical case for athlete autonomy, the reality of genetic inequality, the entertainment value of enhanced performances, the medical necessity of certain substances, the demonstrated ineffectiveness of the current prohibitionist system, and the potential for harm reduction through regulation. The six con arguments emphasize the severe health risks of PEDs, the fundamental importance of fair play and integrity, the coercive dynamics of a doping arms race, the damage to sports' reputation and commercial interests, the corruption of the spirit of sport as a celebration of human excellence, and the exacerbation of global inequalities that legalization would likely produce.

The evidence suggests that the current system is costly, imperfect, and often ineffective, yet it also reflects a genuine societal commitment to the idea that sport should be a celebration of human potential developed through dedication and natural talent. The path forward may lie not in an all-or-nothing approach but in nuanced, evidence-based policies that address the legitimate concerns on both sides of this complex debate.
