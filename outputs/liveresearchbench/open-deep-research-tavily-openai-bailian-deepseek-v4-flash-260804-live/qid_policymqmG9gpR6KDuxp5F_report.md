# GDPR Enforcement Analysis: High-Value Cases (August 2023 – August 2026)

## Executive Summary

This report presents a systematic analysis of GDPR enforcement cases across EU member states that resulted in fines exceeding €10 million, issued within the period from August 2023 to August 2026. Cumulative GDPR fines across the European Economic Area have exceeded **€7.1 billion** since May 2018, with more than 60% of all fines (over €3.8 billion) imposed since January 2023 alone [1][2][3]. Annual fines stabilized at approximately **€1.2 billion** for both 2024 and 2025 [2][3].

The analysis identifies **14 qualifying cases** from **7 EU member states** (Ireland, France, Italy, Spain, Netherlands, Germany, and Czech Republic), totaling over **€2.5 billion** in fines. The most frequent and highest-value violations involve insufficient legal basis for data processing, unlawful international data transfers, and failures in technical and organizational security measures.

---

## 1. Top 10 Most Frequent Violation Types in High-Value Cases

### 1.1 Insufficient Legal Basis for Data Processing (Article 6 GDPR)

**Description:** This is the most common violation type across all GDPR enforcement, accounting for approximately 34% of total fines [4]. It occurs when a controller processes personal data without a valid lawful basis under Article 6 GDPR. The six lawful bases are: consent, contract performance, legal obligation, vital interests, public task/public interest, and legitimate interests. Common failures include using "contractual necessity" for behavioral advertising (which is not genuinely necessary for the service), relying on "legitimate interests" without conducting a proper balancing test, and failing to document which legal basis applies to each processing activity.

**Standard Penalty Range:** This is a Tier 2 violation under Article 83(5)(a), with a maximum of €20 million or 4% of total global annual turnover, whichever is higher. The average fine for this violation type is approximately €2.9 million [4]. High-value cases have ranged from €40 million (Criteo) to €390 million (Meta).

**Key Cases:** LinkedIn €310 million (2024, Ireland), Meta €390 million (2023, Ireland), Criteo €40 million (2023, France, upheld 2026) [5][6][7].

### 1.2 Non-Compliance with General Data Processing Principles (Article 5 GDPR)

**Description:** This category involves failure to adhere to the seven core principles of data processing: lawfulness, fairness and transparency; purpose limitation; data minimization; accuracy; storage limitation; integrity and confidentiality (security); and accountability. Total fines for this category amount to €3.63 billion, making it the largest by total fine amount [4]. This category frequently overlaps with other violation types.

**Standard Penalty Range:** Tier 2 violation under Article 83(5)(a), maximum €20 million or 4% of global annual turnover. The average fine is approximately €3.8 million [4]. Five of the top 10 fines globally have involved this category.

**Key Cases:** Meta/Instagram €405 million (2022, Ireland), Meta €265 million (2022, Ireland), Enel Energia €79.1 million (2024, Italy), H&M €35.25 million (2020, Hamburg) [8][9][10].

### 1.3 Insufficient Technical and Organizational Measures for Information Security (Article 32 GDPR)

**Description:** This violation occurs when a controller or processor fails to implement appropriate technical and organizational measures to ensure a level of security appropriate to the risk. This includes encryption, access controls, pseudonymization, incident response capabilities, and regular security testing. Total fines for this category amount to €1.73 billion [4].

**Standard Penalty Range:** This is a Tier 1 violation under Article 83(4)(a), with a maximum of €10 million or 2% of global annual turnover. However, when combined with violations of Article 5 (integrity and confidentiality principle), the penalty can reach Tier 2 levels. The average fine is approximately €2.0 million [4].

**Key Cases:** Meta €251 million (2024, Ireland) for 2018 data breach, Vodafone €30 million (2025, Germany) for authentication failures, Meta €91 million (2024, Ireland) for storing passwords in plaintext [11][12][13].

### 1.4 Unlawful International Data Transfers (Chapter V, Articles 44-49 GDPR)

**Description:** This violation occurs when a controller transfers personal data to a third country without adequate safeguards. Following the Schrems II ruling (CJEU, July 2020), which invalidated the Privacy Shield framework, transfers require Standard Contractual Clauses (SCCs) supplemented with Transfer Impact Assessments (TIAs) and additional measures, or Binding Corporate Rules (BCRs). This category has generated the largest individual fines in GDPR history.

**Standard Penalty Range:** Tier 2 violation under Article 83(5)(c), maximum €20 million or 4% of global annual turnover. For very large companies, the 4% of turnover cap has resulted in billion-euro fines.

**Key Cases:** Meta €1.2 billion (2023, Ireland), TikTok €530 million (2025, Ireland), Uber €290 million (2024, Netherlands), Yango €100 million (2026, Netherlands) [14][15][16][17].

### 1.5 Insufficient Fulfillment of Information/Transparency Obligations (Articles 12-14 GDPR)

**Description:** This violation occurs when a controller fails to provide data subjects with required information about processing in a concise, transparent, intelligible, and easily accessible form using clear and plain language. Total fines for this category amount to €1.25 billion [4]. The EDPB's 2026 Coordinated Enforcement Framework (CEF) specifically targets transparency obligations, with 25 DPAs participating [18].

**Standard Penalty Range:** Tier 2 violation under Article 83(5)(b), maximum €20 million or 4% of global annual turnover.

**Key Cases:** WhatsApp Ireland €225 million (2021, Ireland), LinkedIn €95 million (component of €310 million, 2024, Ireland), TikTok €45 million (component of €530 million, 2025, Ireland) [5][19][20].

### 1.6 Insufficient Fulfillment of Data Subjects' Rights (Articles 15-22 GDPR)

**Description:** This violation occurs when a controller fails to respond appropriately to data subjects exercising their rights, including the right of access (Article 15), right to erasure (Article 17), right to data portability (Article 20), and right to object (Article 21). The EDPB's 2025 CEF action on the right to erasure involved 32 DPAs and 764 controllers, identifying seven systemic weaknesses [21]. The 2024 CEF action on the right of access investigated 1,185 controllers [22].

**Standard Penalty Range:** Tier 2 violation under Article 83(5)(b), maximum €20 million or 4% of global annual turnover.

**Key Cases:** Uber €10 million (2024, Netherlands) for obstructing driver data access requests, Criteo €40 million (2023, France) for failing to respect right to withdraw consent and right to erasure [7][23].

### 1.7 Insufficient Data Breach Notification (Articles 33-34 GDPR)

**Description:** This violation occurs when a controller fails to notify a personal data breach to the supervisory authority within 72 hours (Article 33) or fails to communicate the breach to affected data subjects (Article 34). Data breach notifications surged to an average of 443 per day in 2025, a 22% year-on-year increase [2][3].

**Standard Penalty Range:** Failure to notify the authority is a Tier 1 violation (€10 million or 2% of turnover). Failure to communicate to data subjects is a Tier 2 violation (€20 million or 4% of turnover).

**Key Cases:** Meta €11 million (component of €251 million, 2024, Ireland) for missing information in breach notification, Free Mobile/Free €42 million (2026, France) for failure to fully inform affected individuals [11][24].

### 1.8 Insufficient Data Processing Records (Article 30 GDPR)

**Description:** This violation occurs when a controller or processor fails to maintain proper records of processing activities, including purposes of processing, categories of data subjects and personal data, recipients, international transfer details, and security measures. This is a common violation for SMEs but also affects larger organizations when combined with other violations.

**Standard Penalty Range:** Tier 1 violation under Article 83(4)(a), maximum €10 million or 2% of global annual turnover. Fines are typically lower (tens of thousands to low millions) unless combined with other violations.

**Key Cases:** While fines for this specific violation alone rarely exceed €10 million, failures in record-keeping frequently contribute to larger penalties when combined with other violations. The European Commission's May 2025 Single Market Simplification proposal aimed to simplify record-keeping obligations for organizations with under 750 employees [25].

### 1.9 Failure to Appoint a Data Protection Officer (Articles 37-39 GDPR)

**Description:** This violation occurs when a controller or processor fails to designate a Data Protection Officer where required, including when core activities involve regular and systematic monitoring of data subjects on a large scale, or large-scale processing of special categories of data. The EDPB conducted a 2023 CEF action on DPO designation and position [26].

**Standard Penalty Range:** Tier 1 violation under Article 83(4)(a), maximum €10 million or 2% of global annual turnover. Fines are typically lower for this category alone.

**Key Cases:** While individual fines for this category alone are typically under €1 million, the 2023 CEF action signaled that DPAs consider DPO designation a fundamental compliance requirement. The 2024 CEF report on the right of access noted that larger controllers with dedicated DPOs showed better compliance [22][26].

### 1.10 Insufficient Data Protection Impact Assessment (Article 35 GDPR)

**Description:** This violation occurs when a controller fails to conduct a DPIA where processing is likely to result in a high risk to rights and freedoms, including systematic and extensive profiling, large-scale processing of special categories of data, and systematic monitoring of publicly accessible areas on a large scale. The EDPB announced in 2026 that it is rolling out a harmonized DPIA template [27].

**Standard Penalty Range:** Tier 1 violation under Article 83(4)(a), maximum €10 million or 2% of global annual turnover. However, failure to conduct a DPIA is often linked to other substantive violations, resulting in Tier 2 penalties.

**Key Cases:** The TikTok €530 million (2025, Ireland) case found that TikTok's Transfer Risk Assessments were inadequate because they failed to properly assess Chinese laws and government access risks [15]. Intesa Sanpaolo €17.6 million (2026, Italy) included failure to conduct a DPIA for large-scale profiling [28].

## 2. Detailed Case Analysis by Member State

### 2.1 Ireland – Data Protection Commission (DPC)

Ireland's DPC has issued cumulative fines of **€4.04 billion**, approximately 57% of all GDPR fine value, driven by its role as lead supervisory authority for major US-headquartered tech firms [2][3].

#### Case 1: Meta Platforms Ireland Limited – €1,200,000,000 (May 22, 2023)

**Violation Type:** Unlawful International Data Transfers (Article 46(1) GDPR)

**Summary:** Meta was fined for transferring Facebook users' personal data from the EU/EEA to the United States without adequate safeguards following the Schrems II ruling. Meta continued to rely on Standard Contractual Clauses (SCCs) and supplementary measures, but the DPC found these did not address risks from US surveillance laws (FISA 702). The EDPB issued a binding decision finding Meta's infringement "very serious... systematic, repetitive and continuous." Meta was ordered to suspend EU-US data transfers within five months. The fine is under appeal. [14][29][30][31]

#### Case 2: TikTok Technology Limited – €530,000,000 (May 2, 2025)

**Violation Types:** Unlawful International Data Transfers (Article 46(1), €485 million) + Transparency Failures (Article 13(1)(f), €45 million)

**Summary:** The DPC found TikTok failed to verify, guarantee, and demonstrate that personal data of EEA users, remotely accessed by staff in China, was afforded protection essentially equivalent to EU standards. TikTok's Transfer Risk Assessments inadequately assessed Chinese laws (Anti-Terrorism Law, Counter-Espionage Law, Cybersecurity Law, National Intelligence Law). Supplementary measures (including Project Clover encryption) were deemed general security measures that did not address government access risks. TikTok is appealing. [15][32][33][34]

#### Case 3: LinkedIn Ireland Unlimited Company – €310,000,000 (October 24, 2024)

**Violation Types:** Insufficient Legal Basis (Article 6) + Transparency Failures (Articles 13-14)

**Summary:** The DPC found LinkedIn could not validly rely on consent, contractual necessity, or legitimate interest for processing user data for behavioral analysis and targeted advertising. Consent was not freely given, informed, or unambiguous. Contractual necessity failed because behavioral analysis is not essential to the service. The fine comprised €105 million for invalid consent on third-party data, €110 million for invalid contractual necessity/legitimate interest, and €95 million for transparency violations. [5][6][35][36]

#### Case 4: TikTok Limited – €345,000,000 (September 1, 2023)

**Violation Types:** Data Minimization (Article 5(1)(c)), Integrity and Confidentiality (Article 5(1)(f)), Data Protection by Design (Article 25), Transparency (Articles 12-13)

**Summary:** The investigation covered July 31, 2020 to December 31, 2020. Key findings included child user profiles set to public by default, inadequate technical and organizational measures, security risks from the 'Family Pairing' feature, failure to provide child users with clear information, and use of 'dark patterns' nudging users toward less privacy-friendly settings. TikTok was ordered to bring processing into compliance within three months. [37][38]

#### Case 5: Meta Platforms Ireland Limited – €251,000,000 (December 17, 2024)

**Violation Types:** Data Protection by Design (Article 25, €240 million) + Breach Notification (Article 33, €11 million)

**Summary:** The DPC fined Meta for a 2018 data breach affecting 29 million Facebook accounts worldwide, including 3 million in the EU/EEA. The breach exposed names, email addresses, phone numbers, and children's data. Meta failed to ensure data protection principles were protected in the design of processing systems. [11][39][40]

#### Case 6: Meta Platforms Ireland Limited – €91,000,000 (September 2024)

**Violation Type:** Integrity and Confidentiality (Article 5(1)(f), Article 32)

**Summary:** Meta was fined for storing user passwords in plaintext on internal systems, a fundamental failure of technical security measures. [12][41]

### 2.2 France – Commission Nationale de l'Informatique et des Libertés (CNIL)

The CNIL imposed €486.8 million in GDPR fines in 2025, a nearly ninefold increase from €55.2 million in 2024, despite similar enforcement volume [42].

#### Case 7: Google LLC and Google Ireland Limited – €325,000,000 (September 1, 2025)

**Violation Types:** Cookie consent violations (ePrivacy Directive), unsolicited direct marketing

**Summary:** The CNIL found Google displayed advertisements disguised as emails in Gmail's 'Promotions' and 'Social' tabs without user consent, affecting over 74 million accounts (53 million French users). During Google account creation, users were steered toward accepting personalized advertising cookies without clear information. The CNIL considered Google's prior fines (€100 million in 2020, €150 million in 2021) as aggravating circumstances. Google was ordered to comply within six months or face daily penalties of €100,000. [43][44][45]

#### Case 8: Infinite Styles Services Co. Limited (SHEIN) – €150,000,000 (September 1, 2025)

**Violation Types:** Cookie consent violations (ePrivacy Directive)

**Summary:** An inspection of shein.com in August 2023 revealed that advertising cookies were placed on users' devices before consent; cookie information banners were incomplete and lacked details about cookie purposes and third parties; the 'refuse all' mechanism still placed new cookies. Approximately 12 million monthly visitors in France were affected. SHEIN is appealing. [46][47][48]

#### Case 9: ORANGE – €50,000,000 (November 14, 2024)

**Violation Types:** Unsolicited direct marketing (ePrivacy Directive) + Cookie consent violations

**Summary:** The CNIL found Orange displayed advertisements disguised as emails in users' inboxes without consent, affecting over 7.8 million people. When users withdrew cookie consent, previously stored cookies continued to be read. Orange had ceased the practice in November 2023. [49]

#### Case 10: FREE MOBILE and FREE – €42,000,000 (January 13, 2026)

**Violation Types:** Insufficient Security Measures (Article 32) + Breach Notification (Article 34) + Excessive Data Retention (Article 5(1)(e))

**Summary:** Following a data breach in October 2024 exposing personal data of 24 million subscriber contracts (including IBANs), the CNIL found basic security measures were lacking, including weak VPN authentication and ineffective anomaly detection. FREE MOBILE retained millions of former subscriber records without justification. Companies were ordered to implement strengthened security measures within three months. [24]

#### Case 11: Yahoo EMEA Limited – €10,000,000 (December 29, 2023)

**Violation Types:** Cookie consent violations (ePrivacy Directive)

**Summary:** Yahoo placed cookies on users' devices without proper consent, and users faced significant difficulties withdrawing consent. Approximately 5 million unique visitors in France were affected. [50]

### 2.3 Italy – Garante per la Protezione dei Dati Personali

#### Case 12: Enel Energia – €79,100,000 (February 2024)

**Violation Types:** Insufficient Legal Basis (Article 6) + Data Processing Principles (Article 5) + Security Measures (Article 32) + Accountability (Article 5(2))

**Summary:** The highest fine ever issued in Italy. The Garante found Enel failed to implement adequate technical and organizational measures to prevent unauthorized agents from conducting unlawful telemarketing activities. Enel acquired 978 contracts from four previously sanctioned companies not part of its sales network. Serious security flaws allowed an illicit business of nuisance calls and fraudulent contracts to persist for years (at least 9,300 contracts). The Court of Rome upheld the decision in September 2025. [10][51][52]

#### Case 13: Intesa Sanpaolo – €49,400,000 (March 2026)

**Violation Types:** Unlawful Processing (Articles 5-6) + Security Measures (Article 32) + Breach Notification (Articles 33-34)

**Summary:** Two separate fines. First (€17.6 million, March 12, 2026): Unlawful processing of approximately 2.4 million customers' data by unilaterally transferring accounts to subsidiary Isybank SpA without appropriate legal basis, transparency, or DPIA. Second (€31.8 million, March 30, 2026): An employee made over 6,600 unauthorized inquiries into 3,573 customers' banking information over more than two years (Feb 2022 – Apr 2024) without detection due to inadequate internal monitoring. [28][53]

#### Case 14: Poste Italiane and Postepay – €12,500,000 (April 20, 2026)

**Violation Types:** Data Minimization, Transparency, Consent, Storage Limitation (Articles 5-6)

**Summary:** The Garante fined Poste Italiane and Postepay for unlawfully tracking data of 14.5 million Android users via third-party SDKs in mobile banking apps. The Garante rejected the companies' fraud-prevention justification. [54]

#### Case 15: OpenAI – €15,000,000 (December 2024, annulled March 18, 2026)

**Violation Types:** Insufficient Legal Basis for AI training + Transparency Failures + Age Verification Failures + Breach Notification Failure

**Summary:** The Garante's investigation found OpenAI trained ChatGPT without adequate legal basis, failed to meet transparency obligations, lacked age verification for users under 13, and did not properly report a data breach affecting 440 Italian users. The fine was calculated as €9 million for unlawful processing, €320,000 for breach notification failure, and €5.68 million for non-compliance with prior corrective measures. On March 18, 2026, the Court of Rome annulled the fine on jurisdictional grounds (one-stop-shop), leaving substantive GDPR questions unresolved. [55][56]

### 2.4 Spain – Agencia Española de Protección de Datos (AEPD)

#### Case 16: Meta Platforms, Inc. – €479,000,000 (November 19, 2025) – Court Ruling

**Violation Types:** Unlawful Processing (Article 6) – Behavioral advertising without valid consent

**Summary:** The Commercial Court No. 15 of Madrid ordered Meta to pay €479 million to 87 Spanish digital press publishers for GDPR violations and unfair competition. The court found Meta's behavioral advertising unlawfully processed personal data after abandoning the consent requirement in favor of 'performance of the contract,' gaining a significant competitive advantage. The judge estimated Meta earned at least €5.3 billion in advertising profits during the period (May 2018 to August 2023). Meta is appealing. [57][58]

#### Case 17: Amadeus IT Group – €18,000,000 (May 2026, reduced to €14.4 million via voluntary payment)

**Violation Types:** Insufficient Legal Basis (Article 6) + Transparency (Article 14) + Compatibility Assessment (Article 6(4))

**Summary:** The AEPD fined Amadeus for a pilot project called 'PLATAFORMA.1' that repurposed passenger data collected from flight bookings to create traveler profiles for hyper-personalized offers, without proper notice or valid legal basis. Amadeus attempted to rely on broad privacy policy language and legitimate interest, but the AEPD rejected these arguments. [59]

#### Case 18: AENA S.M.E., S.A. – €10,043,002 (November 2025)

**Violation Types:** Insufficient Legal Basis + Transparency Failures

**Summary:** The AEPD fined the Spanish airport authority for processing personal data without adequate legal basis and failing to provide transparent information to data subjects about processing activities. [60]

### 2.5 Netherlands – Autoriteit Persoonsgegevens (AP)

#### Case 19: Uber B.V. and Uber Technologies Inc. – €290,000,000 (August 26, 2024)

**Violation Type:** Unlawful International Data Transfers (Article 44 GDPR)

**Summary:** The Dutch AP fined Uber for transferring European drivers' personal data to the United States without adequate safeguards over a 27-month period (August 6, 2021 to November 27, 2023). Sensitive data transferred included account details, taxi licenses, location data, photos, payment details, identity documents, and in some cases criminal and medical data. Uber had stopped using SCCs in August 2021 and only resumed compliant transfers at the end of 2023. The AP rejected Uber's arguments that its US entity was already subject to GDPR under Article 3. Uber is appealing. [16][61][62]

#### Case 20: MLU B.V. (Yango taxi app) – €100,000,000 (May 8, 2026)

**Violation Type:** Unlawful International Data Transfers (Chapter V, Articles 44-49)

**Summary:** The AP fined Yango (part of the Yandex group) for transferring personal data of users from Norway and Finland to Russia without adequate safeguards. Data transferred included driving license scans, home addresses, GPS locations, trip data, images, chat content, and social security numbers. The AP found that despite encryption and pseudonymization, shared executive control meant re-identification was feasible. The wrong SCC module was used. Russian law (Yarovaya Law, Taxi Law, SORM) compels data access by security services. The AP imposed an immediate ban on further transfers. MLU is challenging the fine. [17][63]

#### Case 21: Uber B.V. and Uber Technologies Inc. – €10,000,000 (January 2024)

**Violation Types:** Obstruction of Data Subject Rights (Articles 12-15) + Transparency Failures

**Summary:** The AP fined Uber for making it unnecessarily complex for drivers to request access to their personal data (the form was buried deep in the app), providing requested data only in English, failing to specify data retention periods and security measures, and failing to mention the right to data portability. This was the AP's third fine against Uber. [23][64]

### 2.6 Germany – Federal Commissioner for Data Protection and Freedom of Information (BfDI)

#### Case 22: Vodafone GmbH – €45,000,000 (June 3, 2025)

**Violation Types:** Insufficient Processor Oversight (Article 28(1), €15 million) + Insufficient Security Measures (Article 32(1), €30 million)

**Summary:** The BfDI imposed two fines on Vodafone. The first (€15 million) for failing to adequately review and monitor partner agencies whose employees tricked customers into signing fraudulent contracts. The second (€30 million) for security vulnerabilities in the "MeinVodafone" portal and customer hotline that allowed unauthorized third parties to access customers' eSIM profiles, enabling mobile number takeover and potential misuse for other digital services. Vodafone accepted the fines, terminated partner contracts, revised processes, and replaced technical systems. A follow-up audit is planned. [13][65][66]

### 2.7 Czech Republic – Office for Personal Data Protection (UOOU)

#### Case 23: Avast Software s.r.o. – €13,900,000 (CZK 351,000,000) (Appellate decision April 10, 2024)

**Violation Types:** Insufficient Legal Basis (Article 6) + Transparency Failures (Article 13(1))

**Summary:** Avast transferred pseudonymized internet browsing histories and unique identifiers of approximately 100 million users of its antivirus software and browser extensions to its US affiliate Jumpshot, which marketed the data for consumer insights. The UOOU found the data was not properly anonymized—re-identification of at least some data subjects was possible—and users were misled about the nature of the transfer. The UOOU President emphasized that Avast, as a cybersecurity company, should have been particularly sensitive to privacy obligations. The decision is final and enforceable. Separately, the US FTC fined Avast $16.5 million for the same conduct. [67][68]

## 3. Compliance Gaps Enterprises Should Avoid

Based on patterns observed across these high-penalty cases, the following compliance gaps represent the most significant risks for enterprises:

### 3.1 Using Invalid Legal Bases for Behavioral Advertising

**The Gap:** Relying on "contractual necessity" or "legitimate interests" as legal bases for processing personal data for behavioral advertising and targeted marketing.

**Why It Fails:** The LinkedIn €310 million and Meta €390 million cases established that behavioral advertising is not "necessary" for the performance of a contract for social media or professional networking services. Legitimate interests also fail because users' reasonable expectations and fundamental rights outweigh the company's commercial interests. Consent is increasingly the only viable legal basis for this type of processing.

**Recommendation:** Obtain valid, freely given, specific, informed, and unambiguous consent before processing personal data for behavioral advertising. Ensure consent is as easy to withdraw as it is to give. Do not make access to core services conditional on consent for non-essential processing (cookie walls).

### 3.2 Inadequate Transfer Impact Assessments for International Data Transfers

**The Gap:** Performing superficial Transfer Impact Assessments (TIAs) that fail to meaningfully assess the legal framework and government surveillance practices in the recipient country.

**Why It Fails:** The TikTok €530 million case found that TikTok's TIAs inadequately assessed Chinese laws (Anti-Terrorism Law, Counter-Espionage Law, Cybersecurity Law, National Intelligence Law) and the risk of government access. The Meta €1.2 billion case found that SCCs alone were insufficient to address US surveillance laws (FISA 702). The Uber €290 million case found that Uber stopped using any transfer tool altogether for 27 months.

**Recommendation:** Conduct substantive TIAs that examine the specific legal framework of the recipient country, including surveillance laws, data access powers, and the independence of the local data protection authority. Document the assessment thoroughly. Implement supplementary measures (encryption, pseudonymization, access controls) that address the specific risks identified. Ensure SCCs use the correct module (controller-controller, not controller-processor). Regularly review and update TIAs.

### 3.3 Insufficient Vendor and Third-Party Oversight

**The Gap:** Failing to adequately vet, monitor, and audit data processors and partner agencies.

**Why It Fails:** The Vodafone €45 million case found that malicious employees at partner agencies committed fraud through fictitious contracts due to Vodafone's inadequate oversight. The Enel Energia €79.1 million case found that Enel failed to prevent unauthorized agents from conducting unlawful telemarketing and acquired contracts from previously sanctioned companies.

**Recommendation:** Implement robust vendor due diligence processes before onboarding. Conduct regular audits of processor compliance with data processing agreements. Ensure contracts include clear data processing terms, audit rights, and breach notification obligations. Terminate relationships with non-compliant partners. Monitor for red flags such as partners previously sanctioned by DPAs.

### 3.4 Weak Authentication and Security Measures

**The Gap:** Implementing inadequate authentication mechanisms and failing to conduct regular security testing.

**Why It Fails:** The Vodafone €30 million case found that weak authentication in the "MeinVodafone" portal allowed unauthorized access to eSIM profiles. The Meta €91 million case found that Meta stored passwords in plaintext. The Free Mobile €27 million case found weak VPN authentication and ineffective anomaly detection.

**Recommendation:** Implement multi-factor authentication for all customer-facing portals and internal systems. Adopt zero-trust architecture principles. Conduct regular penetration testing and vulnerability assessments. Never store passwords in plaintext. Implement robust logging and monitoring to detect unauthorized access patterns. Establish incident response procedures with clear escalation paths.

### 3.5 Cookie Consent and Dark Pattern Failures

**The Gap:** Placing cookies before obtaining consent, using misleading consent banners, and making it difficult to refuse or withdraw consent.

**Why It Fails:** The Shein €150 million case found that advertising cookies were placed before user consent, banners were incomplete, and the 'refuse all' mechanism still placed cookies. The Google €325 million case found that Google steered users toward accepting personalized advertising cookies during account creation. The Orange €50 million case found that previously stored cookies continued to be read after consent withdrawal.

**Recommendation:** Implement consent management platforms that obtain genuine prior consent before placing any non-essential cookies. Design consent banners that are clear, balanced, and equally easy to accept or refuse. Avoid dark patterns such as pre-ticked boxes, confusing language, or making "refuse all" harder to find than "accept all." Ensure consent withdrawal is as easy as consent grant. Maintain detailed consent records.

### 3.6 Inadequate Transparency and Privacy Notices

**The Gap:** Providing vague, incomplete, or misleading privacy notices that fail to clearly inform data subjects about processing purposes, legal bases, data recipients, retention periods, and international transfers.

**Why It Fails:** The LinkedIn €310 million case found that LinkedIn's privacy policy failed to clearly link data categories, purposes, and legal bases. The TikTok €530 million case found that the October 2021 EEA Privacy Policy did not name China as a recipient country or explain remote access as a transfer. The Avast €13.9 million case found that Avast misinformed users by claiming transferred data was anonymized.

**Recommendation:** Draft privacy notices using clear and plain language. Clearly identify each processing purpose, the legal basis relied upon, categories of data processed, recipients (including third countries), retention periods, and data subject rights. Use layered notices to provide key information upfront with detailed information available on request. Ensure privacy notices are regularly reviewed and updated when processing activities change.

### 3.7 Failure to Conduct Proper Data Protection Impact Assessments

**The Gap:** Conducting DPIAs that are superficial, procedural, or fail to address substantive risks.

**Why It Fails:** The TikTok €530 million case found that TikTok's Transfer Risk Assessments were inadequate because they failed to properly assess Chinese laws. The Intesa Sanpaolo €17.6 million case found that the bank failed to conduct a DPIA for large-scale profiling of 2.4 million customers.

**Recommendation:** Conduct DPIAs for any processing activity that is likely to result in high risk to rights and freedoms, including systematic profiling, large-scale processing of special categories of data, and systematic monitoring. DPIAs must be substantive, not procedural—they must meaningfully assess risks, identify mitigation measures, and be reviewed and updated regularly. For international transfers, ensure TIAs are integrated into the DPIA process.

### 3.8 Inadequate Breach Notification Procedures

**The Gap:** Failing to notify data breaches within 72 hours, providing incomplete information, or failing to communicate breaches to affected data subjects.

**Why It Fails:** The Meta €11 million (component of €251 million) case found missing information in the breach notification. The Free Mobile/Free €42 million case found failure to fully inform affected individuals. The Intesa Sanpaolo €31.8 million case found late and incomplete breach notification and delayed communication to affected customers.

**Recommendation:** Establish clear incident response procedures with defined roles, responsibilities, and escalation paths. Ensure the 72-hour notification deadline is tracked and met. Prepare breach notification templates in advance. Conduct regular tabletop exercises to practice incident response. Document all steps taken during breach response. Ensure breach notifications include all required information: nature of the breach, categories of data affected, likely consequences, and measures taken.

### 3.9 Failure to Respect Data Subject Rights

**The Gap:** Creating obstacles to data subject rights, including buried access request forms, excessive identification requirements, and failure to respond to erasure requests.

**Why It Fails:** The Uber €10 million case found that the data access request form was buried deep in the app, information was provided only in English, and the privacy statement failed to mention the right to data portability. The Criteo €40 million case found failure to respect the right to withdraw consent and request erasure. The EDPB's 2025 CEF report on the right to erasure found that only 25% of public-sector controllers regularly review their internal procedures [21].

**Recommendation:** Make data subject rights request mechanisms easily accessible through user-friendly online forms or self-service systems. Provide responses in the language of the data subject. Ensure responses are timely (within one month, extendable by two months for complex requests). Document all internal procedures for handling rights requests. Train staff on recognizing and processing rights requests. Regularly review and update procedures based on regulatory guidance.

### 3.10 Excessive Data Retention

**The Gap:** Retaining personal data for longer than necessary for the purposes for which it was collected.

**Why It Fails:** The Free Mobile €27 million (component of €42 million) case found that Free Mobile retained millions of former subscriber records without justification. The EDPB's 2025 CEF report on the right to erasure identified difficulties in determining retention periods as a systemic weakness [21]. The Poste Italiane €12.5 million case included storage limitation violations.

**Recommendation:** Establish clear data retention policies that specify retention periods for each category of data based on legal, regulatory, and business requirements. Implement automated deletion mechanisms to purge data after the retention period expires. Ensure backup systems are included in deletion processes. Regularly review and audit data inventories to identify and delete obsolete data.

## 4. Key Enforcement Trends and Recommendations

### 4.1 Accelerating Enforcement

More than 60% of all GDPR fines (over €3.8 billion) have been imposed since January 2023, indicating significantly accelerating enforcement [1][2][3]. Organizations should expect continued scrutiny, particularly in areas of international data transfers, consent management, and transparency obligations.

### 4.2 Cross-Border Data Transfer Scrutiny

Cross-border data transfers have generated the highest individual penalties (Meta €1.2B, TikTok €530M, Uber €290M, Yango €100M). Organizations transferring data to third countries must ensure robust TIAs, appropriate safeguards (SCCs with supplementary measures), and ongoing compliance monitoring.

### 4.3 Sectoral Expansion

Enforcement has expanded beyond Big Tech into financial services (Intesa Sanpaolo €49.4M, Enel Energia €79.1M), telecommunications (Vodafone €45M, Orange €50M, Free €42M), energy (Enel €79.1M), and employment (H&M €35.25M, Amazon France Logistique €32M). All sectors should review their compliance programs.

### 4.4 AI-Related Enforcement

AI-related enforcement is intensifying, with the Italian DPA imposing a €15 million fine on OpenAI (later annulled on jurisdictional grounds). The EU AI Act will add further penalties up to €35 million or 7% of global turnover from August 2026 [55][56].

### 4.5 Personal Liability of Directors

The Dutch DPA has investigated personal liability of directors at Clearview AI, signaling a shift toward holding management individually accountable for GDPR compliance failures [2].

### 4.6 EDPB Coordinated Enforcement Framework

The EDPB's CEF continues to drive harmonized enforcement:
- 2024: Right of access (Article 15) – 30 DPAs, 1,185 controllers
- 2025: Right to erasure (Article 17) – 32 DPAs, 764 controllers
- 2026: Transparency and information obligations (Articles 12-14) – 25 DPAs [18][21][22]

Organizations should proactively review their compliance in these areas.
