# Comprehensive Analysis: The State of Remote Work for Early-Career Professionals in the United States and Europe (2023–2026)

## Executive Summary

The remote work landscape has undergone a significant transformation since the pandemic, settling into a new equilibrium that presents both opportunities and challenges for recent graduates and early-career professionals. This report synthesizes data from official statistics (Bureau of Labor Statistics, Eurostat, Eurofound), academic research (NBER, Stanford, Administrative Science Quarterly), industry surveys (Gallup, McKinsey, LinkedIn, Pew Research Center), and policy reports to provide a comprehensive analysis of how remote work is reshaping entry-level employment in the United States and Europe.

Key findings include: (1) Remote work has become a primary driver of rising youth unemployment, with studies attributing up to 64% of the increase in young graduate unemployment to remote work rather than AI; (2) Early-career professionals face a "proximity penalty" with 31% fewer promotions, reduced mentorship, and significant networking challenges; (3) Remote work demands higher levels of self-management, digital literacy, and asynchronous communication skills compared to traditional office roles; (4) While technology and finance lead in remote adoption, a wave of return-to-office mandates since 2024 is reshaping the landscape, creating a two-tier system where flexibility is increasingly tied to seniority and bargaining power.

## 1. The Remote Work Landscape: Current State in the US and Europe

### 1.1 United States: Remote Work Has Stabilized at Elevated Levels

As of mid-2025, approximately 34–37 million Americans work remotely at least partially, representing 21–23% of the employed workforce according to the Bureau of Labor Statistics. This marks a dramatic increase from just 6.5% in 2019, though the rate has stabilized since late 2022 [1][2][3].

The Congressional Research Service reports that 29.4% of total paid workdays were performed fully from home as of January 2025, compared to 7.2% in January 2019 and a peak of 61.5% in May 2020 [4]. Gallup's Q2 2025 survey of 17,660 U.S. adults reveals the current distribution of remote-capable workers: 28% fully remote, 51% hybrid, and 21% on-site [5].

The shift toward hybrid work is notable. The BLS reports that the share of teleworkers who worked all their hours from home decreased from 54.0% to 47.9% between Q1 2023 and Q1 2024, while the average weekly hours worked from home declined by 2.1 hours over the same period [1]. This indicates a maturation of remote work arrangements into a predominantly hybrid model.

### 1.2 Europe: A Heterogeneous Landscape

In the European Union, 22% of employed people worked from home at least occasionally in 2023, an 8 percentage point increase since 2019 according to Eurostat [6]. This breaks down as 9% usually working from home and 13% working from home occasionally. By 2025, 8.9% of EU employees usually worked from home (defined as at least half the time in a 4–12 week reference period) [7].

Significant variation exists across EU member states. The Netherlands leads with 51.9% of workers working from home at least occasionally, followed by Sweden (45.3%) and Finland (approximately 42%). At the lower end, Romania and Bulgaria report approximately 3%, while Italy and Spain sit under 15% [6]. This variation reflects differences in industrial structure, digital infrastructure, and cultural attitudes toward flexible work.

The Global Survey of Working Arrangements (G-SWA), covering 16,422 college-educated full-time employees across 40 countries, shows that the average number of work-from-home days per week has stabilized at roughly 1.2 days globally since 2023, with North America and Europe leading and Asia showing the lowest rates [8]. Hybrid working is now the dominant form of remote work in Europe, with nearly one in five European workers in a hybrid arrangement by 2023, while only one in ten worked fully remotely [9].

### 1.3 Demographic Disparities in Remote Work Access

A critical finding for this report is the significant age-based disparity in remote work access. According to the BLS, only 7.9% of workers aged 16–24 teleworked in Q1 2024, compared to approximately 25% for workers aged 25–54 and 24% for those 55+ [1]. In the UK, only 6% of 16–29 year olds worked from home compared to 15% of 30–49 year olds and 16% of 50–60 year olds as of April 2026 [10].

Educational attainment is the strongest predictor of remote work. The BLS reports that 43.6% of workers with advanced degrees telework, compared to just 3.1% of those without a high school diploma [1]. In the UK, workers with a degree or equivalent qualification were 10 times more likely to hybrid work than those with no qualifications [11].

Industry concentration also matters. Finance and insurance leads with 30% fully remote, while information/technology has 22% fully remote and 49% hybrid [3]. The occupations with the highest telework rates include computer and mathematical roles (68.5%), management and professional roles (37.9%), and sales and office support (24.9%) [2].

## 2. Remote Work and Entry-Level Job Opportunities

### 2.1 The Remote Work Paradox: Fewer Opportunities for New Graduates

Perhaps the most significant finding for this report is the emerging evidence that remote work—not artificial intelligence—is the primary driver of rising unemployment among young college graduates. A June 2026 study from the Federal Reserve Bank of New York by Natalia Emanuel, Emma Harrington, and Amanda Pallais argues that the rise in remote work since the pandemic accounts for 64% of the increase in youth unemployment among college graduates under 29 [12][13].

The data is striking: youth unemployment (under 29) rose from 3.1% in 2017–2019 to 3.7% in 2022–2025, while unemployment for experienced workers (29+) dipped from 1.9% to 1.8%. The authors find that this increase is concentrated in "remotable" occupations, and the timing of the surge suggests remote work—not generative AI—explains the bulk of the rise [12].

A separate study by Lambert and Schindler, analyzing resume and job board data from 2017–2019 versus 2023–2025, found that entry-level hiring dropped 29% and junior-level hiring 26%, while senior-level hiring increased 5%. When controlling for both AI exposure and remote work, remote work remained a robust predictor of declining junior hiring, while AI's effect became statistically insignificant [14].

The mechanism is clear: employers are reluctant to hire and train inexperienced workers when they cannot provide in-person mentorship and oversight. As the NY Fed study notes, when people work next to their colleagues, they receive more feedback on their output and more mentorship. When separated by even a short distance, that feedback tapers off dramatically [12].

### 2.2 BLS Youth Employment Data

The BLS reported on August 21, 2025, that youth employment (ages 16–24) declined in July 2025 compared to July 2024. The employment-population ratio fell to 53.1% from 54.5%, and the unemployment rate rose to 10.8% from 9.8%. The youth labor force increased by 1.9 million (8.9%) from April to July 2025, reaching 23.7 million, with a labor force participation rate of 59.5% [15].

The top industry for employed youth was leisure and hospitality (25%), followed by retail trade (17%) and education/health services (14%). These are sectors with relatively low remote work adoption, suggesting that young workers are being channeled into in-person roles while remote-capable positions increasingly go to experienced professionals [15].

### 2.3 The European Perspective: Similar Patterns Emerge

In France, an INSEE analysis from March 2026 reveals that employment of under-30s in IT and information services sectors has fallen sharply, coinciding with the rise of generative AI and remote work. Between end 2023 and end 2025, salaried employment in these sectors fell by 3%, driven by the 15–29 age group who contribute -3.8 percentage points to this decline. In Q4 2025, employment of under-30s in IT fell by 7.4% year-on-year, while the sector's value added continued to progress [16].

INSEE notes that "as in the United States, these results seem to suggest that the adjustment of employment with respect to AI is first materialized by a slowdown in entries and recruitment for early career positions rather than by a generalized contraction" [16].

The French statistical agency also found that juniors telework less than mid-career employees, partly due to job eligibility but also because "a higher proportion of them report that their employers do not want them to telework, possibly to promote their supervision and integration into the work collective" [17].

### 2.4 The Supply-Demand Mismatch in Remote Hiring

LinkedIn data reveals a stark supply-demand imbalance. Bonnie Dilber, a recruiting leader at Zapier, highlights that only about 8% of LinkedIn postings in the US are remote (~300k out of 4M), yet they attract 52% of applications [18]. Madeline Mann reports that while 75% of professionals want remote work, only 12% of Q3 2025 jobs were remote [19].

Robert Half's 2026 data shows that senior-level roles see more flexibility (20% hybrid, 8% remote) than entry-level roles (13% hybrid, 6% remote) [20]. This creates a Catch-22 for early-career professionals: the jobs most accessible to them are least likely to offer remote flexibility, while the remote roles they desire increasingly require experience they cannot obtain.

## 3. Career Growth, Mentorship, and Promotion Rates

### 3.1 The Proximity Penalty: Promotion Rates and Remote Work

The evidence is clear that remote workers face significant disadvantages in career advancement. An analysis of two million white-collar workers by Live Data Technologies, reported by the Wall Street Journal, found that fully remote workers were promoted 31% less frequently than those working in-office or hybrid. Specifically, 5.6% of in-office/hybrid workers received promotions in 2023 versus 3.9% of remote workers [21].

Stanford economist Nick Bloom attributes this disparity to "proximity bias," which he calls discrimination [21]. The Oyster report confirms these findings, noting that remote workers are promoted 31% less frequently, hybrid roles pay $22,000 less per year, and fully remote workers are 35% more likely to be laid off [22].

A Forbes article from September 2023 reports that entry-level remote employees at Fortune 500 companies had a 50% lower promotion rate than on-site colleagues [23].

### 3.2 Gender Disparities in Remote Career Progression

The 2025 LeanIn.org and McKinsey Women in the Workplace report reveals a significant gender gap in promotions for remote workers: 37% of women who work remotely 3+ days per week received a promotion in the past two years, compared to 49% of men. For entry-level remote workers, the gap is even wider: 25% of women versus 44% of men. In contrast, among on-site employees, the promotion rate is nearly equal (53% women, 54% men) [24].

LeanIn.org CEO Rachel Thomas calls the remote work penalty a "double whammy," and McKinsey partner Alexis Krivkovich warns that remote jobs may carry a "hidden penalty" specifically for women [24].

### 3.3 The Feedback and Mentorship Deficit

The NY Fed study of a Fortune 500 tech company provides granular evidence of the feedback deficit. Software engineers received about 20% more feedback when sitting near colleagues than when distant from them, with young workers affected most severely [13]. A separate "Power of Proximity" study showed that in-person software engineers received 18% more coding feedback and were more likely to ask follow-up questions [14].

The NBER study by Emanuel, Harrington, and Pallais (NBER Working Paper 31880) examines these tradeoffs in detail. Pre-pandemic, engineers in the same building received 22% more code comments than those in separate buildings, but produced 23% fewer programs. This feedback benefited junior engineers at the cost of senior engineers' productivity. Women received 40% more feedback when co-located, but senior women faced larger output declines [25].

The study also found that the tech company shifted hiring from new graduates to older workers during remote work, then resumed hiring new graduates after implementing a strict return-to-office policy. This suggests that employers are willing to invest in training junior workers when proximity is feasible but avoid inexperienced workers when distance creates barriers to development [25].

### 3.4 European Evidence on Skills and Career Progression

A research article published in Administrative Science Quarterly (May 2026) by Wang, Zhang, and Liao investigates the relationship between remote work and hiring requirements across 28 European countries using over 50 million job postings from 2018–2021. The authors find that remote work is associated with higher skill and qualification requirements. Mechanisms include reduced face-to-face interaction (making training harder), a larger applicant pool, and greater reliance on quantifiable metrics. The results show that remote work leads to more required skills and more work experience demanded, indicating skill upgrading in the labor market [26].

McKinsey's analysis of how AI is reshaping entry-level jobs advises organizations to redesign early-career development by focusing on three areas: targeting hiring based on intrinsic capabilities like adaptability rather than perfect skill matches; defining entry-level roles by developmental task combinations rather than leftovers after automation; and providing mentorship opportunities [27].

## 4. Skills Required for Remote vs. Traditional Office Jobs

### 4.1 The Shifting Skills Landscape

The skills required for remote work differ significantly from those needed in traditional office environments. Research consistently identifies several key competencies that are more critical for remote success.

LinkedIn's 2025 Future of Recruiting Report finds that 9 out of 10 executives agree that soft skills (human skills) are more important than ever [28]. LinkedIn highlights three essential soft skills for entry-level jobs: communication (especially conflict mitigation), adaptability, and creativity [29].

The World Economic Forum's Future of Jobs Report 2025, based on a survey of over 1,000 employers representing 14+ million workers across 22 industries and 55 economies, finds that analytical thinking remains the most sought-after core skill, with seven out of 10 companies considering it essential in 2025. AI/big data, networks/cybersecurity, and technological literacy are the fastest-growing skills [30].

### 4.2 Digital Competencies: The DigComp Framework

A study published in the National Institutes of Health database examined the demand for digital skills using the European Commission's DigComp 2.0 framework. Based on a cross-sectional online survey of 376 companies, the research found that the top three current digital skills in demand are "information and data literacy," "problem-solving," and "digital content creation." For future demand (next five years), the top skills shift to "problem-solving," "safety," and "communication and collaboration" [31].

Significant skill gaps were identified across all five categories. The largest gaps were in "communication and collaboration" (mean difference -0.927), followed by "problem-solving" (-0.864) and "safety" (-0.771). Entry-level graduates were rated as having average to poor competence in most digital skills, particularly in communication/collaboration (mean 2.713) and safety (mean 3.000) [31].

### 4.3 Self-Management and Asynchronous Communication

An academic paper from LACCEI (2025) examining the role of soft skills in remote work found that emotional intelligence predicts 18.8% of productivity and well-being variance, while assertive communication predicts 28.7%. Age and occupational maturity are strongly associated with higher soft skill proficiency. Professionals scored 13.5% higher than university students in self-management. Despite their technical fluency, digital natives (ages 18–24) show notable gaps in boundary-setting and emotional regulation [32].

The Toggl blog reports that 83% of HR leaders believe workforce demand is developing faster than workers' skills. Since 2016, 25% of job skills have changed, and by 2030, another 65% will further change. By 2027, almost half (44%) of workers' core skills will be disrupted. Two-thirds of employers say soft skills are more important than educational qualifications in hiring, and 92% of job posts already include requirements for digital skills alongside more traditional competencies [33].

### 4.4 Technical Skills and Platform Proficiency

The Stack Overflow Developer Survey 2025, with nearly 49,000 responses from 177 countries, provides a detailed picture of the technical skills landscape for remote-capable roles. 84% of respondents use or plan to use AI tools (up from 76% in 2024), and 51% use AI tools daily. Python adoption grew 7 percentage points in 2025, driven by AI workflows. JavaScript remains the most used language (66%), and Rust is the most admired language [34].

For remote work specifically, proficiency with collaboration tools is essential. LinkedIn lists "Proficiency with Collaboration Tools" as a top five skill remote employers are looking for in 2025 [35]. The Wokxstation emphasizes seven soft skills for remote work: communication, time management, self-motivation, adaptability, emotional intelligence, problem solving, and collaboration [36].

### 4.5 The Skills Gap in Europe

The OECD Skills Outlook 2025 examines how factors beyond individual control (gender, parental education, immigrant background, age, childhood residential context) are strongly associated with the acquisition of 21st-century skills. Socio-economic background is the strongest driver of skills gaps. Gender gaps vary by skill domain (women lead in literacy, men in numeracy and adaptive problem solving). Disparities begin early and widen through life [37].

McKinsey's May 2024 report on the future of work in Europe and the United States finds that by 2030, demand for social/emotional skills will rise 11% in Europe and 14% in the US. Europe may require up to 12 million occupational transitions (double the pre-pandemic pace), while the US faces nearly 12 million occupational transitions. Demand for STEM, healthcare, and high-skill roles will rise sharply (17–30% growth), while office support, customer service, and production work will decline [38].

## 5. Earning Potential: Remote vs. In-Office

### 5.1 The Wage Premium Debate

The relationship between remote work and earnings is complex and varies significantly by occupation, education level, and bargaining power. The Congressional Research Service reports that remote workers earned a 13.3% higher average per-hour wage in 2021 compared to on-site workers, but the premium varies by field, with some occupations (e.g., health care support) facing wage penalties [4].

A study of over 2 million job postings on a Chilean job board (January 2026) found that fully remote jobs carry a 24% wage premium in instrumental variable specifications, while hybrid jobs have a 3.6% wage discount. The results suggest that fully remote roles reduce local monopsony power and improve matching/talent, whereas hybrid jobs reflect amenity value [39].

The Federal Reserve Bank of San Francisco found that employees who work from home at least part of the time earned, on average, 12% more per hour than those who work entirely on-site. However, the study notes that the most productive workers or those with better negotiation skills are more likely to secure both higher hourly wages and the right to work from home. About half of that premium is explained by demographics (age, gender, education), and the remaining 6% is attributed to a "teleworking premium" linked to seniority and bargaining power [40].

### 5.2 The Compensating Differential: Willingness to Pay for Flexibility

The NBER working paper by Cullen, Pakzad-Hurson, and Perez-Truglia (January 2025) provides perhaps the most rigorous estimate of how much employees value remote work. Using revealed preferences from real job offers, the study finds that employees are willing to accept a 25% pay cut (95% CI: 10.5%–40.7%) for remote or hybrid roles—three to five times larger than previous hypothetical-survey estimates. Despite this strong preference, a compensating wage differential is not observed; using salary data from 4,352 groups (same company, position, location, experience), remote positions pay on average 1.1% more than identical in-person roles [41].

The Owl Labs 2025 State of Hybrid Work report confirms that workers would sacrifice an average of 9% of salary for flexible hours, and the average cost of working in-office is $55 per day [42]. The Interview Guys reports that 40% of workers would accept a pay cut of 5% or more for flexibility, valued at an 8% salary increase equivalent [43].

### 5.3 The Remote Work Pay Gap for Entry-Level Professionals

For early-career professionals, the wage dynamics are more challenging. The Oyster report notes that hybrid roles pay $22,000 less per year, and fully remote workers are 35% more likely to be laid off [22]. The CRS report on real wage trends (2014–2024) shows that cumulative real wage growth was strongest at the bottom (23.0% at the 10th percentile), but these gains are concentrated among in-person roles in sectors like leisure and hospitality, not remote-capable positions [44].

The paper "Remote Work, Wages, and Hours Worked in the United States" (Pabilonia and Vernon, 2023) found that real wages grew 4.4 percent faster for remote workers within detailed occupation groups, and remote work intensity was positively associated with wage growth. However, the authors note that this premium is driven by sorting—more productive workers select into remote work—rather than a causal effect of remote work on wages [45].

### 5.4 European Wage Context

Eurofound data shows that teleworkers are twice as likely to exceed the 48-hour working time limit, take insufficient rest, and work in their free time, with knock-on effects on their physical and mental health. Despite the flexibility, there are risks that telework leads to deterioration of work-life balance, higher stress levels, and failing worker health [46].

The European Commission's Quality Jobs Roadmap (December 2025) emphasizes that "every job in Europe must be a quality job" and outlines five focus areas: creating and maintaining quality jobs; ensuring fairness and modernisation in the world of work; supporting workers and employers in green, digital and demographic transitions; strengthening social dialogue and collective bargaining; and ensuring effective access to rights, quality public services, and adequate investment [47].

## 6. Professional Networking and Social Capital

### 6.1 The Networking Deficit for Remote Workers

The impact of remote work on professional networking for early-career professionals is substantial. The NY Fed study found that software engineers received 20% more feedback when sitting near colleagues than when distant, and this deficit hit young workers much harder [13]. The "Power of Proximity" study confirmed that in-person engineers received 18% more coding feedback and were more likely to ask follow-up questions [14].

The Qooper blog on early career mentoring (2026) reports that 25% of mentees experienced a salary grade change compared to only 5% of those without mentoring programs. 83% of professionals who have had a mentor reported being satisfied with their career progression. However, only 15% of employees say their manager helped them build a career plan in the past six months—a decline of 5 percentage points from 2024 [48].

### 6.2 The Loneliness and Isolation Factor

Gallup's State of the Global Workplace 2025 report reveals that 20% of employees report daily loneliness, with the highest rates among fully remote workers (25%). Exclusively remote workers report the highest engagement at 31% compared with hybrid (23%) and on-site remote-capable (23%), but they are also more likely to report experiencing anger, sadness, and loneliness than hybrid and on-site workers. Only 36% of fully remote workers are thriving in life overall, versus 42% for hybrid and on-site remote-capable workers [49].

The Owl Labs 2025 survey found that 90% of employees feel stressed at work, driven by lack of career growth, limited flexibility, job stability concerns, and monitoring. Managers' top concerns are employee engagement, IT support, and satisfaction [42].

### 6.3 Building Networks in a Remote World

For early-career professionals, the challenge of building professional networks remotely is acute. McKinsey's 2023 survey found that 74% of remote workers reported facing challenges in maintaining regular communication with colleagues [50]. The Eurofound 2024 e-survey notes that remote work may change the nature of harassment, as isolation and disconnection from colleagues can enable more subtle or hidden forms of harassment [51].

The LinkedIn 2025 Workplace Learning Report shows that only 36% of organizations qualify as "career development champions" with robust programs that yield business results. 91% of L&D professionals agree continuous learning is more important than ever, and 88% of organizations are concerned about retention, with learning as the top retention strategy [52].

### 6.4 European Perspectives on Networking and Social Capital

Eurofound's "Future of telework and hybrid work" report (2023) emphasizes that equitable telework/hybrid work improves job quality and organisational practices, but without careful planning, changing work arrangements have the potential to disrupt workplace dynamics and accentuate gender imbalances. The report recommends organisations invest in manager training, re-examine health and safety risk assessments, build a culture of trust, and consider advanced technologies for remote management [53].

The U.S. Career Institute reports that 36% of remote workers found their onboarding experience at a new remote company confusing [54]. Gallup finds that only 21% of hybrid workers have received required or optional training for how to work effectively in a hybrid work environment. Having an effective manager leads to four times higher employee engagement and wellbeing, regardless of how often they work remotely [55].

## 7. Industries and Companies Most Committed to Flexible Work

### 7.1 Industry Rankings: Where Remote Work Thrives

Technology and information services lead in remote work adoption. Forbes Advisor reports that the top industry for remote workers in 2025 is Computer & IT, followed by Accounting & Finance, Marketing, and Medical & Health [56]. Within technology, 67% of tech employees work remotely, and 68% of US tech jobs are performed from home [57].

Finance and fintech show strong adoption, with 85% of financial firms having adopted remote or hybrid arrangements. However, only 5% of finance roles are fully remote, with hybrid being the dominant model [57]. Marketing and creative roles have the highest fully remote share at 9% [58].

Robert Half's 2026 data provides detailed industry breakdowns:
- Marketing & creative: 21% hybrid, 9% remote
- Legal: 23% hybrid, 5% remote
- Administrative & customer support: 8% hybrid, 5% remote (87% on-site)
- Healthcare: 6% hybrid, 9% remote (85% on-site) [20]

By seniority level, the data shows a clear hierarchy: senior-level roles (5+ years) see 20% hybrid and 8% remote, while entry-level roles see only 13% hybrid and 6% remote [20].

### 7.2 US Company Policies: The Return-to-Office Wave

The period from 2024 to 2026 has seen a significant shift toward return-to-office mandates, creating a diverging landscape of company policies.

**Full RTO (5 days in-office):**
- Amazon: Full 5-day RTO from January 2025 [59][60]
- JPMorgan Chase: 5 days/week from March 2025 [60]
- Goldman Sachs: 5 days [59]
- Tesla: Strict 5-day since 2022 [59]
- Dell: 5 days/week from March 2025 [60]
- Walmart: 5 days/week with relocation requirement to Bentonville, AR [60]
- AT&T: 5 days/week from January 2025 [60]
- Home Depot: 5 days/week from April 2026 (800 corporate roles cut; most were remote) [60]

**Hybrid (3 days in-office):**
- Google: 3 set days, tightened for previously remote teams near offices. Co-founder Sergey Brin told employees he recommends being in the office at least every weekday and that 60 hours a week is the "sweet spot of productivity" [59][60][61]
- Meta: 3 days hybrid (Instagram now 5 days from February 2026). Mark Zuckerberg stated: "Our early analysis of performance data suggests that engineers who either joined Meta in-person and then transferred to remote or remained in-person performed better on average than people who joined remotely" [59][60]
- Apple: 3 days [59][60]
- Microsoft: 3 days/week from February 2026 for Puget Sound, then other US offices, then international. Chief People Officer Amy Coleman stated: "How we work has forever changed... We're not going back, and we shouldn't... when people work together in person more often, they thrive — they are more energized, empowered, and they deliver stronger results" [62][63]
- Uber: 3 days/week from June 2025 [60]
- EA: 3 days/week from May 2025 (phasing out remote roles) [60]

**Remote-First/Flexible:**
- Spotify: Fully flexible, "Work From Anywhere" policy. Attrition down 50%. HR Chief Katarina Berg stated: "You can't spend a lot of time hiring grown-ups and then treat them like children" [59][64]
- Atlassian: Remote-first [59]
- GitLab: Remote-first [59]
- Coinbase: Remote-first [59]
- Airbnb: Remote-first/structured hubs [59]

### 7.3 European Company and Policy Context

In Europe, the regulatory environment is more supportive of remote work. The European Commission launched a two-stage social partner consultation on potential EU-wide legislation for remote work and the right to disconnect. The Commission cites EU data showing the proportion of people working from home nearly doubled from 11.1% in 2019 to 20% in 2022, with over 60% of Eurofound survey respondents wanting to work remotely at least part-time [65].

As of 2025, 13 countries had provisions on the right to disconnect in their national regulatory frameworks [46]. The European Commission established a new working group preparing provisions for European social security regulation regarding cross-border telework, effective July 1, 2023, allowing homeworkers to work from home up to 49% of their total hours without affecting their social security position [66].

In Germany, the ifo Institute reports that 24.1% of all German employees worked at least partially from home as of February 2024, a figure that has remained stable for two years. The rate varies by sector: 34.1% in services, 32.1% in large companies, 20.5% in small/medium enterprises, 16% in industry, 12.2% in trade, and 5.4% in construction [67].

In the UK, the CIPD report "Flexible and hybrid working practices in 2025" shows that 91% of employers offer some flexible working, and 74% have hybrid working (down from 84% in 2023). 41% of employers believe that an increase in home/hybrid working has led to increased productivity/efficiency, while 80% of employees say that working flexibly has had a positive impact on their quality of life [68].

### 7.4 The Two-Tier System: Entry-Level vs. Senior-Level Flexibility

A consistent pattern across both the US and Europe is that remote and hybrid flexibility is increasingly concentrated among senior professionals and high-skilled workers. The BLS data showing that only 7.9% of workers aged 16–24 telework versus 25% of those aged 25–54 is a clear indicator of this divide [1].

Robert Half's data confirms that entry-level roles have significantly less flexibility than senior-level roles [20]. This creates a structural disadvantage for early-career professionals: they are most likely to work in entry-level roles that require in-person presence, yet they are also the cohort that would benefit most from the flexibility and cost savings of remote work.

The OECD Skills Outlook 2025 notes that adult learning opportunities are unequally distributed, with those who already have higher skills more likely to receive training [37]. This compounds the disadvantage for early-career professionals in remote positions, who may lack both the formal training and the informal learning that occurs through workplace observation.

## 8. Policy Implications and Recommendations

### 8.1 For Employers

The research clearly indicates that employers must deliberately design remote and hybrid work arrangements to support early-career development. McKinsey advises organizations to redesign early-career development by focusing on three areas: targeting hiring based on intrinsic capabilities like adaptability rather than perfect skill matches; defining entry-level roles by developmental task combinations rather than leftovers after automation; and providing structured mentorship opportunities [27].

Gallup's research shows that work teams that set their hybrid policy together are the most likely to say that the policy is fair and has a positive impact on collaboration, but only 11% of employees benefit from this approach. Teams with a formal hybrid plan are 66% more likely to be engaged [69].

### 8.2 For Policymakers

The European Commission's Quality Jobs Roadmap (December 2025) represents a significant policy commitment to addressing the challenges of remote work. The roadmap focuses on five areas: creating and maintaining quality jobs; ensuring fairness and modernisation in the world of work; supporting workers and employers in green, digital and demographic transitions; strengthening social dialogue and collective bargaining; and ensuring effective access to rights, quality public services, and adequate investment [47].

Eurofound's "Future of telework and hybrid work" report recommends that national policymakers set minimum standards for areas like the right to disconnect, equipment costs, energy costs, health and safety, mental health, and equal treatment. EU-level monitoring is needed for cross-border telework, including tax and social security implications [53].

### 8.3 For Early-Career Professionals

For recent graduates and early-career professionals navigating the remote work landscape, the research suggests several strategies. First, prioritize in-person or hybrid roles during the first few years of employment to maximize mentorship, feedback, and networking opportunities. Second, actively develop the digital skills and self-management competencies that are essential for remote work success. Third, seek out employers with structured mentorship programs and formal hybrid work policies.

The LinkedIn data showing that remote/hybrid roles attract 60% of all job applications but represent only 20% of job postings indicates that the competition for remote roles is intense [70]. Early-career professionals may need to be flexible in their expectations, considering hybrid roles as a compromise that offers some flexibility while maintaining access to in-person mentorship and networking.

## 9. Conclusion

The remote work landscape for early-career professionals in the United States and Europe presents a complex picture of opportunity and challenge. While remote work has become a permanent feature of the labor market, its benefits are unevenly distributed. Young professionals face a "proximity penalty" that reduces their access to mentorship, feedback, and promotion opportunities.

The evidence from the Federal Reserve Bank of New York, NBER, and multiple academic studies suggests that remote work—not AI—is the primary factor driving the recent rise in youth unemployment among college graduates. Employers are reluctant to hire and train inexperienced workers when they cannot provide in-person mentorship and oversight.

The skills required for remote work are different from those needed in traditional office environments, with greater emphasis on self-management, digital literacy, and asynchronous communication. Early-career professionals often lack these competencies, creating a skills gap that limits their remote work opportunities.

The wage dynamics are equally complex. While remote workers generally earn higher wages, this premium is driven by sorting (more productive workers select into remote work) rather than a causal effect of remote work on wages. For early-career professionals, the lower promotion rates and reduced networking opportunities may offset any initial wage benefits.

The industry and company landscape is diverging, with technology and finance leading in remote adoption while a wave of return-to-office mandates since 2024 is reshaping the landscape. The most flexible arrangements are increasingly concentrated among senior professionals, creating a two-tier system that disadvantages early-career workers.

For policymakers, employers, and early-career professionals alike, the challenge is to design remote and hybrid work arrangements that preserve the benefits of flexibility while mitigating the costs to career development, mentorship, and social capital. The European Commission's Quality Jobs Roadmap and the growing adoption of right-to-disconnect legislation represent important steps in this direction, but much work remains to be done.
