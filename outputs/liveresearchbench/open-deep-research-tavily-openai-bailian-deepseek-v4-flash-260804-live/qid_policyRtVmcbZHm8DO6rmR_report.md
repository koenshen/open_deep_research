# Comprehensive Comparison of SEC Climate Disclosure Rule, EU CSRD, and California SB-219 (As of August 2026)

## Executive Summary

As of August 2026, the global climate disclosure landscape is characterized by significant regulatory divergence. The SEC's climate disclosure rule has been proposed for full rescission after never having taken effect, the EU's CSRD has undergone a major simplification via the Omnibus I package that reduced its scope by approximately 90%, and California's SB-219 (consolidating SB-253 and SB-261) is actively moving forward with its first emissions reporting deadline deferred to November 10, 2026. Multinational enterprises face a fragmented compliance environment where the most stringent requirements now come from the EU and California, while federal U.S. requirements have been effectively abandoned.

## 1. Framework Descriptions

### 1.1 SEC Climate Disclosure Rule

**Official Title:** *The Enhancement and Standardization of Climate-Related Disclosures for Investors* (Release No. 33-11275)

**Current Status (as of August 4, 2026):** The SEC proposed full rescission of the rules on May 29, 2026, with the comment period closing on August 3, 2026. The rules **never went into effect** after being adopted on March 6, 2024, and immediately stayed by the Fifth Circuit on March 15, 2024. The SEC voted to end its defense of the rules on March 27, 2025. A final rescission is expected by late 2026 or early 2027 [1][2][3].

**Purpose:** The rule would have required SEC-registered public companies to provide consistent, comparable information about climate-related risks reasonably likely to have a material impact on business strategy, results of operations, or financial condition. It was based on the TCFD framework and GHG Protocol [4][5].

**Key Features (as adopted):**
- New Subpart 1500 of Regulation S-K (governance, strategy, risk management, targets, GHG emissions, attestation)
- New Article 14 of Regulation S-X (financial statement footnote disclosures)
- Inline XBRL tagging required
- Scope 3 emissions were removed from the final rule

**SEC Chairman Paul S. Atkins (May 2026) stated:** "SEC disclosure obligations should comply with the Commission's statutory authority, be guided by materiality as the North Star, avoid the practical effect of dictating corporate behavior, and be imposed only when the expected benefits justify the likely costs and burdens" [6].

### 1.2 EU Corporate Sustainability Reporting Directive (CSRD)

**Current Status (as of August 2026):** The CSRD was adopted in November 2022 and entered into force in January 2023. The **Omnibus I Simplification Directive (EU) 2026/470** was published in the Official Journal on February 26, 2026, and entered into force on March 19, 2026. This significantly revised the CSRD's scope, thresholds, and requirements [7][8][9].

**Purpose:** The CSRD requires companies operating in the EU to report on environmental, social, and governance metrics with the same rigor as financial reporting, replacing and significantly expanding the Non-Financial Reporting Directive (NFRD). It aims to improve transparency, combat greenwashing, and provide investors with reliable sustainability data [10][11].

**Post-Omnibus I Key Changes:**
- **Scope reduced by approximately 90%:** Only companies with >1,000 employees AND >€450 million net annual turnover remain in scope
- **Listed SMEs removed** from mandatory scope (voluntary VSME standard available)
- **Non-EU company threshold raised** from €150M to €450M EU turnover
- **ESRS mandatory data points reduced by 61%** (from ~1,100 to ~430)
- **Reasonable assurance requirement removed** (limited assurance only)
- **Value chain cap** limits information requests from companies with ≤1,000 employees
- **Transposition deadline:** March 19, 2027 for CSRD amendments

**Revised ESRS:** The simplified ESRS was finalized by the European Commission on July 3, 2026, and is currently under scrutiny by the European Parliament and Council. Mandatory reporting applies to financial years beginning on or after January 1, 2027 [12][13].

### 1.3 California SB-219 (Consolidating SB-253 and SB-261)

**Current Status (as of August 2026):** SB-219 was enacted on September 27, 2024, consolidating and amending SB-253 (Climate Corporate Data Accountability Act) and SB-261 (Climate-Related Financial Risk Act). CARB approved initial regulations on February 26, 2026, but withdrew them in June 2026 for modifications. Proposed changes released July 27, 2026, defer the first SB-253 reporting deadline from August 10 to November 10, 2026. SB-261 enforcement is paused due to a Ninth Circuit injunction [14][15][16].

**Purpose:** SB-253 requires US-based public and private companies with annual revenues exceeding $1 billion that do business in California to annually disclose Scope 1, 2, and 3 greenhouse gas emissions in conformance with the GHG Protocol. SB-261 requires companies with annual revenues of $500 million or more to publish biennial climate-related financial risk reports aligned with the TCFD framework [17][18].

**Key Features:**
- First state-level mandatory climate disclosure requirements in the US
- Affects approximately 5,000+ entities for SB-253 and 10,000+ for SB-261
- Parent-level consolidated reporting permitted
- Scope 3 safe harbor for good-faith errors until 2030
- No private right of action; enforcement by CARB and California Attorney General

## 4. Data Verification Standards

### 4.1 SEC Climate Disclosure Rule (As Adopted)

- **Scope 3:** No requirement (removed from final rule)
- **Limited Assurance:** Required for LAFs by FY 2029; for AFs by FY 2031
- **Reasonable Assurance:** Required for LAFs only by FY 2033; AFs not required
- **Assurance Providers:** Must be independent, accredited, and subject to PCAOB oversight
- **Exemptions:** SRCs, EGCs, and non-accelerated filers fully exempt from GHG disclosure and assurance
- **Safe Harbor:** Forward-looking statements protected by PSLRA; historical facts and GHG emissions not protected

### 4.2 EU CSRD (Post-Omnibus I)

- **Limited Assurance:** Required from the first reporting year
- **Reasonable Assurance:** **Removed by Omnibus I**; the planned transition to reasonable assurance has been eliminated
- **Assurance Standards:** The European Commission must adopt limited assurance standards by July 1, 2027 (previously October 1, 2026). Until then, national standards may be used
- **Scope:** Covers all sustainability information reported under CSRD, including:
  - Compliance with ESRS
  - Materiality assessment process
  - Digital tagging (XHTML/ESEF format)
  - EU Taxonomy alignment
- **Assurance Providers:** Statutory auditor or other independent assurance services provider (Member State discretion)
- **Value Chain Cap:** Companies with ≤1,000 employees cannot be required to provide information to reporting companies

**PwC Analysis of Wave 1 Reports (March 2025):** Among 101 CSRD reports analyzed, 85% obtained limited assurance. Less than 5% had qualified opinions, approximately 25% had modified opinions, and about 10% received reasonable assurance (voluntarily provided) [20].

### 4.3 California SB-219

- **SB-253 Scope 1&2:**
  - **Limited assurance waived for 2026** (first reporting cycle)
  - Limited assurance begins in 2027
  - Transition to reasonable assurance by 2030
- **SB-253 Scope 3:**
  - Limited assurance begins in 2030
  - Safe harbor for good-faith errors until 2030 (no penalties for inaccuracies if made on reasonable basis and in good faith)
- **SB-261:** No specific assurance requirement for the climate risk report
- **Assurance Standards Under Consideration:** ISSA 5000, AA1000, ISO 14060, and AICPA standards
- **Assurance Provider Requirements:** Must be independent third-party verifiers; cannot both prepare and verify the same GHG inventory
- **CARB's Enforcement Policy:** Voluntary disclosure can result in 25-75% penalty reduction; minor violations may qualify for 75-100% reduction

## 5. Materiality Thresholds

### 5.1 SEC Climate Disclosure Rule: Financial Materiality

The SEC's rule used the **traditional U.S. Supreme Court materiality standard**:
- Information is material if "there is a substantial likelihood that a reasonable investor would consider it important in determining whether to buy, sell, or vote their securities"
- **Double materiality explicitly rejected**
- The SEC stated: "The rule states that materiality 'refers to the importance of information to investment and voting decisions about a particular company, not to the importance of the information to climate-related issues outside of those decisions'"
- **1% threshold** for financial statement effects: capitalized costs, expenditures, and losses from severe weather events must be disclosed if they exceed 1% of pre-tax income (with $100,000 de minimis) or 1% of stockholders' equity (with $500,000 de minimis)
- **The SEC's 2026 rescission proposal reaffirms:** "The Commission emphasizes that 'materiality is a concept inherently rooted in financial considerations'"

### 5.2 EU CSRD: Double Materiality

The CSRD uses **double materiality**, which is its defining feature and the most significant difference from other frameworks:

1. **Financial Materiality (Outside-In):** How sustainability issues affect the company's financial performance, position, and development
2. **Impact Materiality (Inside-Out):** How the company's activities impact people and the environment

**Key Parameters:**
- Companies must report on all ESRS topics that are material from either perspective
- Climate change (ESRS E1) has a special status: if deemed non-material, a detailed explanation is required
- The double materiality assessment determines which of the ~430 mandatory data points apply
- No financial threshold is specified; materiality is assessed qualitatively and quantitatively
- Under Omnibus I, all topical disclosures are now subject to materiality (no mandatory disclosures beyond the general requirements)

**The University of Chicago Business Law Review noted:** "There are over 1,000 references to material or materiality in the [SEC] Rule. The SEC has determined that in the context of the Rule a 'reasonable investor' only attaches importance to climate-related disclosures that helps her evaluate the material financial risks involved in investing in securities, not e.g., the impact on the environment caused by the public company's greenhouse gas emissions" [21].

### 5.3 California SB-219: Financial Materiality

- **SB-253:** No materiality threshold for determining which emissions to report; all applicable activities must be reported for covered entities
- **SB-261:** Climate risk disclosures based on TCFD framework, which uses financial materiality
- **No double materiality** required
- The laws apply based on revenue thresholds and "doing business in California" criteria, not on materiality of climate impacts

**Summary Comparison:**

| Aspect | SEC | CSRD | California |
|---|---|---|---|
| Type | Financial only | Double (financial + impact) | Financial only |
| Determination | "Reasonable investor" standard | Qualitative + quantitative assessment | No materiality filter for reporting |
| Climate-specific | 1% threshold for financial statement effects | Must explain if climate deemed non-material | N/A |
| Scope | Narrower (investor focus) | Broadest (stakeholder focus) | Intermediate |

## 6. Enforcement Mechanisms

### 6.1 SEC Climate Disclosure Rule

**Regulatory Bodies:**
- SEC Division of Enforcement (primary)
- SEC Division of Corporation Finance (disclosure review)
- PCAOB (auditor oversight)

**Penalties:**
- No fixed penalties specified; subject to securities laws
- Potential liability under:
  - Exchange Act Section 18 (civil liability for false/misleading statements)
  - Securities Act Section 11 (material misstatements in registration statements)
  - Exchange Act Rule 10b-5 (anti-fraud provisions)
- SEC administrative proceedings: cease-and-desist orders, civil money penalties, disgorgement
- Precedent: Deutsche Bank fined $19 million for greenwashing (2023)

**Private Right of Action:**
- No explicit private right of action created by the rules
- However, disclosures were to be "filed" (not furnished), subjecting them to anti-fraud provisions
- Investors could potentially bring private lawsuits under Rule 10b-5

**Current Status:** Since the rules never went into effect, no enforcement actions have been taken under the climate disclosure rules. The SEC's 2010 climate guidance remains the operative standard.

### 6.2 EU CSRD

**Regulatory Bodies:**
- National competent authorities in each EU member state (e.g., AMF in France, FCA in UK, BaFin in Germany)
- European Securities and Markets Authority (ESMA) for coordination
- European Commission for oversight of implementation

**Penalties:**
- Member state-dependent; each country must establish effective, proportionate, and dissuasive penalties
- Generally: up to 2.5% of annual turnover or €10 million+ for non-compliance
- Administrative fines, public reprimands, and injunctions

**Private Right of Action:**
- Indirect via member state implementation
- Some member states allow NGOs and affected parties to challenge non-compliance
- The CSRD itself does not create a direct EU-level private right of action

**Enforcement Examples (Post-Omnibus I):**
- Member states must transpose the Omnibus I amendments by March 19, 2027
- The value chain cap protects smaller companies from excessive information requests
- The removal of reasonable assurance reduces enforcement complexity

### 6.3 California SB-219

**Regulatory Bodies:**
- California Air Resources Board (CARB) — primary implementing and enforcing authority
- California Attorney General — may be involved in enforcement actions

**Penalties:**
- SB-253: Up to **$500,000 per year** for non-compliance (failure to file, late filing, or misstatements)
- SB-261: Up to **$50,000 per year** or $2,500 per day (not to exceed $50,000)
- Scope 3 errors shielded if reported in good faith (safe harbor until 2030)
- First-year enforcement discretion for SB-253 (2026)

**Private Right of Action:**
- **No private right of action** in the enrolled bill
- Enforcement handled exclusively by CARB or referred to the Attorney General

**Litigation Status (as of August 2026):**
- U.S. Chamber of Commerce and business groups sued CARB, challenging constitutionality
- Federal district court dismissed claims without prejudice (February 3, 2025) — not ripe for review
- Federal judge declined to grant preliminary injunction (August 13, 2025)
- Ninth Circuit granted temporary injunction for SB-261 only (November 18, 2025)
- Oral arguments on SB-261 appeal scheduled January 9, 2026
- SB-253 remains enforceable while SB-261 is paused

## 8. Real-World Enterprise Compliance Challenges

### 8.1 SEC Climate Disclosure Rule: Challenges (Despite Never Taking Effect)

**1. Regulatory Whiplash and Wasted Investment:**
- Companies spent significant resources preparing for the 2022 proposed rule, then the 2024 final rule, only to see it stayed and proposed for rescission
- Many large multinationals invested in climate data infrastructure, GHG inventory systems, and internal controls that are now without a federal mandate
- **Cost impact:** Companies that voluntarily prepared estimate first-year implementation costs of $500,000 to $2+ million for large companies

**2. Fragmented Compliance Landscape:**
- Harvard Business School Professor Ethan Rouen: "Major companies are going to wind up in the business of disclosure, and many are already doing some type of reporting"
- Companies face a "multijurisdictional nightmare" — SEC (rescinding), California (actively enforcing), EU CSRD (phasing in), ISSB (voluntary but widely adopted)
- "The bigger challenge is a fragmented reporting landscape where state laws, international standards, customer requirements, and investor expectations all diverge" [23]

**3. Data Collection and Quality:**
- A Deloitte survey found: 74% disclose Scope 1, 53% disclose Scope 2, only 15% disclose Scope 3
- Carbon Tracker report (October 2022): 98% of 134 carbon-exposed companies did not provide sufficient information to demonstrate how financial statements include climate impacts
- 99% of companies did not comprehensively disclose relevant assumptions and estimates

**4. Liability Concerns:**
- Safe harbor only covers forward-looking statements — not historical facts or GHG emissions
- "A materiality determination is costly and complex, and companies are vulnerable to liability, including lawsuits from their own shareholders, if their assessment is wrong" [21]

### 8.2 EU CSRD: Enterprise Compliance Challenges

**1. Data Collection and Scope 3 Complexity:**
- Scope 3 emissions represent 60-90% of most companies' total carbon footprint but are notoriously difficult to measure
- 70% of Scope 3 disclosures are rejected for quality
- 75% of companies feel unprepared for assurance
- **Cost impact:** Companies spend an average of $677,000 annually on climate-related disclosure

**2. Technology and Talent Gaps:**
- 90% of 'wave 2' organizations still rely on spreadsheets for ESG data management
- 47% of organizations still rely on spreadsheets for ESG data
- GHG accounting, climate scenario modeling, and regulatory assurance professionals are in critically short supply
- AI adoption for sustainability reporting nearly tripled to 28% (from 11% the prior year)

**3. Supply Chain Data Collection:**
- 62% of firms point to supply chain unpreparedness as a major challenge
- Private companies are indirectly affected through supply chain demands from larger companies
- 45,000 suppliers were requested to disclose through CDP by 330 leading corporate buyers in 2025

**4. Compliance Burden and Costs:**
- 57% of firms cite compliance complexity as a major challenge
- 49% flag high costs
- More than half of surveyed companies now spend over $1 million annually on ESG reporting compliance alone
- Nearly half of sustainability budgets are consumed by reporting requirements rather than environmental or social projects

**5. Post-Omnibus I Transition Challenges:**
- Companies that were previously preparing for Wave 2 (FY 2025 reporting) must now adjust to the delayed timeline (FY 2027)
- Companies that may now be out of scope (those with 250-999 employees) must decide whether to continue voluntary reporting
- The simplified ESRS (July 3, 2026) requires companies to update their gap analyses and materiality assessments

### 8.3 California SB-219: Enterprise Compliance Challenges

**1. Timing and Data Collection Pressure:**
- SIFMA member survey: Internal data collection and review can take 4-5 months; third-party assurance can require up to 3-6 additional months
- The November 10, 2026 deadline is still challenging for companies that have not yet started
- Many companies were left "in the dark" regarding whether they would be required to disclose due to CARB's delayed regulation adoption

**2. Definitional Uncertainty:**
- "Doing business in California" definition still being finalized
- Revenue is defined as "gross receipts" under California Revenue and Taxation Code §25120(f)(2), which is broader than financial statement revenue (includes interest, dividends, property sale proceeds)
- Entities must use the lesser of the prior two fiscal years of revenue to determine applicability

**3. Scope 3 Data Challenges:**
- Collecting accurate data from suppliers, many of whom lack tools to track emissions
- The law applies to emissions "regardless of location," meaning global suppliers are within scope
- CARB's initial Scope 3 requirement (2027) covers 5 mandatory categories: Purchased Goods and Services, Fuel and Energy Related Activities, Waste Generated During Operations, Business Travel, and Employee Commuting

**4. Bifurcated Compliance Landscape:**
- SB-253 remains enforceable while SB-261 is paused due to Ninth Circuit injunction
- Companies must comply with SB-253's November 10, 2026 deadline but face uncertainty on SB-261
- "A wait-and-see approach only increases risks of non-compliance" [24]

**5. Penalty Risk:**
- SB-253 penalties of up to $500,000 per year for non-compliance
- First-year enforcement discretion provides some relief but does not eliminate risk
- Scope 3 safe harbor applies only to good-faith errors

## 9. Strategic Implications for Multinational Enterprises

### 9.1 Managing Overlapping and Duplicative Requirements

Multinational enterprises face the most complex compliance challenge: navigating three (or more) regulatory frameworks with different scopes, materiality definitions, timelines, and assurance requirements. The following strategies are critical:

**1. Build a Single Data Platform:**
- "The most common, and costly, ESG compliance mistake is building separate reporting processes for each framework or regulation" [25]
- Companies should collect all ESG information in one place and use it as a single source of truth for multiple outputs
- Workiva, Persefoni, Novata, and other platforms integrate finance, governance, sustainability, and risk records

**2. Adopt "No-Regrets Moves" (PwC Recommendation):**
- Conduct a gap assessment using AI to compare current practices to requirements
- Perform climate risk analysis (physical and transition risks) even if qualitative
- Compile a thorough emissions inventory aligned with the GHG Protocol
- Strengthen internal controls and assurance processes to meet limited-to-reasonable assurance requirements

**3. Start with the GHG Protocol and TCFD (KPMG Recommendation):**
- "Starting with the Greenhouse Gas Protocol and TCFD frameworks as a baseline... will cover about 80% of the requirements" [26]
- The GHG Protocol is the common foundation for all three frameworks
- TCFD (now effectively IFRS S2) provides the climate risk disclosure framework

**4. Leverage CDP as a Trial Run:**
- "For 2026, the CDP questionnaire acts as a high-quality 'trial run' for the Corporate Sustainability Reporting Directive (CSRD)" [27]
- Companies reporting to CDP will be well prepared for mandatory frameworks
- CDP disclosure fees: $3,100–$7,300 for North America

**5. Use Interoperability Guidance:**
- The ISSB and EFRAG jointly published guidance on interoperability between ISSB Standards and ESRS to reduce reporting costs
- ISSA 5000, released in November 2024, is the first international standard for sustainability assurance, providing a common framework for all three regimes

### 9.2 Which Framework is Most Stringent?

**EU CSRD is the most stringent framework** for the following reasons:

1. **Double Materiality:** Requires reporting from both financial and impact perspectives, covering a much broader range of ESG issues (not just climate)
2. **Scope of Reporting:** Covers 12 ESRS standards across environmental, social, and governance topics, with ~430 data points
3. **Scope 1, 2, and 3 Required:** Mandatory GHG emissions reporting including full value chain
4. **Mandatory Assurance:** Limited assurance from the first year (though reasonable assurance was removed)
5. **Broad Applicability:** Applies to companies with >1,000 employees and >€450M turnover (though reduced from original scope)

**California SB-219 is the most immediately enforceable** for U.S. companies:
- First reporting deadline of November 10, 2026 (SB-253)
- SB-253 penalties up to $500,000/year
- No private right of action, but CARB enforcement is active

**SEC Climate Rule is the least stringent (and effectively dead):**
- Proposed for rescission; never took effect
- Would have been the least burdensome if implemented (climate-only, financial materiality, no Scope 3, phased assurance)

### 9.3 Practical Recommendations for Multinational Enterprises

**1. Prioritize CSRD Compliance:**
- "If companies have to comply with the CSRD, they're probably 75% to 80% of the way there in terms of generating the information that they need to comply with the SEC" (KPMG's Rob Fisher) [28]
- CSRD compliance will also substantially prepare companies for California requirements
- Start the double materiality assessment now, even for companies not yet in scope

**2. Treat California as the Immediate Priority:**
- "California is one of the few [sustainability reporting regulations] that is actually effective right now and clients have to comply with" (KPMG's Maura Hodge) [26]
- "We're seeing companies make the mistake of thinking regulatory uncertainty means they have more time. ... don't wait. It's not going away, and you're going to be in a much better position if you get 80% of the work done now" [26]
- Companies should have their Scope 1 and 2 emissions inventory ready for the November 10, 2026 deadline

**3. Build Interoperable Systems:**
- Design data collection and reporting systems that can produce outputs for multiple frameworks
- Use the GHG Protocol as the common denominator for emissions accounting
- Implement ISSA 5000-ready assurance processes to satisfy all three regimes

**4. Monitor Regulatory Developments:**
- SEC rescission (expected late 2026 or early 2027)
- CARB's final regulation for SB-253 (ongoing rulemaking)
- Ninth Circuit decision on SB-261 appeal
- Omnibus I implementation in EU member states (transposition deadline March 19, 2027)
- New state legislation in New York, Illinois, Washington, Colorado, New Jersey

**5. Engage with Assurance Providers Early:**
- The market for carbon assurance professionals faces a "severe shortage of specialized talent" [22]
- The global carbon accounting and emissions management consulting market is valued at $14.90 billion in 2026, growing at 12.6% CAGR
- Early engagement with assurance providers is critical to avoid last-minute capacity constraints

**6. Consider Voluntary Reporting:**
- Companies that fall out of scope for CSRD (250-999 employees) should consider continued voluntary reporting under the VSME standard
- CDP disclosure provides valuable preparation for mandatory frameworks
- Investor demand continues: 86% of S&P 500 companies disclosed climate-related data through CDP in 2023

---

## 10. Conclusion

The climate disclosure regulatory landscape as of August 2026 is characterized by significant divergence and fragmentation. The SEC's federal rule has been abandoned, leaving the EU CSRD and California SB-219 as the two primary mandatory frameworks. The CSRD remains the most comprehensive and stringent framework globally, even after the Omnibus I simplification reduced its scope by 90% and removed the reasonable assurance requirement. California's SB-219 provides the most immediate compliance deadline for U.S. companies (November 10, 2026) and imposes penalties of up to $500,000 per year.

For multinational enterprises, the strategic imperative is clear: **build a single, interoperable ESG data and reporting infrastructure** that can satisfy the requirements of all three frameworks. The companies that invest now in robust data collection, double materiality assessments, and assurance-ready processes will be best positioned to manage the evolving regulatory landscape efficiently and cost-effectively.
