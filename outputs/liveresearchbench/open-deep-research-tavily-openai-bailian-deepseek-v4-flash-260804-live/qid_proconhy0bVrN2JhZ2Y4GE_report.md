# Social Media and European Teenagers (13–18): A Comprehensive Evidence-Based Analysis (2021–2026)

## Executive Summary

This report synthesizes evidence from over 70 credible sources published between 2021 and 2026 to evaluate whether social media is "good for society" for teenagers aged 13–18 in Europe (including the UK, EU member states, and Nordic countries). The analysis is structured across four dimensions—mental health and well-being, educational and developmental impacts, social and civic outcomes, and safety and risk factors—and presents six pro arguments and six con arguments, each supported by statistics, expert testimony, case studies, and official reports.

The evidence reveals a deeply nuanced picture. Social media offers transformative benefits for marginalized youth, enhances digital literacy and civic engagement, and has driven unprecedented youth-led movements. However, it is also causally linked to rising rates of anxiety, depression, and loneliness; damages attention spans and displaces learning; exposes teenagers to pervasive misinformation and radicalization; and subjects them to record levels of cyberbullying, grooming, and exploitation—despite significant regulatory progress in Europe.

The central finding is that **the effects of social media depend profoundly on how it is used, by whom, and in what context**, rather than on any inherent property of the technology itself. European policymakers face the challenge of maximizing the demonstrable benefits while implementing robust, evidence-based safeguards against the well-documented harms.

## PRO ARGUMENTS: Six Ways Social Media Benefits European Teenagers

---

### 1. Mental Health & Well-being: Social Support for Marginalized Youth

**Thesis Statement:** For LGBTQ+ youth and other marginalized teenagers in Europe, social media serves as a vital lifeline for peer connection, identity validation, and social support that may be absent in their offline environments, with strong evidence showing protective effects on mental health when used actively.

**Supporting Evidence:**

- **The Trevor Project's 2025 research brief** (18,663 participants, 65% transgender/non-binary) found that 74% of LGBTQ+ youth agreed they go online to connect because it's hard to find in-person connections, and 73% agreed they can be their complete selves online. Transgender and non-binary youth were significantly more likely than cisgender peers to report these motivations. The brief warns that "using these results to justify limiting access to online platforms could have harmful consequences, possibly increasing isolation and reducing support for LGBTQ+ youth, thereby exacerbating mental health challenges and suicide risk." [1]

- **A 2024 systematic review in the Journal of Medical Internet Research** examined 26 studies and found that social media (especially Instagram, Tumblr, Twitter) provides a safe space for LGBTQ youth to connect anonymously, explore identities, and obtain social support. Quantitative evidence from 3 of 8 studies showed that social media use was associated with reduced mental health concerns (e.g., less depression, anxiety, loneliness) and increased well-being. [2]

- **The National Academies of Sciences report (2024)** on the potential benefits of social media states: "Social media can be valuable to adolescents who otherwise may feel excluded or lack offline support, including patients with rare diseases or disabilities, and those who struggle with obesity or mental illness, or come from marginalized groups such as LGBTQ+ young people." [3]

- **A 2025 study in *Children* (MDPI)** with 275 participants aged 13–19 found that rural youth reported significantly more social media use and online social support than urban youth. LGBT+ youth in rural communities reported greater social media use and support online compared to LGBT+ youth in urban communities. The authors conclude that "social media may increase accessibility to social support for youth who may be at risk for social isolation." [4]

- **A 2024 viewpoint paper in JMIR Mental Health** argues against social media bans, highlighting "increased social connection, reduced loneliness, safe spaces for marginalized groups (e.g., LGBTQ+ youth), and potential for mental health interventions." [5]

- **The WHO/Europe HBSC report (2024)** on nearly 280,000 adolescents across 44 countries found that non-problematic heavy users of social media report stronger peer support, even as problematic use (11% in 2022, up from 7% in 2018) is linked to lower well-being. [6]

**Analysis:** The evidence consistently shows that for marginalized youth—particularly LGBTQ+ adolescents who may face rejection, bullying, or isolation in their offline environments—social media is not merely a distraction but a necessary infrastructure for survival and well-being. The implication for European policymakers is clear: blanket restrictions or bans on social media access could disproportionately harm the most vulnerable teenagers, rather than protect them. This is not an argument for unregulated use, but for targeted, nuanced interventions that preserve access while mitigating risks.

### 2. Mental Health & Well-being: Identity Exploration and Self-Expression

**Thesis Statement:** Social media platforms enable European teenagers to explore their identities, express themselves creatively, and access mental health resources in ways that support healthy development, with Nordic research particularly highlighting the importance of active, authentic engagement over passive consumption.

**Supporting Evidence:**

- **A 2024 systematic review in *Adolescent Research Review* (32 studies, N=19,658 adolescents)** found that "active participation in social media, rather than the amount of time spent on it, was associated with more identity exploration. Authenticity on social media, not idealized self-presentation, correlated with higher self-concept clarity." The review concludes: "Overall, it seems to matter more for identity development what young people do on social media than how much time they spend on it." [7]

- **The National Academies report (2024)** notes that 70% of teens see social media as a creative outlet, with higher rates among Black and Hispanic teens. The report discusses how social media allows identity exploration, creativity, and authentic self-presentation. [3]

- **A Norwegian PhD thesis (Andersen, University of Bergen, 2025)** examined social media use, stress, and mental health among Norwegian adolescents and young adults using national survey data from SHoT 2022 (N=47,163). The research found that "students with more mental health problems often used social media for distraction and engaged in negative social comparisons, while those with higher life satisfaction more frequently received positive attention." The thesis concludes that "social media use might not be inherently harmful to young people's mental health, but certain experiences and patterns of use may pose risks for some individuals." It advocates for "school-based social media literacy programs as a promising avenue for health promotion." [8]

- **Pew Research Center (2025)** found that 74% of teens say social media makes them feel more connected to friends, and 34% of teens use social media to get mental health information, with Black teens more likely to do so (49%). [9]

- **The University of Rochester Medical Center** quotes child and adolescent psychiatrist Meredith Gansner, MD: "Teenagers are going through a pivotal stage of identity formation—dissecting where they come from, what they value, and who they want to be." The article notes that social media helps teens explore identity, find affirming communities, build friendships for those with social challenges, serve as a creative outlet, and provide a platform for advocacy. [10]

- **The MIT Press essay on adolescents' social media use and body image (Choukas-Bradley, July 2024)** recommends that "clinicians assess for how adolescents spend time on SM—not their overall screen-time. Recommending abstention from SM use is not only unrealistic, but it would likely be developmentally maladaptive for many adolescents as well." [11]

**Analysis:** The Nordic research tradition, with its emphasis on developmental psychology and nuanced measurement, provides particularly valuable insights. The distinction between active and passive use—between creating, connecting, and expressing versus passively scrolling and comparing—is the most important factor in determining whether social media helps or harms teenagers' identity development. European educational systems that integrate social media literacy into curricula, as recommended by the Norwegian research, can help adolescents develop the self-regulation and critical awareness needed to maximize benefits and minimize harms.

### 3. Educational & Developmental: Digital Literacy and Informal Learning

**Thesis Statement:** Social media platforms, particularly YouTube and TikTok, have become powerful informal learning environments where European teenagers develop digital literacy, critical thinking skills, and access educational content that supplements formal schooling, as documented by European Commission and OECD reports.

**Supporting Evidence:**

- **YouTube is the dominant educational platform for teens.** Pew Research (2023) found that 95% of U.S. teens aged 13–17 have ever used YouTube, making it the most widely used platform. YouTube has published data on how young audiences utilize the platform for education, demonstrating that entertainment and learning are not mutually exclusive. [12]

- **A 2025 study in *Information Management and Business Review* ("From Likes to Learning")** reviews the educational benefits of social media, finding that platforms like YouTube, TikTok, and Facebook offer diverse learning opportunities, support various learning styles through multimedia content, enhance engagement and knowledge retention, and facilitate collaborative learning and critical thinking. Studies indicate improved academic performance when social media is integrated into learning. [13]

- **The European Commission and OECD jointly published an AI Literacy Framework (2025)** establishing 19 non-binding competences across four domains: Engage with AI, Create with AI, Manage AI, and Shape AI. The framework notes that 88% of European teens aged 13–15 use AI tools weekly. Pia Ahrenkilde Hansen, Director-General for Education at the European Commission, stated: "Preparing young people for a world shaped by artificial intelligence starts in the classroom." [14]

- **The OECD report "Shaping Digital Education" (2023)** states: "If used effectively, these technologies promise to transform teaching and learning practices and enhance educators' ability to provide high-quality instruction, to reduce learning inequalities through more differentiated learning approaches, and to create more inclusive and efficient education systems." [15]

- **The EU DigComp framework (2025)** defines digital competence across five areas: information search/evaluation/management; communication and collaboration; content creation; safety/wellbeing/responsible use; and problem identification and solving. The European Commission's JRC states: "The importance of equipping individuals in Europe with robust digital skills cannot be overstated." [16]

- **The TEDx talk by Zoe Ruth Diego Jacob (2025)** notes that the #BookTok hashtag has 239 billion views, fostering digital literacy and a love of reading among teens. [17]

**Analysis:** The educational potential of social media is often overlooked in public discourse fixated on harms. Platforms like YouTube and TikTok host vast libraries of educational content—from science tutorials to history documentaries to language learning—that reach teenagers who may be disengaged from formal schooling. The EU's DigComp framework and the OECD's emphasis on digital skills recognize that competence with digital tools is now a fundamental requirement for employability and civic participation. The challenge is not to reduce social media use per se, but to ensure that educational use is maximized and that teenagers develop the critical evaluation skills needed to distinguish reliable information from misinformation.

### 4. Educational & Developmental: 21st-Century Skills Development

**Thesis Statement:** Active engagement with social media platforms helps European teenagers develop the 4Cs of 21st-century skills (critical thinking, communication, collaboration, creativity) as well as digital literacy competencies defined by the EU's DigComp framework, preparing them for modern workplaces and civic life.

**Supporting Evidence:**

- **The EU Digital Competence Framework (DigComp 3.0, 2025)** retains five competence areas including communication and collaboration, content creation, and problem identification and solving. Key statistics: in 2023 only 56% of EU adults had basic digital skills (target 80% by 2030), and 43% of secondary students lacked basic digital skills. [16]

- **A 2023 article in the *Journal of Intelligence*** examines the 4Cs of 21st-century skills (creativity, critical thinking, collaboration, communication) and notes that up to 50% of employees may need reskilling by 2025 (World Economic Forum). The article proposes a "Dynamic Interactionist Model of the 4Cs" for pedagogy and policy. [18]

- **Pew Research Center (2022)** found that 71% of teens say social media helps them show their creative side, and 80% say it helps them feel more connected to friends. [12]

- **The SmartSocial.com resource (2025)** notes that social media enables students to "share their creativity and their ideas with a neutral audience and get an honest response" and allows students to "dip their toes into networking – albeit digitally." [19]

- **The University of Rochester Medical Center** notes that social media serves as a "creative outlet" and "platform for advocacy and purpose" for teens, helping them find "a sense of purpose and belonging" that is "deeply meaningful for their development." [10]

**Analysis:** The skills required for the 21st-century economy—digital communication, online collaboration, content creation, and personal branding—are exactly the skills that active social media use develops. A teenager who manages a YouTube channel, collaborates on a Discord server, or creates educational TikTok content is developing competencies that are directly transferable to modern workplaces. The EU's Digital Decade target of 80% of adults having basic digital skills by 2030 underscores the importance of these competencies. The key distinction is between passive consumption (scrolling, watching) and active creation (posting, collaborating, producing), with the latter being strongly associated with skill development.

### 5. Social & Civic Outcomes: Youth Civic Engagement and Political Awareness

**Thesis Statement:** Social media has become a primary driver of youth civic engagement and political awareness across Europe, exemplified by the Greta Thunberg-led climate activism movement, with Eurobarometer data showing that a majority of young Europeans use social media as their main source of political information and participate in civic activities online.

**Supporting Evidence:**

- **65% of young Europeans aged 15–24 say social media is their main source of information** (Flash Eurobarometer, 2025, as cited in the European Parliament's EPRS briefing, December 2025). Almost all young people in the EU use the internet daily. [20]

- **The European Parliament's Youth Survey 2024** shows that young people are actively engaged in civic and democratic life. The survey found that 64% of young Europeans have participated in the activities of one or more organizations in the past year. [21]

- **Eurostat data (2025)** shows that nearly one in four Europeans aged 16–29 engaged in civic or political issues online in 2025. [22]

- **Youth turnout in European Parliament elections rose significantly** between 2014 and 2019: under-25s increased by 14% (to 42%) and 25–39 year olds by 12% (to 47%), driven by a sense of civic duty and responsibility for the future. Climate change is a top priority for 40% of youth aged 15–24 (vs. 31% of the general EU population). [23]

- **The Greta Thunberg effect is the most powerful example of social media-driven civic engagement.** Greta Thunberg (born 2003) began her first "School Strike for Climate" in August 2018 at age 15 outside the Swedish parliament, sparking the global Fridays For Future movement. She was named Time Person of the Year (2019, youngest ever). Key quotes: "How dare you! You have stolen my dreams and my childhood with your empty words" (UN Climate Action Summit, 2019). [24]

- **A study in *Sustainability* (2020)** analyzed 1,744,446 English tweets containing "Greta Thunberg" from August 2018 to October 2019. Results showed that 44% of tweets were positive, 19% negative, and the rest neutral. Celebrities and public figures played an essential role in amplifying her message. [25]

- **The CIRCLE analysis (May 2025)** found that 77% of young people (ages 18–34) rely on social media/digital platforms for political information. Media literacy is strongly linked to voting: 81% of voters checked if online information was true vs. 65% of non-voters. [26]

**Analysis:** The Greta Thunberg phenomenon demonstrates how social media can transform individual teenage activism into a global movement, giving young people a voice in political debates that have historically excluded them. The rise in youth voter turnout in European elections, coinciding with increased social media use, suggests that digital platforms are not merely entertaining teenagers but are mobilizing them as citizens. However, the same platforms that enable authentic civic engagement also expose young people to misinformation and manipulation—a tension that European policymakers must navigate carefully.

### 6. Safety & Risk Factors: Regulatory Progress and Safety Tools

**Thesis Statement:** The UK and EU have developed comprehensive regulatory frameworks—the UK Online Safety Act 2023 and the EU Digital Services Act—that are now being enforced to protect teenagers online, requiring platforms to implement age assurance, safety-by-design features, and content moderation, while also promoting digital literacy and digital citizenship education.

**Supporting Evidence:**

- **The UK Online Safety Act 2023** received Royal Assent on 26 October 2023 and is being implemented by Ofcom in three phases. The Act creates a duty of care for online platforms, requiring them to tackle illegal content and content harmful to children. Companies can be fined up to £18 million or 10% of their annual global turnover, whichever is greater. [27]

- **The Protection of Children Codes of Practice came into force on 25 July 2025**, completing Phase 2 of Ofcom's implementation roadmap. Under sections 11 and 12 of the Act, user-to-user services used by significant numbers of UK children must conduct annual children's risk assessments and comply with safety duties. [28]

- **The EU Digital Services Act (DSA)** requires online platforms accessible to minors to conduct a "risk review" giving primary consideration to the best interests of the child. On July 14, 2025, the European Commission published final guidelines on the protection of minors under the DSA. The Commission also released a prototype age verification app to allow users to prove they are over 18 without disclosing other personal information. [29]

- **The European Commission's comprehensive approach** includes: the DSA banning targeted advertising to minors; the AI Act prohibiting manipulative AI systems targeting children; the Audiovisual Media Services Directive (AVMSD) mandating age verification, parental controls, and content rating; and the Better Internet for Kids (BIK+) strategy promoting digital literacy. 92% of EU citizens think that protecting children online should be a high priority. [30]

- **The European Parliament (November 2025)** adopted a report calling for an EU-wide minimum age of 16 for social media access (with parental consent for ages 13–16), a ban on addictive design practices (e.g., engagement-based recommender algorithms, loot boxes, dark patterns, targeted ads for minors), and age verification systems that preserve privacy. MEPs stated: "The attention economy has decided to make our children into guinea pigs." [31]

- **The European Commission's Special Panel on Child Safety Online (July 2026)** delivered recommendations proposing a shift in burden of proof requiring platforms to demonstrate safety before allowing children under 13 access. Commission President Ursula von der Leyen stated: "The burden of proof needs to be on providers, not regulators, parents and children." [32]

**Analysis:** Europe is leading the world in regulating social media platforms to protect children and teenagers. The UK Online Safety Act and EU Digital Services Act represent a paradigm shift from self-regulation to statutory duty of care, with substantial fines for non-compliance. The age verification initiatives, safety-by-design requirements, and bans on targeted advertising to minors are concrete measures that address the most serious harms. While enforcement remains a challenge—and critics argue that the regulations do not go far enough—the trajectory is clearly toward greater protection. The European model, which combines regulation with digital literacy education and youth participation in policy development, offers a template that other regions are beginning to follow.

## CON ARGUMENTS: Six Ways Social Media Harms European Teenagers

---

### 1. Mental Health & Well-being: Anxiety, Depression, and Self-Esteem

**Thesis Statement:** Social media use is causally linked to rising rates of anxiety, depression, and poor self-esteem among European teenagers, driven by social comparison, fear of missing out (FOMO), and algorithmic amplification of perfectionism culture, with the strongest evidence emerging from UK longitudinal studies and EU-wide surveys between 2021 and 2026.

**Supporting Evidence:**

- **NHS Digital's Mental Health of Children and Young People in England survey (2023)** found that 20.3% of 8 to 16-year-olds and 23.3% of 17 to 19-year-olds had a probable mental disorder in 2023, up from 12% in 2017 for children aged 8–16. [33]

- **The Guardian (June 2025)** reports on the NHS Adult Psychiatric Morbidity Survey: "Sharp rises in rates of anxiety, depression and other disorders have led to one in four young people in England having a common mental health condition." Among 16–24 year olds, prevalence rose from 18.9% in 2014 to 25.8% in 2024. Young women are disproportionately affected (36.1% vs 16.3% of men). [34]

- **The UK Millennium Cohort Study (published in *EClinicalMedicine*, 2019, of 10,904 14-year-olds)** found: "Greater social media use related to online harassment, poor sleep, low self-esteem and poor body image; in turn these related to higher depressive symptom scores." The magnitude of association was larger for girls: "≥5 h daily use: 50% increase in scores vs 35% for girls and boys respectively." [35]

- **The World Happiness Report 2026** states: "The evidence of harm is so strong and comes from so many sources in so many countries that we believe policymakers around the world now have enough evidence to justify action to protect children and adolescents." [36]

- **Internal Meta communications** cited in the World Happiness Report 2026 include: "Oh my gosh yall IG is a drug […] We're basically pushers […] We are causing Reward Deficit Disorder bc people are binging on IG so much they can't feel reward anymore." A TikTok executive acknowledged: "The product in itself has baked into it compulsive use." [36]

- **Pew Research (2025)** found: "Roughly half of teens (48%) say these sites have a mostly negative effect on people their age, up from 32% in 2022." [9]

- **The National Center for Health Research** reports: "In 2021, leaked documents revealed that researchers at Instagram found that using the app was harmful to teen girls' and boys' body image. About 1 out of 3 teen girls felt worse about their bodies due to using the app, and so did 14% of boys." [37]

**Analysis:** The convergence of evidence from multiple sources—longitudinal cohort studies, internal industry documents, government surveys, and international reports—makes the case for a causal link between social media use and declining mental health among European teenagers exceptionally strong. The dose-response relationship (more hours of use = worse outcomes) and the specific mechanisms (social comparison, sleep disruption, harassment) are well-documented. Young women are disproportionately affected, reflecting the particular pressures of appearance-based social comparison and online harassment. The fact that social media companies' own internal research confirmed these harms years before they were publicly acknowledged is particularly damning.

### 2. Mental Health & Well-being: Loneliness and Social Isolation

**Thesis Statement:** Intensive social media use among European teenagers is associated with increased loneliness and social isolation, as passive consumption of social networking sites displaces meaningful face-to-face interaction, with EU-wide evidence from the 2022 EU Loneliness Survey demonstrating this link.

**Supporting Evidence:**

- **The European Commission's Joint Research Centre (JRC) policy brief (2024),** based on the first EU-wide Loneliness Survey (EU-LS) of 25,646 respondents across all 27 Member States, found: "Spending more than 2 hours per day on social network sites is associated with a substantial increase in the prevalence of loneliness." Among 16–30 year-olds, 34.5% use SNS for over 2 hours/day, and "over one third of young respondents exhibit patterns of social media addiction." The JRC found: "There is not a significant association between intensive use of instant messaging tools or active use of social media and loneliness," suggesting that passive consumption (scrolling, watching) drives the effect. [38]

- **A GLO Discussion Paper (2025)** using the same EU-LS data found: "Intensive use of social networking sites correlates positively with loneliness and emotional distress, whereas excess use of messaging tools plays a very limited role. Further analysis suggests that the harmful effects of excessive social networking site consumption may arise from its predominantly passive nature, while instant messaging tools, which primarily involve active engagement, do not exhibit this detrimental impact." [39]

- **The Mental Health Foundation's research briefing on loneliness in young people** states: "During the first lockdown (April/May 2020), 35% of young people felt lonely often or most of the time, despite heavy social media use. By November 2020, almost half of 18–24-year-olds reported being lonely, and 69% of adolescents (13–19) said they felt alone 'often' or 'sometimes.'" [40]

- **WHO Regional Office for Europe (September 2024):** "A sharp rise in problematic social media use among adolescents in Europe, central Asia, and Canada: from 7% in 2018 to 11% in 2022." The HBSC study surveyed nearly 280,000 11-, 13-, and 15-year-olds across 44 countries. Problematic social media use is defined by "addiction-like symptoms (loss of control, withdrawal, neglect of activities, negative daily consequences)." [6]

- **A UK cohort study of 1,632 young adults (mean age 26)** found: "Total time spent online was associated with greater loneliness; however, use of social media specifically was not. Problematic technology use and experiences of victimization online were also associated with greater loneliness." [41]

**Analysis:** The EU Loneliness Survey provides the most robust European evidence for the link between social media use and loneliness. Crucially, the distinction between passive consumption (scrolling through social networking sites) and active communication (using messaging tools) is supported by the data: it is not social media per se that is harmful, but the passive, comparative, and often addictive modes of engagement. This finding has direct implications for intervention design: rather than simply reducing screen time, interventions should target the quality of engagement, encouraging active communication and reducing passive consumption.

### 3. Educational & Developmental: Attention Span and Deep Learning

**Thesis Statement:** Social media platforms' design features—including constant notifications, infinite scrolling, and algorithmic content cycling—fundamentally impair teenagers' attention spans, concentration capacity, and ability to engage in deep learning, with OECD PISA data and neuroscience research demonstrating significant negative effects on academic performance.

**Supporting Evidence:**

- **OECD PISA in Focus (2024/124):** "Nearly one in three students, on average across OECD countries, reported that students in their classes are distracted by using digital devices in most or every mathematics lesson." Those distracted scored an average of 15 points lower in mathematics—equivalent to three-quarters of a year of learning. [42]

- **The OECD report "Students, digital devices and success" (2024)** found: "65% of OECD students report being distracted by their own devices in at least some maths lessons, and 59% are distracted by peers using devices. Distracted students score about three-quarters of a year lower in maths. Students who used [devices] more than an hour a day for leisure… saw a big drop in Maths scores." [43]

- **Dr. Gloria Mark's research (cited in 2024)** found that "average attention span on a digital device dropped from 150 seconds in 2004 to 75 seconds in 2012, then to 47 seconds in 2024." [44]

- **The IJRE study (Menon, July 2024)** of 150 teen learners found: "A statistically significant negative correlation was observed between the duration of digital exposure and attention span (r = -0.65, p < 0.01). 80% of students with <2 hrs/day digital usage had high attention scores, while only 30% of those with >4 hrs/day did." [45]

- **Children and Screens (expert guidance)** states: "Adolescents struggle to control their use of devices and apps not because they're lazy or trying to be defiant, but primarily due to the brain science of adolescence itself." The guide notes: "Smartphones deliver 150+ notifications daily, causing both short-term task disruption and long-term attention problems. Forceful interruptions (e.g., notifications) are more harmful than intentional media breaks." [46]

- **The UK House of Commons Education Committee report (May 2024)** concluded: "The overwhelming weight of evidence submitted to us suggests that the harms of screen time and social media use significantly outweigh the benefits for young children, whereas limited use of screens and genuinely educational uses of digital technology can have benefits for older children." [47]

**Analysis:** The OECD PISA data is particularly powerful because it measures real-world academic outcomes on a large scale across multiple countries. The finding that device distraction is associated with three-quarters of a year of lost learning is staggering. The neuroscience research explains why: the adolescent brain is particularly vulnerable to the reward mechanisms that social media platforms exploit, and the constant interruptions from notifications train the brain to be in a state of perpetual partial attention. The result is a generation that may be less capable of the sustained concentration required for deep learning, complex problem-solving, and creative work. This is not merely an educational issue but a long-term economic and societal concern.

### 4. Educational & Developmental: Displacement of Traditional Learning

**Thesis Statement:** The time European teenagers spend on social media actively displaces engagement in traditional learning activities such as reading books, completing homework, and participating in extracurriculars, with UK Ofcom data and EU studies showing a significant and growing displacement effect.

**Supporting Evidence:**

- **The European Commission's Flash Eurobarometer (July 2026)** found: "On average, young people in the EU spend 4.5 hours online on school days and more than 6 hours at weekends." [48]

- **UK ONS data (year ending March 2023)** showed: "92.6% of children aged 10–15 went online daily or almost daily, with 58.1% spending three or more hours a day online on an ordinary school day, an increase from 47.6% in the year ending March 2020." [49]

- **The OECD report "How's Life for Children in the Digital Age?" (2025)** found: "By age 10, 93% had an Internet connection in 2021, and roughly 70% already own a smartphone. Among 15-year-olds, access is nearly universal: 96% have a desktop, laptop, or tablet at home, and 98% have a smartphone with Internet connection. At least 50% of 15-year-olds in most OECD countries spend 30 or more hours per week on digital devices." [50]

- **The OECD PISA analysis** found that leisure use of digital devices exceeding one hour per day at school is linked to lower mathematics scores (9+ points lower). [42]

- **The OECD Education Working Paper (No. 340, 2025)** on digital resources for learning found: "Moderate use is positively associated with mathematics performance, but excessive use (>5 hours/day) is linked to lower scores." [51]

- **The review in *Children* (2025),** analyzing 46 peer-reviewed studies from 2014–2024, concluded: "Higher screen time is linked to reduced physical activity, poorer sleep, attention difficulties, and challenges in emotional and social functioning." [52]

- **YoungMinds found:** "85% of young people aged 8–16 with a probable mental health condition experienced regular disruption to their sleep." [53]

**Analysis:** The displacement effect is straightforward: every hour spent on social media is an hour not spent reading, studying, exercising, sleeping, or engaging in face-to-face social interaction. European teenagers now spend an average of 4.5 hours online on school days and over 6 hours on weekends—time that, for many, directly replaces activities essential for healthy development. The displacement of sleep is particularly concerning, given the well-established link between sleep deprivation and both mental health problems and academic underperformance. The evidence suggests that even moderate social media use can push teenagers past the threshold where displacement effects become harmful.

### 5. Social & Civic Outcomes: Misinformation, Disinformation, and Radicalization

**Thesis Statement:** Social media platforms expose European teenagers to pervasive misinformation, disinformation, and radicalization through algorithmic echo chambers that systematically distort political awareness and civic understanding, with the European Commission, Oxford Internet Institute, and Reuters Institute documenting that young people are uniquely vulnerable to these harms.

**Supporting Evidence:**

- **The European Parliamentary Research Service (December 2025)** reports: "65% of 15–24 year-olds rely on social media as their main news source. 66% of youth encountered fake news in the past week. 49% of 16–29 year-olds saw hostile messages." [20]

- **The Reuters Institute Digital News Report 2025** (based on 100,000 respondents across 48 markets) found: "58% of respondents remain concerned about their ability to tell what is true from what is false when it comes to news online." Online influencers and personalities "are seen as the biggest threat worldwide (47%), along with national politicians (47%)." [54]

- **Ofcom (2022)** found that "only 11% of 11 to 17-year-olds could reliably recognise the signs that indicated a post was genuine." [55]

- **The ICWSM position paper (2025)** found: "42% of Europeans aged 16–30 get political news from TikTok, YouTube, or Instagram; 27% of TikTok users struggle to identify misleading content; 76% of young respondents encounter fake news at least weekly." [56]

- **The ISD Policy Digest (March 2025)** documents that "teenage terrorism offenders in England and Wales are increasing," with a UN Security Council report "highlighting decreasing age of radicalisation, use of AI and video games for recruitment, and the Gaza-Israel conflict as a catalyst." [57]

- **The DIIS Policy Brief (Denmark, 2025)** by Dino Krause reports: "Islamic State (IS)-related terror plots in Western Europe increasingly involve individuals under the age of 18. Central to this trend is IS's ability to disseminate propaganda online, effectively targeting younger audiences." Teenage plots account for 27% of IS-related plots in Europe. [58]

- **The UK Parliament Science, Innovation and Technology Committee inquiry (2024)** examined the anti-immigration riots of July–August 2024, which were "partly fueled by false claims spreading on social media after the killing of three children in Southport." Ofcom reported that "illegal content and disinformation spread 'widely and quickly' online following the attack, and that the riots demonstrated the role that 'algorithmic recommendations' can play in driving divisive narratives in a crisis period." [59]

**Analysis:** The misinformation crisis is not a future threat but a present reality that has already caused demonstrable harm. The UK summer 2024 riots, in which false claims about the perpetrator of a horrific crime spread across social media within hours, leading to violent attacks on mosques and asylum-seeker hotels, showed that online disinformation can have deadly real-world consequences. European teenagers are uniquely vulnerable because they rely on social media as their primary news source yet lack the critical evaluation skills to distinguish reliable information from falsehoods. The algorithmic radicalization of teenagers—documented across jihadist, far-right, and other extremist movements—represents a national security threat that European governments are only beginning to address.

---

### 6. Safety & Risk Factors: Cyberbullying, Grooming, Exploitation, and Harmful Content

**Thesis Statement:** Despite regulatory efforts including the EU Digital Services Act and UK Online Safety Act, European teenagers continue to face pervasive and growing safety risks on social media—including cyberbullying, online grooming, sexual exploitation, privacy violations, and exposure to harmful content—with official data from Europol, NSPCC, Ofcom, and EU Kids Online documenting record levels of harm.

**Supporting Evidence:**

- **The ONS (March 2024)** for England and Wales, year ending March 2023: "An estimated 847,000 children aged 10 to 15 years (19.1%) experienced an online bullying behaviour in the last year." Girls were more likely to experience online bullying (22.5% vs 16.0%). "18.1% who experienced an online bullying behaviour did not tell anyone." [49]

- **The WHO HBSC 2024 study** of 44 countries found: "16% of school-aged children experienced cyberbullying, with rates rising since 2018." [6]

- **The NSPCC (2024)** reports: "Online grooming crimes have reached record levels across the UK, with offences topping 7,000 for the first time. The figures show 7,062 Sexual Communication with a Child offences were recorded in 2023/24 – up 89% since 2017/18." [60]

- **The Childlight Global Child Safety Institute (University of Edinburgh)** found: "Over 300 million children a year are victims of technology-facilitated sexual exploitation and abuse — a rate of 10 per second. 12.6% of children (302 million) experienced non-consensual taking, sharing, or exposure to sexual images in the past year, and 12.5% (300 million) were subjected to online solicitation." [61]

- **The Internet Watch Foundation (IWF) 2024 Annual Report:** "AI-generated CSAM reports surged 380% (from 51 to 245 confirmed reports). 62% of all CSAM webpages found by the IWF in 2024 were hosted in EU countries—an 11-percentage point increase from 2023. 97% of identifiable victims were girls." [62]

- **Europol's 2025 IOCTA report** warns: "AI-generated child sexual abuse material (CSAM) reports rose 380% from 2023 to 2024. Criminals are using AI, stolen data, and encrypted channels to industrialize child exploitation." [63]

- **Ofcom research** (cited in UK public inquiry documents) found: "Children aged 8–17 described encountering content depicting or encouraging violence as 'unavoidable'." Specific statistics: "7% of 13–17-year-olds experienced persistent bullying, 5% saw self-harm encouragement, and 4% saw suicide encouragement in a four-week period. Over half of boys (52%) are aware of manosphere influencers, correlating with a rise in sexist language reported by 70% of teachers." [64]

- **The coroner's report for 14-year-old Molly Russell (UK)** concluded that "watching high volumes of content promoting suicide and self-harm had contributed to her death by suicide." [64]

**Analysis:** The safety data is the most alarming dimension of the evidence. The scale of online child sexual exploitation—300 million children worldwide per year, according to the Childlight Institute—is a global public health emergency. The surge in AI-generated child sexual abuse material, which has increased by 380% in a single year, represents a new and rapidly escalating threat that existing regulatory frameworks are not equipped to handle. The fact that 62% of CSAM webpages found by the IWF are hosted in EU countries is a direct challenge to the effectiveness of the Digital Services Act. The Molly Russell case, in which a 14-year-old girl was driven to suicide by algorithmically promoted self-harm content, demonstrates that these are not abstract risks but matters of life and death. The widespread nature of cyberbullying (19% of UK children affected) and the unwillingness of many victims to report it (18% tell no one) suggest that current safety measures are inadequate.

---

## Synthesis and Policy Implications

The evidence reviewed in this report reveals that social media's impact on European teenagers is neither uniformly good nor uniformly bad, but profoundly conditional on how, why, and by whom it is used. Several key patterns emerge:

**1. The "active vs. passive" distinction is the most important moderating factor.** Across mental health, educational, and social outcomes, active engagement (creating content, communicating with friends, joining communities) is consistently associated with positive outcomes, while passive consumption (scrolling, watching, comparing) is associated with negative outcomes. This finding has direct implications for intervention design.

**2. The effects are heterogeneous across populations.** Marginalized youth—particularly LGBTQ+ adolescents, those with chronic illnesses, and those in rural areas—derive disproportionate benefits from social media as a source of community and support. Blanket restrictions would harm these groups most.

**3. The harms are severe and well-documented.** The evidence for causal links between social media use and mental health deterioration, attention impairment, misinformation exposure, and exploitation risk is strong enough to justify urgent regulatory action. The internal documents from social media companies confirm that they were aware of these harms years ago.

**4. European regulation is progressing but insufficient.** The UK Online Safety Act and EU Digital Services Act represent significant steps forward, but enforcement remains inconsistent, and the rapid evolution of AI-generated harms is outpacing regulatory responses.

**5. Digital literacy education is a critical complement to regulation.** The European Commission, OECD, and Nordic research all emphasize that teaching teenagers how to use social media critically and safely is essential. The EU's DigComp framework and the OECD's AI Literacy Framework provide foundations for this.

**6. Age-based restrictions require careful calibration.** The evidence does not support a simple ban on social media for all teenagers, but does support graduated restrictions based on age, with stronger protections for younger adolescents (13–15) and more autonomy for older teens (16–18), combined with mandatory safety-by-design features.

---

## Conclusion

Social media is neither fundamentally good nor fundamentally bad for European teenagers. It is a powerful technology that amplifies both the best and worst aspects of adolescent social life. The evidence from 2021–2026 demonstrates that it can provide life-saving support for marginalized youth, enhance digital literacy, and drive civic engagement—but it can also cause severe mental health damage, impair learning, spread dangerous misinformation, and expose teenagers to exploitation. The challenge for European policymakers, educators, and parents is not to decide whether social media is good or bad, but to create the conditions—through regulation, education, and support—that maximize its benefits while minimizing its harms. The European approach, which combines statutory regulation with digital literacy and youth participation, provides a promising framework, but the evidence shows that much more needs to be done.
