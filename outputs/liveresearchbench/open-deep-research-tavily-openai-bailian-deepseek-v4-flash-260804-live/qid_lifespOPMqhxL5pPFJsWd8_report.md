# High-Altitude Pulmonary Edema (HAPE) in Ski Mountaineers at Altitudes ≥3,000 m: A Comprehensive Evidence-Based Synthesis

## Introduction

High-altitude pulmonary edema (HAPE) is a life-threatening, non-cardiogenic form of pulmonary edema that typically occurs in otherwise healthy individuals who ascend rapidly to altitudes above 2,500–3,000 meters. HAPE is the most common cause of death from high-altitude exposure, with mortality as high as 50% if left untreated [1][2][3]. For ski mountaineers—individuals who engage in ski touring, ski rando racing, or ski alpinism—the combination of intense physical exertion, cold air exposure, and rapid ascent creates a particularly potent stimulus for the development of this condition. This synthesis provides a comprehensive, evidence-based analysis of HAPE in ski mountaineers operating at altitudes ≥3,000 m, covering the predominant pathophysiological mechanisms, documentation of comparable cases, acute and long-term treatments with best outcomes, and acclimatization and prevention protocols. While the full historical record is considered, special emphasis is placed on literature published between 2020 and 2026.

## 1. Predominant Pathophysiological Mechanisms

### 1.1 Exaggerated Hypoxic Pulmonary Vasoconstriction (HPV) as the Central Initiating Event

The critical initiating event in HAPE is an exaggerated rise in pulmonary artery pressure in response to hypoxia [1][2][4]. The majority of current literature supports diffuse hypoxic pulmonary vasoconstriction (HPV) as the primary pathophysiological basis for HAPE [5]. HAPE develops not as a result of left ventricular dysfunction, but rather due to exaggerated HPV and marked rises in pulmonary artery pressure following ascent [2]. The crucial pathophysiology is an excessive hypoxia-mediated rise in pulmonary vascular resistance (PVR) leading to increased microvascular hydrostatic pressures despite normal left atrial pressure [4]. Drugs that lower pulmonary artery pressure (PAP) prevent HAPE, confirming that excessive PAP preceding edema formation is the crucial pathophysiological factor [4].

A key mechanism is that hypoxic pulmonary vasoconstriction is uneven, so that regions with less vasoconstriction are over-perfused and become edematous [4][6]. Recent studies using microspheres in swine and magnetic resonance imaging in humans strongly support the concept and primacy of nonuniform hypoxic arteriolar vasoconstriction [4]. In areas of high blood flow due to lesser HPV, edema develops due to pressures that exceed the dynamic and structural capacity of the alveolar capillary barrier [6]. This nonuniform regional arteriolar vasoconstriction, visualized by MRI, leads to overperfusion in areas with less constriction, raising capillary pressure above the critical threshold of 17–24 mmHg [4].

### 1.2 Nitric Oxide and Endothelin-1 Imbalance

Measurements of nitric oxide (NO) in exhaled air, of nitrites and nitrates in bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL) fluid, and forearm NO-dependent endothelial function all point to a reduced NO availability in hypoxia as a major cause of the excessive hypoxic PAP rise in HAPE-susceptible individuals [4][6]. HAPE-susceptible individuals have been shown to have decreased exhaled NO concentrations during acute hypoxic exposure at low elevation and during more prolonged exposure at terrestrial high altitude compared to nonsusceptible individuals [2]. Reduced NO bioavailability contributes to the excessive hypoxic pulmonary vasoconstriction [1][4].

Endothelin-1 (ET-1), the most potent endogenous vasoconstriction agent identified, plays a complementary role [1][5]. ET-1 is a 21-amino acid peptide produced primarily by endothelial cells, vascular smooth muscle cells, macrophages, and the renal medulla [1]. ET-1 is a potent vasoconstrictor with proliferative, pro-fibrotic, pro-oxidative, and pro-inflammatory properties, and maintains the tone of vascular smooth muscle cells [1]. In HAPE-susceptible individuals, measurements of endothelin and NO in exhaled air, NO metabolites in BAL fluid, and NO-dependent endothelial function in the systemic circulation all point to reduced NO availability and increased endothelin in hypoxia as a major cause of the excessive hypoxic PA pressure rise [4]. Elevated ET-1 levels contribute to excessive HPV, and several observations suggest that susceptibility to HAPE is associated with endothelial dysfunction in pulmonary vessels [4][6].

### 1.3 Alveolar Fluid Clearance Defects

Alveolar fluid clearance (AFC), which mainly depends on sodium transport in alveolar epithelial cells, is an important mechanism by which excess water in the alveoli is removed [7]. Transepithelial Na⁺ transport drives alveolar fluid clearance: Na⁺ enters the cell interior passively following an electrochemical gradient via Na⁺ channels (ENaC) on the apical membrane, and is then pumped out by Na⁺,K⁺-ATPase on the basolateral membrane, creating an osmotic gradient that drives water from the alveolar space into the interstitium [8][9].

Hypoxia impairs alveolar fluid clearance by reducing the expression and activity of ENaC and Na⁺,K⁺-ATPase in alveolar epithelial cells [8][10]. Under hypoxia, alveolar epithelial cells show reduced sodium transport and altered tight junctions, impairing fluid clearance and increasing permeability [10][11]. Hypoxia reduces ENaC surface expression and triggers Na⁺,K⁺-ATPase endocytosis via mitochondrial ROS, AMPK, PKCζ, and ubiquitination [8]. Both low O₂ and elevated CO₂ levels are sensed by the alveolar epithelium and by distinct and specific molecular mechanisms impair the function of the Na⁺,K⁺-ATPase and ENaC, thereby inhibiting AFC and leading to persistence of alveolar edema [12].

A study by Sartori et al. (2002) showed that high altitude impairs nasal transepithelial sodium transport, suggesting a systemic defect in sodium transport in HAPE-susceptible individuals [13]. This finding was further supported by the landmark salmeterol trial, which demonstrated that HAPE-susceptible subjects had over 30% lower nasal transepithelial potential difference values at low altitude, suggesting defective sodium-driven alveolar fluid clearance contributes to HAPE susceptibility [14].

### 1.4 Pulmonary Capillary Stress Failure

The seminal work by West and Mathieu-Costello (1992) proposed that HAPE is caused by stress failure of pulmonary capillaries [15][16]. The main features to be accounted for are the strong association with pulmonary hypertension, the high permeability characteristics of the edema, and the presence of inflammatory markers in the lung lavage fluid [15]. When the capillary pressure is raised to about 40 mmHg in anesthetized rabbits, ultrastructural damage to the capillary walls is seen including breaks in the capillary endothelial layer, alveolar epithelial layer, and sometimes all layers of the wall [15][17].

Excessive pulmonary artery pressure and pulmonary capillary pressures lead to a noninflammatory leak of the alveolar-capillary barrier with egress of red cells, plasma proteins, and fluid into the alveolar space [6]. The nature of the leak involves structural damage (stress failure) rather than simple pore opening [6]. Studies in humans confirm that high capillary pressure induces a high-permeability-type lung edema in the absence of inflammation, a concept first introduced under the term "stress failure" [4]. The pulmonary blood-gas barrier is an extraordinary bioengineering structure because of its vast area (50–100 m²) but extreme thinness (0.2–0.3 μm) [18][19]. The strength of the thin region of the barrier comes from type IV collagen in the basement membranes [18][19].

### 1.5 Genetic Predispositions

The ACE gene I/D polymorphism has been associated with HAPE susceptibility [20][21]. The DD genotype (deletion allele) is associated with higher ACE levels and was found more frequently in HAPE-susceptible individuals (p=0.02) [20]. The DD ACE genotype in a young population is associated with a blunting of stimulated endothelial NO and donated NO responses [22].

The EDN G(8002)A polymorphism showed higher AA genotype in HAPE-susceptible individuals (p=0.04) [20]. Haplotype analysis identified A-3A haplotype in EDN as risk-associated [20]. The eNOS Glu(298)Asp polymorphism showed higher TT genotype in HAPE-susceptible individuals (p=0.01) [20]. The authors conclude that mutations in ACE, EDN1, and eNOS genes, which impair pulmonary endothelial function and reduce nitric oxide bioavailability, are important genetic factors for HAPE susceptibility [20].

EGLN1 polymorphisms are associated with both high-altitude adaptation and increased risk of developing HAPE [23]. The TT genotype of rs479200 in EGLN1, which was more frequent in Kapha Ayurvedic types and correlated with higher expression of EGLN1, was associated with patients suffering from HAPE, whereas it was present at a significantly lower frequency in Pitta and nearly absent in natives of high altitude [23]. A 2024 review found that the genes EPAS1, EGLN1, eNOS, ACE, and TIMP3 are significantly associated with susceptibility to HAPE and play key roles in various biological processes [24].

### 1.6 Inflammation as a Secondary Phenomenon

The majority of current evidence supports that inflammation in the lungs modulates but does not cause the process of development of HAPE [5]. Early HAPE is characterized by high pulmonary artery pressures that lead to a protein-rich and mildly hemorrhagic edema, with normal levels of leukocytes, and inflammatory markers such as CRP, IL-6, and TNF-α rise only after edema onset [5][25]. The main study finding is that early HAPE is characterized by high PA pressures that lead to a protein-rich and mildly hemorrhagic edema, with normal levels of leukocytes, and inflammation is a secondary consequence rather than the initiating cause [25][26]. HAPE is a form of hydrostatic pulmonary edema with altered alveolar-capillary permeability, caused by high microvascular pressures, not inflammation [25]. However, a secondary inflammatory reaction may occur in some individuals after barrier disruption, and respiratory infections in children may lower the threshold for edema via HPV [6].

### 1.7 Unique Physiologic Considerations for Ski Mountaineers

Ski mountaineers face several unique physiologic challenges that potentiate HAPE risk. Exercise and cold exposure exacerbate pressure rises in HAPE-susceptible individuals [2]. HAPE-susceptible individuals have been shown to have exaggerated pulmonary vascular responses to hypoxia and exercise [2]. In ski mountaineers, the combination of intense physical exertion (skiing, climbing) and hypobaric hypoxia creates a particularly strong stimulus for pulmonary hypertension [2][27]. Maximal exercise in elite athletes causes alterations in the blood-gas barrier, with capillary pressures exceeding 30 mmHg, and stress failure of pulmonary capillaries is a limiting factor for maximal exercise [28][29].

Cold weather is a well-established risk factor for HAPE [1][2][30]. Cold exposure increases sympathetic tone and can exacerbate pulmonary vasoconstriction, contributing to the rise in pulmonary artery pressure [2]. In ski mountaineers, inhaled cold air may cause bronchoconstriction and further increase pulmonary vascular resistance, compounding the hypoxic vasoconstriction [2].

Ski mountaineers also engage in repeated ascents and descents over multiple days, creating a pattern of intermittent hypobaric hypoxia and reoxygenation. This may lead to repeated cycles of pulmonary vasoconstriction and relaxation, potentially exacerbating the inflammatory response and vascular remodeling [2]. The rate of ascent and altitude gained during sleep is more significant than that gained during the day [27]. Prevention emphasizes gradual ascent with sleeping altitude increase ≤300–350 m/day above 2,500 m, with extra rest days every 600–1,200 m [31].

## 2. Documentation of Comparable Cases

### 2.1 Landmark Ski Resort Case Series: Hultgren (1996)

The most directly relevant study for HAPE in skiers comes from Hultgren et al. (1996), who reviewed medical records of 150 patients with HAPE over a 39-month period at a Colorado Rocky Mountain ski resort at 2,928 m (9,600 ft) [2][32]. The mean age was 34.4 years, with 84% male (126 men, 24 women). Patients were predominantly skiers and visitors to the resort. The mean time to onset of symptoms was 3 ± 1.3 days after arrival at altitude. Common symptoms included dyspnea, cough, headache, chest congestion, nausea, fever, and weakness. Orthopnea, hemoptysis, and vomiting were rare. Cerebral edema symptoms occurred in 14%. Fever >100°F was noted in 20%. Systolic hypertension (≥150 mm Hg) occurred in 17%. Rales were present in 85%, and pulmonary infiltrates in 88% (mostly bilateral or right-sided). Mean oxygen saturation by pulse oximetry in 45 patients was 74% (range 38% to 93%). All patients received initial clinic oxygen, and treatment ranged from portable nasal oxygen to descent to Denver. No deaths or complications were reported. This study established that HAPE occurs in skiers at moderate altitudes (2,928 m) with a typical onset 3 days after arrival, and that the condition is readily treatable if recognized early [2][32].

### 2.2 Sierra Nevada Skiing Case (2019)

A case report published in *Archivos de Bronconeumología* described a 40-year-old white male athlete, normally resident at sea level, who ascended to Sierra Nevada (maximum altitude 3,300 m) in less than 3 hours and skied intensively for 48 hours [33][34]. He presented with sudden dyspnea, cough with pinkish expectoration, hypoperfusion, cyanosis, tachycardia, tachypnea, low-grade fever, and hypoxia (SatO₂ ~90% on FiO₂ 0.6). Chest X-ray showed bilateral reticular cotton-wool infiltrates and ground-glass opacities. CT-angiogram ruled out pulmonary embolism. Echocardiogram revealed pulmonary hypertension with estimated systolic pulmonary artery pressure of 70 mmHg. He was treated with oxygen therapy, low-dose furosemide, and supportive care, and symptoms resolved within 3 days. Follow-up at one year was normal. The article discusses HAPE pathophysiology, risk factors (rate of ascent, exercise, prior HAPE), and treatment (descent, oxygen, nifedipine, sildenafil, acetazolamide) [33][34].

### 2.3 Colorado Summit County Ski Area Study (1983)

A study of 29 cases of HAPE among visitors to Summit County, Colorado found that all patients were male, with a mean age of 37.8 years, and all resided below 3,000 ft (sea level or near-sea level residents) [35]. This study proposed two distinct types of HAPE: Type 1 (nonresident-ascent HAPE) affects visitors to altitudes above 8,000 ft and primarily affects adult men; Type 2 (resident-reascent HAPE) affects residents of high altitudes when they descend to an elevation below 8,000 ft and then return, affecting male and female residents almost equally and being a disease of childhood and adolescence. The study suggests that women and children from low altitudes may be resistant to HAPE up to 15,000 ft, but protection may be lost at higher altitudes or with pulmonary anomalies [35].

### 2.4 Aconcagua Case Studies

A 2025 study published in *Wilderness & Environmental Medicine* examined 17 climbers diagnosed with HAPE on Mount Aconcagua (6,962 m) compared to 42 non-HAPE climbers [36]. HAPE climbers spent fewer nights at the 14,000-foot camp (3.6 vs. 5.0 nights for non-HAPE climbers). Seventy-one percent of those who developed HAPE reported acute mountain sickness (AMS) at 14,000 feet and continued climbing despite unresolved symptoms. A higher proportion of HAPE climbers had taken acetazolamide or had recent respiratory infections. None died (all evacuated by helicopter). The study concluded that ascending slowly and heeding AMS symptoms are critical to preventing severe altitude illness, and that Aconcagua's non-technical nature encourages dangerous rapid ascents [36].

A separate case study from the UIAA described a 61-year-old fit male climber who summited Aconcagua [37]. During the night, he woke on hands and knees with shallow panting and inability to breathe. He experienced hallucinations, was unable to sit up unsupported, and had an oxygen saturation of 43% at the rescue hut at 5,500 m. The guide administered oxygen and drugs, and immediate descent from high camp to the rescue hut took 2–3 hours with assistance. He was evacuated by helicopter at first light. Recovery was rapid: 80% recovered quickly once at park gate altitude, 90% within one week, and 100% within one month. CT scan showed temporary lung damage that mostly healed within six months [37].

### 2.5 Swiss Alps Expedition Doctor Case

A 2017 case report described a 25-year-old expedition doctor climbing Monte Rosa (4,634 m) in the Swiss Alps who developed a mild lower respiratory tract infection (LRTI) producing yellow sputum two days before travel and took acetazolamide prophylactically [38][39]. On day 5 at 3,500 m, he experienced decreased exercise tolerance, and at 4,200 m, dyspnea at rest, cough, and tachycardia. He was clinically diagnosed with HAPE and treated with descent, nifedipine, salbutamol, levofloxacin (for the LRTI), and rehydration. He recovered fully over 14 days. Chest radiograph did not show pulmonary edema, consistent with evidence that imaging is unreliable for HAPE. This case highlights that lower respiratory tract infection may be a significant risk factor for HAPE, an often-overlooked risk factor despite impaired gas exchange from infection [38][39].

### 2.6 Pheriche Himalayan Rescue Association Case Series (2012)

A retrospective case series from the Pheriche Medical Aid Post (4,240 m) reviewed 56 patients clinically diagnosed with HAPE during the 2010 spring and fall seasons [40]. Twenty-three percent of patients were Nepalese, 73% were male, and 28% also had suspected high altitude cerebral edema (HACE). The median altitude of symptom onset was 4,834 m. Treatment included oxygen (83%), nifedipine (87%), sildenafil (44%), dexamethasone (32%), and acetazolamide (39%). Ninety-three percent of patients descended, and 38% descended on foot without supplemental oxygen. One death occurred in a 28-year-old Nepalese guide with possible underlying cardiac disease. No adverse effects from polypharmacy were observed [40].

### 2.7 Subedi et al. (2023) – Three Rare Presentations at Everest Region Clinic

A 2023 case series published in *Clinical Case Reports* described three rare presentations of HAPE at a high-altitude clinic in the Everest region (4,371 m) [41][42]. Case 1 was a 41-year-old male with a history of HAPE 13 years prior who ascended from Lukla (2,860 m) to Everest Base Camp (5,364 m) within 4 days and developed HAPE despite prior history. This case highlights that delayed onset HAPE can occur even in those with prior HAPE history. The series also demonstrated the value of point of care ultrasound (POCUS) as a diagnostic tool in remote high-altitude settings, showing multiple B-lines with absent A-lines in HAPE patients [41][42].

### 2.8 Multi-Center Retrospective Study in China (2012–2022)

A 2025 multi-center retrospective study of 411 adult inpatients with HAPE from four hospitals in Qinghai, China found that the prevalence of pulmonary hypertension was 55.2%, thromboembolic events occurred in 5.4%, HACE in 12.2%, and overall in-hospital mortality was 11.7% [43][44]. Risk factors for pulmonary hypertension included onset altitude ≥3,500 m (OR = 1.79) and elevated LDH (>250 IU/L). Thromboembolism risk was higher in patients with history of hypertension (OR = 6.71) and longer hospital stays. Mortality risk factors included headache (OR = 2.11), pulmonary hypertension (OR = 2.96), HACE, elevated BUN (OR = 5.26), and elevated NT-proBNP (OR = 2.63). The study emphasizes the importance of early identification and management of these risk factors to improve outcomes [43][44].

### 2.9 Highlander Porters and Native Highlanders

Several case reports document HAPE in native highlanders, challenging the assumption that lifelong altitude residence confers immunity. A 41-year-old male of Rai ethnicity, born and raised in the Himalayan region of Nepal, developed HAPE while ascending Mount Everest at approximately 5,000 m after descending to 3,420 m for two weeks [45][46]. This represents reentry-type HAPE in a highlander returning from lower altitude. He presented with progressive shortness of breath, orthopnea, and loss of consciousness (GCS 3/15). After treatment with high-concentration oxygen, intubation, mechanical ventilation for 4 days, ICU for 8 days, IV antibiotics, calcium channel blockers, and diuretics, he was discharged after 10 days with full recovery [45][46].

A separate case described a 24-year-old healthy Nepalese porter who ascended from Lukla (2,840 m) to Gorakshep (5,140 m) over 3 days (gain of 2,300 m) and presented with shortness of breath, cough with frothy sputum, and oxygen saturation of 40% [47]. He was treated with oxygen, nifedipine, acetazolamide, and ibuprofen, and advised rapid descent. He was medically clear within one week. The authors note that HAPE in porters is "pretty uncommon with only a few cases reported till date" [47].

### 2.10 Experienced Mountaineer with Possible Genetic Predisposition

A 2014 case report described a 25-year-old previously healthy Caucasian male experienced mountaineer with extensive experience hiking above 2,500 m who made a rapid ascent from sea level to 3,200 m [48]. He developed nausea, headache, cough, progressive dyspnea, and required air evacuation. In the emergency department, his oxygen saturation was 80% on room air with coarse breath sounds. The patient's father had a history of aborting a high-altitude hike due to sudden "asthma," suggesting possible genetic predisposition. He was treated with 100% oxygen, nebulized albuterol/ipratropium, IV dexamethasone, IV hydralazine, and IV furosemide, and was discharged the next day. The authors note that evidence suggests genetics may play a role in HAPE susceptibility through pathways involving renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system, nitric oxide pathway, hypoxia-inducible factors, mitochondrial DNA, and heat shock protein polymorphisms [48].

## 3. Acute and Long-Term Treatments with Best Outcomes

### 3.1 Definitive Therapy: Immediate Descent and Supplemental Oxygen

The Wilderness Medical Society (WMS) 2024 Clinical Practice Guidelines state that "descent is indicated for individuals with HAPE (Recommendation Grade: 1A)" [49][50]. Descent remains the single best treatment for HAPE, and the definitive treatment for all severe altitude illnesses is immediate descent until symptoms resolve [49][51]. For HAPE specifically, the treatment is immediate descent of at least 1,000 m (3,280 ft) or until symptoms resolve [51]. The Joint Trauma System Clinical Practice Guideline (March 2024) states: "The most appropriate treatment in all altitude illnesses is to descend" and "HAPE leads to high mortality and morbidity; thus, descent to lowest possible altitude in the most expeditious manner should be of the highest priority until symptoms fully resolve" [52]. The CDC Yellow Book 2026 notes: "Descent is urgent and mandatory for HAPE" [53].

Supplemental oxygen should be administered with a target SpO₂ >90% [49][50]. The Center for Wilderness Safety recommends high flow oxygen at 2 to 4 liters per minute with a goal oxygen saturation of >90% to decrease pulmonary artery pressures and improve the patient's tachypnea over 24 to 48 hours [54]. The objective of treatment is to reduce pulmonary artery pressure by descent, administering oxygen, or giving drugs that relax pulmonary vasoconstriction [17].

### 3.2 Pharmacologic Treatment: Nifedipine

Nifedipine is identified as the preferred pharmacologic agent for HAPE treatment when descent and oxygen are unavailable [49][55]. The WMS 2019/2024 guidelines recommend nifedipine 30 mg extended-release every 12 hours for HAPE treatment [49][50]. The landmark nifedipine prophylaxis trial by Bärtsch et al. (1991) showed a reduction in HAPE incidence from 64% to 10% with nifedipine prophylaxis in susceptible individuals [55]. Nifedipine is the most studied agent for HAPE prophylaxis, typically dosed at 30 mg every 12 hours [1]. The Medscape summary of the 2024 WMS guidelines states: "Nifedipine is the preferred agent when oxygen and descent are unavailable" [55].

### 3.3 Phosphodiesterase-5 Inhibitors (Sildenafil, Tadalafil)

The WMS 2024 guidelines list tadalafil and sildenafil as alternatives to nifedipine for HAPE treatment [49][55]. For HAPE prevention in susceptible individuals, tadalafil 10 mg every 12 hours is recommended as a second-line option after nifedipine [49][51]. The WMS 2024 update notes that tadalafil (10 mg every 12 hours orally) is a second-line preventive medication (strong recommendation, low quality of evidence), and dexamethasone is considered third-line treatment (strong recommendation, low quality of evidence) [56]. The Maggiorini et al. trial demonstrated that tadalafil and dexamethasone reduce HAPE incidence in susceptible subjects [55]. A randomized, placebo-controlled trial demonstrated that both tadalafil (10 mg twice daily) and dexamethasone (16 mg twice daily) are effective in preventing HAPE in susceptible individuals [31].

A systematic review of phosphodiesterase-5 inhibitors and dexamethasone for HAPE concluded that "according to the current available literature, neither phosphodiesterase-5 inhibitors or dexamethasone significantly alter the outcome of individuals affected by HAPE" and that the gold standard treatment remains rapid descent [57].

### 3.4 Dexamethasone

The WMS 2024 guidelines state: "Dexamethasone is not routinely recommended for isolated HAPE, in accordance with the understanding that early disease is driven by hydrostatic rather than inflammatory mechanisms" [49]. However, dexamethasone "should be considered when neurologic dysfunction persists despite supplemental oxygen, suggesting concurrent HACE or hypoxic encephalopathy" [49]. For HAPE prevention in susceptible individuals, dexamethasone 8 mg every 12 hours can be used as a third-line option if nifedipine and tadalafil are not suitable [49][51].

A narrative review of dexamethasone for prevention of altitude illnesses (published in *Military Medical Research*, 2025) examined 13 randomized placebo-controlled trials at altitudes 3,500–5,343 m with dexamethasone doses of 2–16 mg/day [58]. The review found that dexamethasone significantly reduced AMS incidence in 6 of 11 studies at altitudes >4,000 m (large effect sizes), but was less effective at lower altitudes (3,500–3,650 m). In HAPE-susceptible individuals, dexamethasone (2×8 mg/day) prevented HAPE (0% vs. 78% in placebo) and reduced AMS. The review concludes that dexamethasone is most effective at altitudes above 4,000 m at doses of 8–16 mg/day, with no serious adverse effects reported in short-term use (<1 week) [58].

### 3.5 Inhaled Beta-Agonists (Salmeterol)

A landmark double-blind, randomized trial published in the *New England Journal of Medicine* (2002) by Sartori et al. found that inhaled salmeterol significantly reduces the incidence of HAPE [14][59]. Among 37 HAPE-susceptible mountaineers exposed to 4,559 m in under 22 hours, 74% in the placebo group developed HAPE vs. 33% in the salmeterol group (P=0.02, NNT=2.5). The dosing was salmeterol 125 μg inhaled every 12 hours, starting the day before ascent from 1,130 m to 4,559 m over 22 hours. Salmeterol also improved Lake Louise Acute Mountain Sickness scores, arterial oxygen saturations, and chest radiographs [14][59]. The study also measured nasal transepithelial potential difference, a marker of sodium and water transport; susceptible subjects had over 30% lower values at low altitude, suggesting defective sodium-driven alveolar fluid clearance contributes to HAPE [14].

The SWIFTARC Trial (NCT06040268) is a Phase 1/2 clinical study currently investigating the use of Advair HFA (salmeterol/fluticasone 126/270 mcg twice daily) to prevent HAPE and improve exercise performance at altitude [60]. The trial is based on research showing that prophylactic inhalation of salmeterol decreased the incidence of HAPE in susceptible subjects by more than 50% [60].

### 3.6 Continuous Positive Airway Pressure (CPAP)

The HAPER CAPER trial (2025, published in *Academic Emergency Medicine*) was a prospective, multicenter, double-blind, sham-controlled randomized controlled trial evaluating CPAP (10 cm H₂O, FiO₂ 90%) plus high-flow oxygen versus sham CPAP with high-flow oxygen alone for HAPE [61][62]. Sixty-four dyspneic adults with SpO₂ <85%, recent high-altitude arrival, and noncardiogenic pulmonary edema on chest radiograph were randomized. The primary endpoint was clinical resolution of HAPE (able to ambulate on 2 L/min nasal O₂ with SpO₂ ≥92%). Results showed that both groups demonstrated similar rapid recovery; mean time to resolution was 158 ± 62 min for CPAP vs. 178 ± 86 min for oxygen alone (p = 0.297). No significant differences in vital signs, SpO₂ improvement, or adverse events (no intubations or deaths) were observed. The study was underpowered (target 264, achieved 58) due to COVID-19 and equipment expiration. The authors concluded: "Both CPAP with high-flow oxygen and high-flow oxygen alone are highly effective for the treatment of HAPE. CPAP conferred no improvement in the time to resolution of HAPE. High-flow oxygen therapy requires significantly less resource utilization for similar benefits in the treatment of HAPE" [61][62].

The WMS 2019 guidelines state that CPAP may be considered as an adjunctive treatment for HAPE [50]. The 2024 WMS guidelines note that CPAP was included as a newer search term in the updated evidence review [49].

### 3.7 Portable Hyperbaric Chambers (Gamow Bags)

The WMS 2024 guidelines recommend portable hyperbaric chambers as an alternative when descent is not possible [49][51]. The Gamow bag is a portable, soft-sided pressure chamber that simulates a descent of 3,000–9,000 feet at 2 PSI, used as a temporizing measure for HAPE when rapid descent is impossible [53]. The CDC Yellow Book states: "a pressurization bag (e.g., the Gamow bag) can mimic a descent of approximately 1,500–1,800 m (5,000–6,000 ft)" [53]. The Joint Trauma System CPG (March 2024) states that a portable hyperbaric chamber can decrease the altitude by approximately 2,400 m (7,874 ft) and is used intermittently in 1–2 hour sessions [52]. The UIAA Medical Commission consensus statement notes that chambers simulate a descent of 1,500–2,500 m by increasing oxygen pressure and are emergency tools to buy time for descent, not for mild AMS or prevention [63].

### 3.8 Acetazolamide for HAPE (Not Recommended for Treatment)

The WMS 2024 guidelines explicitly state that acetazolamide is not recommended for HAPE treatment [49][55]. The Medscape summary states: "Acetazolamide is not recommended for HAPE specifically but may be used for concurrent acute mountain sickness or high-altitude cerebral edema" [55]. The AAFP summary states: "Acetazolamide is not recommended for HAPE treatment" and "diuretics are not recommended for HAPE treatment" [64].

The Berger et al. (2022) study, a randomized, placebo-controlled, double-blind study investigating whether acetazolamide (250 mg three times daily, started 48 hours before ascent) prevents HAPE in 13 nonacclimatized lowlanders with a history of HAPE who ascended rapidly (<22 hours) from 1,130 m to 4,559 m, found that HAPE incidence was 43% with acetazolamide vs. 67% with placebo (P=0.39), a 35% relative reduction that was not statistically significant [65][66]. The authors concluded: "Limited by small sample size, our data do not support recommending acetazolamide for the prevention of HAPE in mountaineers ascending rapidly to over 4,500 m" [66].

### 3.9 Recurrence Rates and Functional Recovery

Recurrence rates for HAPE are substantial. The Cleveland Clinic states: "If you have a history of HAPE, the likelihood of it happening again is high. With the same rate of ascent, about 60% of people experience HAPE again" [67]. The StatPearls article (updated June 2025) states: "A prior history of high-altitude pulmonary edema significantly increases the risk of recurrence, with rates reaching up to 60% in some studies" [68]. The High Altitude Health blog (March 2024) provides a more nuanced view, noting that the previously estimated recurrence rate of 60–80% may be exaggerated, with actual rates around 21–42% based on more recent studies [69].

With prompt treatment, the prognosis is excellent. The Medscape summary states: "Treatment often results in rapid clearing of pulmonary edema without long-term complications. With prompt treatment, HAPE carries an excellent prognosis" [55]. Recovery can take up to two weeks [67]. The StatPearls article confirms: "Prognosis is excellent with early recognition and treatment" [68].

### 3.10 Return-to-Climbing Criteria After a HAPE Episode

The Return to Activity at Altitude After High-Altitude Illness article provides guidance: "Individuals who develop HAPE may consider further ascent to higher altitude or reascent only when symptoms of HAPE have completely resolved" [70][71]. The article emphasizes that for athletes with a remote history of HAI, careful acclimatization and prophylactic medications can reduce recurrence. For recent HAI, safety depends on severity; mild AMS may allow continued activity with rest, while moderate/severe cases require descent and aggressive treatment [70][71]. The article states: "Most persons can acclimatize up to approximately 5,500 m if given sufficient time, and they deacclimatize in the same time frame upon returning to low altitude" [70].

The WikEM page on HAPE states: "Re-ascend in 2–3 days after mild-moderate symptoms resolve" and for discharge criteria: "Discharge with PaO₂ ≥60 mmHg or SpO₂ >90%" [72]. The High Altitude Health blog concludes that with proper monitoring and early intervention (using pulse oximeters at home, target oxygen saturation ≥90%, and having supplemental oxygen available), many patients can safely return to altitude without recurrence [69].

### 3.11 Long-Term Management: Chronic Pharmacologic Prophylaxis

For individuals with a prior history of HAPE, the WMS recommends pharmacologic prophylaxis. The WMS 2024 guidelines state: "Pharmacologic prophylaxis should be reserved for individuals with a history of HAPE, particularly those with recurrent episodes. Nifedipine is the preferred prophylactic agent" [49]. The specific prophylactic regimens recommended by the WMS include: first-line nifedipine ER 30 mg every 12 hours (Recommendation Grade: 1B); second-line tadalafil 10 mg every 12 hours; third-line dexamethasone 8 mg every 12 hours [49][50][51]. The dosing guidance from the 2024 WMS summary recommends starting medication the day before ascent and continuing for 4–7 days [51][73].

A proof-of-concept randomized controlled trial (2025) in 120 male military personnel (lowlanders) with a history of one mild-to-moderate HAPE episode randomized participants into four groups: acetazolamide 250 mg BD, nifedipine 30 mg SR BD, tadalafil 10 mg BD, or placebo [74]. Medication started one day before ascent to high altitude and continued for 3 days. Results showed that no HAPE occurred in any drug group upon reascent, while 4 patients in the placebo group developed HAPE. SpO₂ and pulmonary artery pressure were significantly better in drug groups versus placebo. The authors conclude that acetazolamide, nifedipine, or tadalafil are effective alternatives for HAPE prophylaxis in susceptible individuals, especially in military exigencies requiring rapid reinduction [74].

## 4. Acclimatization and Prevention Protocols

### 4.1 Gradual Ascent Protocols

The WMS 2024 guidelines universally recommend gradual ascent as the primary prevention strategy for all acute altitude illnesses. The specific recommendation is: "Above an altitude of 3,000 m, individuals should not increase the sleeping elevation by more than 500 m per day and should include a rest day (i.e., no ascent to higher sleeping elevation) every 3 to 4 days" [50][75]. This is a strong recommendation, moderate-quality evidence [75]. The CDC Yellow Book states: "The Wilderness Medical Society recommends avoiding ascent to a sleeping altitude of 2,750 m (≥9,000 ft) in a single day; ascending at a rate of no greater than 500 m (1,650 ft) per night in sleeping altitude once above 3,000 m (9,800 ft)" [53]. The UIAA Medical Commission recommends ascending 300–500 m per day above 2,500–3,000 m, with rest days [76]. The "climb high and sleep low" maxim is fundamental to acclimatization [77].

The most effective method of prevention is slow ascent, 300–350 m/day [2]. Prevention emphasizes gradual ascent with sleeping altitude increase ≤300–350 m/day above 2,500 m, with extra rest days every 600–1,200 m [31]. The rate of ascent and altitude gained during sleep is more significant than that gained during the day [27]. HAPE is preventable for most individuals with a slow rate of ascent and adequate time for acclimatization [1].

### 4.2 Pre-Acclimatization Using Hypoxic Tents or Intermittent Hypoxic Exposure

The WMS 2024 guidelines state that hypoxic tents are not recommended for pre-acclimatization due to insufficient or contradictory evidence [49][75]. However, the guidelines note that "staged ascent and preacclimatization may be considered, but hypoxic tents are not recommended" [75].

A study published in the *Journal of Travel Medicine* (2024) reviewed controlled trials on pre-acclimatization using simulated altitude (normobaric or hypobaric hypoxia) to prevent AMS [78]. Seven controlled studies with cumulative pre-acclimatization times of 7–104 hours at simulated altitudes of 2,200–5,000 m were analyzed. Results show a linear relationship: spending approximately 75 hours in hypoxia reduces the risk of AMS by roughly 50%, and this risk could theoretically be eliminated after accumulation of approximately 200 hours. The duration of hypoxic exposure during pre-acclimatization accounted for 76% of the variance in reducing AMS risk after subsequent hypoxic or altitude exposure. The study concludes that approximately 200 hours of progressive hypoxia at simulated altitudes (e.g., 2,200–5,000 m) can almost eliminate AMS risk for subsequent ascents up to about 6,000 m, especially when combined with gradual ascent and optional acetazolamide use [78].

A study published in *Frontiers in Physiology* (2024) evaluated hypoxic preacclimatization combining intermittent hypoxia exposure (IHE) with physical exercise [79]. One hundred twenty healthy Han Chinese men (18–24 years) were assigned to four groups. Key findings included that SpO₂ was significantly higher in the 5-day rest and 5-day exercise groups vs. controls (p=0.001 and p=0.006). Heart rate was significantly lower in the 5-day rest group vs. controls (p=0.018). AMS incidence did not differ significantly, but moderate/severe headache was less frequent in the 3-day exercise group (p=0.002). VO₂max was highest in the 5-day exercise group (p<0.05 vs. all others). The authors conclude that combining IHE with physical exercise—especially longer duration (≥3 days, ≥3h/session, total ≥10h) and moderate intensity—effectively promotes acute hypoxia tolerance [79].

The Uphill Athlete (2024) provides a nuanced perspective on hypoxic conditioning. Previously skeptical, the organization now recognizes that properly managed hypoxic conditioning can be beneficial. The critical factors are individualized dosing, daily monitoring (SpO₂, heart rate, recovery), and integration with training plans. Key adaptations include faster ventilatory and neural changes (~7–10 days) and slower red blood cell increases (weeks). The effects persist about 1–2 weeks after stopping. The article emphasizes that aerobic fitness is the single most important factor in high-altitude performance, and for athletes who must choose between training quality or hypoxic conditioning, training quality wins [80].

### 4.3 Pharmacologic Prophylaxis

**Acetazolamide for AMS/HACE Prevention:** The WMS 2024 guidelines recommend acetazolamide 125 mg every 12 hours for adults at moderate to high risk of AMS/HACE (strong recommendation, high-quality evidence) [50][75][81]. The pediatric dose is 1.25 mg·kg⁻¹ every 12 hours (max 125 mg per dose) [75]. Acetazolamide should be started the day before ascent and continued 2–4 days at peak altitude [51][73][82]. The WADA TUE Physician Guideline (January 2026) states that acetazolamide is the only medication documented to reduce the risk of developing AMS, and for prevention, the recommended adult dose is 125 mg twice daily, starting 1–2 days before ascent and continuing for 4–7 days [82]. The AAFP summary states: "Acetazolamide and dexamethasone can be used to prevent acute mountain sickness and high altitude cerebral edema, but only acetazolamide aids in acclimatization" [64].

**Nifedipine for HAPE Prevention:** The WMS 2024 guidelines recommend nifedipine ER 30 mg every 12 hours for HAPE prevention in susceptible individuals (Recommendation Grade: 1B) [49][50]. The landmark trial showed a reduction in HAPE incidence from 64% to 10% [55]. The WMS 2024 update confirmed that nifedipine or tadalafil remain preferred for HAPE prophylaxis, as acetazolamide showed only 43% HAPE incidence reduction vs. 67% with placebo [73].

**Tadalafil for HAPE Prevention:** Dosing is tadalafil 10 mg every 12 hours [49][51]. The Maggiorini et al. trial showed tadalafil and dexamethasone reduce HAPE incidence in susceptible subjects [55]. The WMS 2024 guidelines list tadalafil as second-line for HAPE prevention (strong recommendation, low quality of evidence) [56].

**Dexamethasone for HAPE Prevention:** Dosing is dexamethasone 8 mg every 12 hours [49][51]. The WMS 2024 guidelines list dexamethasone as third-line for HAPE prevention (strong recommendation, low quality of evidence) [56].

**Salmeterol for HAPE Prevention:** The Sartori et al. (2002) study showed salmeterol 125 μg inhaled every 12 hours reduced HAPE incidence from 74% to 33% (P=0.02) [14][59]. The authors recommend that "salmeterol or nifedipine be added for individuals at high risk of HAPE" [14].

**Acetazolamide for HAPE Prevention (Limited Role):** The Berger et al. (2022) study showed a non-significant 35% reduction in HAPE incidence with acetazolamide 250 mg three times daily (43% vs. 67%, P=0.39), which is considerably less than the 70–100% reductions seen with nifedipine, tadalafil, and dexamethasone [65][66]. The WMS 2024 update explicitly states: "Acetazolamide has been shown to prevent high altitude pulmonary edema in known susceptible individuals. False" [73]. However, the proof-of-concept trial (2025) in 120 male military personnel showed that acetazolamide 250 mg BD, nifedipine 30 mg SR BD, and tadalafil 10 mg BD were all equally effective in preventing HAPE upon reascent, with no HAPE occurring in any drug group vs. 4 cases in the placebo group [74].

### 4.4 Risk Categories for Prophylaxis

The WMS 2024 guidelines define risk categories based on ascent profile, history of altitude illness, and initial sleeping altitude [75][81]:

- **Low risk:** Individuals with no prior history of altitude illness, ascending at a gradual rate (<500 m/day above 3,000 m)
- **Moderate risk:** Individuals with prior history of AMS, or ascending at a rate ≥500 m/day above 3,000 m, or sleeping elevation ≥2,800 m on day 1
- **High risk:** Individuals with prior history of HACE or HAPE, or ascending at a very rapid rate (>500 m/day above 3,000 m), or pre-existing medical conditions that increase risk

The 2024 WMS update includes a new risk assessment method for planned ascents [73].

### 4.5 Training Modifications and Exercise Intensity Management

The WMS 2024 guidelines address exercise considerations. The Luks presentation (July 2024) discussed the Beidleman 2023 field study, which found no difference in AMS incidence/severity between active and sedentary groups, but noted limitations and concluded no change to current recommendations [73]. For ski mountaineers specifically, the combination of intense physical exertion (skiing, climbing) and hypobaric hypoxia creates a particularly strong stimulus for pulmonary hypertension [2][27]. Exercise limitation at high altitude was related to an exercise-induced worsening of hypoxemia, high ventilation equivalents, reduced external work efficiency, and a lower peak heart rate than predicted for age [83].

The Uphill Athlete emphasizes that aerobic fitness is the single most important factor in high-altitude performance, and for athletes who must choose between training quality or hypoxic conditioning, training quality wins [80]. The Madison Mountaineering guide emphasizes: "Be conservative in going higher. Climbers get into trouble when they push too hard too fast" [84].

### 4.6 Novel Approaches Validated in the 2020–2026 Period

Several novel approaches have been validated in the 2020–2026 period. The Wang et al. (2025) systematic review and Bayesian network meta-analysis found that moderate-dose acetazolamide (125–250 mg BID) effectively prevents AMS with a longer prophylactic window compared to high-dose regimens (375 mg BID) [85]. The proof-of-concept trial (2025) demonstrated that reinduction to high altitude after a mild-to-moderate HAPE episode can be safely accomplished with any of the three prophylactic drugs (acetazolamide, nifedipine, or tadalafil) if exigency exists [74]. The predictive model study from Tibet (2023) developed a nomogram with six predictors (age, cough, mode of transportation to high altitude, SpO₂, coughing sputum, and fatigue) to estimate HAPE risk, with an AUC of 0.934 for the model including SpO₂, designed as a simple, self-administered tool for individuals at high altitude to assess their HAPE risk without medical equipment [86].

The HAPER CAPER trial (2025) provided high-quality evidence that CPAP with high-flow oxygen and high-flow oxygen alone are both highly effective for HAPE treatment, with no significant difference between them, and that high-flow oxygen therapy requires significantly less resource utilization [61][62]. The SWIFTARC Trial (ongoing) is investigating the use of Advair HFA (salmeterol/fluticasone) to prevent HAPE and improve exercise performance at altitude, building on the Sartori et al. (2002) findings [60].

## 5. Conclusion

High-altitude pulmonary edema in ski mountaineers at altitudes ≥3,000 m represents a complex interplay of exaggerated hypoxic pulmonary vasoconstriction, endothelial dysfunction (NO/ET-1 imbalance), impaired alveolar fluid clearance, pulmonary capillary stress failure, and genetic predisposition. The unique physiologic challenges of ski mountaineering—intense physical exertion, cold air exposure, and repeated ascents and descents—create a particularly potent stimulus for HAPE development.

The evidence from case reports and case series spanning from the landmark Hultgren ski resort series (1996) to the most recent studies in 2025–2026 demonstrates that HAPE affects skiers and mountaineers across a wide range of altitudes, from 2,900 m to over 6,900 m, with typical onset 2–5 days after arrival. Key risk factors include rapid ascent, prior HAPE history, male sex, cold exposure, and preceding respiratory infections.

Acute treatment priorities are clear: immediate descent (≥1,000 m) and supplemental oxygen (target SpO₂ >90%) remain the definitive therapies. Pharmacologic adjuncts include nifedipine (first-line), PDE-5 inhibitors (tadalafil, sildenafil), and dexamethasone (for concurrent HACE). The HAPER CAPER trial (2025) provides high-quality evidence that high-flow oxygen alone is as effective as CPAP plus oxygen, with significantly less resource utilization. Portable hyperbaric chambers serve as temporizing measures when descent is impossible.

Long-term management for individuals with prior HAPE requires pharmacologic prophylaxis: nifedipine ER 30 mg every 12 hours (first-line), tadalafil 10 mg every 12 hours (second-line), or dexamethasone 8 mg every 12 hours (third-line). Recurrence rates range from 21% to 60%, but with proper monitoring and preventive measures, many patients can safely return to altitude. Return-to-climbing criteria require complete resolution of symptoms before reascent, with gradual ascent and prophylactic medications.

Prevention remains the cornerstone of HAPE management. Gradual ascent protocols (≤500 m/day increase in sleeping elevation above 3,000 m, with rest days every 3–4 days) are universally recommended. Pre-acclimatization using hypoxic tents or intermittent hypoxic exposure may provide benefit, but the evidence is mixed, and the WMS does not currently recommend hypoxic tents. Pharmacologic prophylaxis for high-risk individuals (those with prior HAPE) is strongly recommended. The 2020–2026 period has contributed important new evidence, including the clarification that acetazolamide should not be used for HAPE prophylaxis (though it remains effective for AMS prevention), the validation of the predictive nomogram for HAPE risk assessment, and the ongoing investigation of salmeterol/fluticasone combinations for HAPE prevention.

Ultimately, for ski mountaineers, the key to preventing HAPE lies in respecting the altitude, ascending slowly, heeding early symptoms, and maintaining a low threshold for descent. With proper education, preparation, and adherence to established guidelines, the risk of HAPE can be substantially reduced, and when it does occur, outcomes are excellent with prompt recognition and treatment.
