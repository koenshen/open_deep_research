# Comprehensive Evidence-Based Analysis: Do Standardized Tests Improve K-12 Education? A Comparative Study of China, Japan, and the United States

## Introduction

Standardized testing remains one of the most contentious issues in global education policy. This report provides a comprehensive, evidence-based analysis of whether standardized tests improve K-12 education, focusing on China, Japan, and the United States. Drawing on research from 2021–2026, the analysis is structured as 6 pro arguments and 6 con arguments, each with clear thesis statements, multiple supporting points substantiated with credible evidence, and comparative insights across the three countries.

The three nations represent distinct educational paradigms: China's Gaokao-driven centralized system, Japan's Common Test (Daigaku Nyūgaku Kyōtsū Tesuto) system with its renowned "examination hell" (shiken jigoku) culture, and the United States' decentralized approach with SAT/ACT and state-level assessments. Understanding how these systems operate—and the evidence for and against their testing regimes—is essential for informed policy decisions.

# Part I: Pro Arguments for Standardized Testing

## PRO Argument 1: Academic Accountability and Measuring Student Learning Outcomes

### Thesis Statement
Standardized testing provides the only common, objective measure of grade-level academic standards for all public school students, creating essential accountability that drives improved educational outcomes across diverse educational systems.

### Supporting Points

**1. Standardized tests are the only common measure of academic standards across all students.**

According to the Center for American Progress report "Future of Testing in Education," "The yearly state standardized assessment alone cannot ensure a high-quality education for every child. But without it, educators do not know their progress toward meeting that goal. Despite the valid concerns that remain about standardized assessments and their role in education, there are a number of upsides and value to standardized testing. For instance, it is the only common measure of grade-level academic standards for all public school students." [1]

The report further emphasizes that "assessments are a way for stakeholders in education to understand what students know and can do. When assessments are well designed and a component of a system of teaching and learning that includes high-quality instruction and materials, they are part of the solution and not a source of the problem." [1]

**2. State tests provide consistent, comparable data for policymakers.**

Victoria McDougald, writing for the Fordham Institute (August 2024), argues that "state tests provide consistent, comparable data for policymakers to identify systemic inequities and hold schools accountable, citing gains under No Child Left Behind." She notes that "U.S. Secretary of Education Miguel Cardona once said that tests can be a 'flashlight' on what works in education." [2]

McDougald further states: "As my colleague Chester Finn wrote recently, 'we have solid evidence over thirty years in America and beyond that students learn more when they—and their schools—are held to account for what and how well they're learning.'" [2]

**3. Standardized testing helps counteract grade inflation.**

McDougald also notes that "test scores help counteract grade inflation, which has risen while SAT/ACT and NAEP scores have stagnated or declined." [2] According to Britannica's standardized testing overview, "Between 2000 and 2005, US math rankings fell from 18th to 40th and reading from 15th to 24th globally." [3]

**4. Frequent testing is positively associated with performance.**

A 2026 study published in *Studies in Educational Evaluation* (Volume 89, June 2026) investigated "the dual role of standardized testing as an informational tool (test frequency) and a coercive tool (test-based accountability) in global education governance." Using data from 65 PISA 2022 economies and three-level hierarchical linear modeling, the authors found that "frequent testing was positively associated with performance, particularly benefiting low-SES students; conversely, high-stakes accountability was linked to lower achievement, especially among high-SES students. These patterns held across high- and low-SES economies, indicating that policy design matters beyond national wealth." [4]

**5. The global K-12 testing and assessment market is growing.**

The global K-12 testing and assessment market size was valued at USD 13.6 billion in 2025 and is projected to grow from USD 14.67 billion in 2026 to USD 26.83 billion by 2034 at a CAGR of 7.84%. [5] The market is driven by "educational accountability (e.g., NAEP, NAPLAN, PISA) and the increasing use of AI and automation." [5] Grand View Research similarly reports that the global K-12 testing and assessment market size was estimated at USD 13.85 billion in 2024 and is projected to reach USD 32.04 billion by 2033. [6]

**6. Standardized testing is being redesigned, not removed.**

According to a January 2026 analysis by Martha Scharping of Simba Information, "Standardized testing is being redesigned, not removed. Post-pandemic, states and institutions reduced test reliance, but as systems stabilize, policymakers seek consistent progress measurement. The overall trend is a selective, fragmented approach that balances flexibility with comparability." [7]

**7. Testing data provides objective information for parents and students.**

McDougald argues that "tests provide an essential source of information for students and parents about student learning, alongside grades and teacher feedback." [2] This is particularly important because "only about a third of students are proficient in reading and math per NAEP," yet parents often overestimate their children's progress. [2]

**8. Standardized tests provide actionable data for teachers.**

The Center for American Progress report notes that through-year assessments "will return data throughout the year that teachers can use to shape instruction while kids are still in the classroom, rather than getting scores after the school year is over." [1] An ETS Global article notes that "standardized tests provide data that helps teachers identify where students are thriving and where they need more support." [8]

### Comparative Insights

**China:** The Gaokao serves as the ultimate accountability mechanism. According to the book *The Highest Exam: How the Gaokao Shapes China* by economists Ruixue Jia and Hongbin Li, "Gaokao, as the book summarizes on p.7, is the 'final match' of 'a centralized, hierarchical tournament' that shapes every aspect of social and economic life of a young person and even his/her sons and daughters well into the future." [9] The authors note that "the exam system persisted for over a millennium, but after years of mounting criticism, the Qing government abolished it. Shortly thereafter, the Qing dynasty itself fell in 1911." [9] The Gaokao "both 'influences and mirrors' Chinese society (p. 187)." [9]

**Japan:** Japan's Common Test (Daigaku Nyūgaku Kyōtsū Tesuto) was introduced in January 2021 to replace the National Center Test. It was "part of a broader educational reform emphasizing critical thinking, data interpretation, and logical expression over rote memorization." [10] Key changes "include longer, more complex texts in Japanese, real-world scenarios in math, and evidence-based analysis in science/social studies." [10] Nearly 500,000 students took the unified university entrance exams in January 2026, an increase of roughly 1,000 from the previous year, marking the second consecutive year of increase. [11]

**United States:** The U.S. system is being redesigned through innovations like matrix sampling, through-year assessments, and performance-based assessments. The Center for American Progress report identifies three innovative approaches: "matrix sampling—which provides individual students with a representative sample of assessment questions—reduces testing time; through-year tests have been piloted in some states involved in the assessment pilot created by ESSA; [and] performance-based assessments use hands-on tasks where students can demonstrate their knowledge and ability." [1] In 2026, state legislative efforts show "a clear divide. Some bills would reduce or fragment the summative assessment systems states rely on to understand student learning and progress, while other bills use the same assessment results to expand access to advanced coursework and target resources to struggling students." [12]

## PRO Argument 2: College Admissions and Meritocratic Selection

### Thesis Statement
Standardized college entrance exams (Gaokao in China, Common Test in Japan, SAT/ACT in the US) provide a meritocratic, objective basis for university admissions that, despite their imperfections, remain the most transparent and equitable mechanism for selecting students from diverse backgrounds.

### Supporting Points

**1. The Gaokao is the central meritocratic channel for social advancement in China.**

According to *The Highest Exam: How the Gaokao Shapes China* by Ruixue Jia and Hongbin Li, the Gaokao is "the central meritocratic channel for social advancement. Scores alone determine university admission, echoing the imperial keju exam system." [9] The Communist Party "gained legitimacy by reinstating the gaokao in 1952 after the fall of the Qing, and its suspension during the Cultural Revolution (eventually restored in 1977) triggered political turmoil." [9]

The Gaokao is "designed as a merit-based system that provides students from various socio-economic backgrounds with the chance to enter prestigious universities based solely on their academic achievements." [13] About 13.35 million students registered for the 2025 Gaokao, and approximately 12.9 million students took the exam in 2026. [14]

**2. The Gaokao provides a meritocratic alternative to wealth-based admissions.**

A review of *The Highest Exam* notes: "In the US, socioeconomic status is highly correlated with college admissions, and the gap in college attendance between rich and poor students is large. But the Chinese system is even more unequal because the gaokao is the only criterion, and the opportunities to prepare for it are so unevenly distributed across regions and income levels." [9] Despite this, the Gaokao is seen as "a pillar of educational equity and social stability." [15]

**3. The SAT/ACT are being reinstated as valuable predictors of college success.**

According to Britannica's standardized testing overview, "By 2024, some elite universities (e.g., Harvard, Yale, Dartmouth) reinstated testing requirements, citing research that test scores can help identify high-potential students from under-resourced backgrounds." [3] As of 2026, "~90% of US colleges no longer require SAT/ACT after pandemic-era test-optional policies, but some elite schools (Yale, MIT, Dartmouth) have reinstated testing, and over 1,000 UC faculty urge restoring a math requirement due to grade inflation and skill gaps." [16]

McDougald argues that "college admissions tests (SAT, ACT) are valid predictors of college success when combined with other measures, and are thoroughly vetted for bias." [2] She further notes that "test scores correlate with later-life outcomes like educational attainment and earnings." [2]

**4. In an era of AI, standardized tests are more valuable as a cheat-proof signal.**

According to an Asia Times opinion piece (June 2026), "The UC professors drew the obvious conclusion: in an era of AI-assisted essays and inflated transcripts, a standardized score is the closest thing colleges have to a signal that a machine cannot easily fake. The irony is sharp: the very subjectivity that made holistic admissions feel fairer is now its greatest weakness. A tightly proctored, synchronized exam is structurally more resistant to AI-assisted fraud than any take-home application file." [16]

**5. The Gaokao serves as a model for other societal institutions.**

The authors of *The Highest Exam* "call the underlying structure of the gaokao a 'centralized hierarchical tournament': the competition between students is centralized because it is the one standard of success, and it is hierarchical because success is defined in relative terms, by outcompeting peers." [9] The Gaokao serves as "a model for other societal institutions, such as local governments competing on GDP growth." [9]

**6. SAT participation and scores show post-COVID recovery trends.**

According to Brighterly's standardized testing statistics for 2025-2026, "SAT participation rose from 1.5 million in 2021 to over 2 million in 2025, but math mean scores declined from 528 to 508, with only 41% meeting math readiness benchmarks in 2025 (down from 49% in 2021)." [17] "ACT participation remained stable at ~1.3 million, but composite scores dropped from 19.8 in 2022 to 19.4 in 2025 (lowest in 30 years)." [17]

**7. The Gaokao's meritocratic promise is widely believed despite nuance.**

A 2022 study in the *Journal of China-ASEAN Studies* by Ye Liu investigates "the role of China's Gaokao in promoting social mobility" and finds that "the Gaokao's impact on 'changing destiny' is nuanced and context-dependent." [13] Despite this, "I believe the majority of people still believe in meritocracy in China," Ye Liu says, "because despite its flaws, the national test is still seen as 'the fairest, most democratic institution in China.'" [16]

A 2025 article in the *American Political Science Review* by Hanzhang Liu argues that "meritocracy can be used to co-opt large numbers of ordinary citizens by providing them with an opportunity of socioeconomic advancement instead of income redistribution, as long as the selection process is viewed as inclusive and rule-based." [18]

### Comparative Insights

**China:** The Gaokao uses the "3+1+2" model introduced in reforms, with "three compulsory subjects (Chinese, Math, Foreign Language, each 150 points), one choice from Physics or History (100 points), and two from Chemistry, Biology, Politics, or Geography (100 points each, level-graded), totaling 750 points." [19] The Gaokao "spans 2-4 days, with no retake within the same year." [19] Professor Xiong Bingqi, director of the 21st Century Education Research Institute, said the reforms "have provided students with greater flexibility in subject selection and aim to place more emphasis on students' individuality and personal interests. However, the issue of solely relying on test scores persists. It is necessary to strengthen the reform of comprehensive quality evaluation." [14]

**Japan:** The Common Test for University Admissions was introduced in January 2021 to replace the National Center Test. It was "part of a broader educational reform emphasizing critical thinking, data interpretation, and logical expression over rote memorization." [10] The phrase "examination hell" (shiken jigoku) "continues to be used to describe the intense competition surrounding university entrance." [10] The test has faced criticism for "still relying heavily on multiple-choice formats, increasing pressure on students, and potentially widening educational inequality between well-resourced and rural schools." [10]

**United States:** The 2025 digital SAT "consists of two sections: Reading & Writing (54 questions, 64 minutes) and Mathematics (33 multiple-choice, 11 grid-in, 70 minutes), using computer-adaptive testing. SAT scores range 200-800 per section (total 400-1600), with rights-only scoring and results in days." [19] The SAT "allows multiple retakes and results within days" while the Gaokao "offers no same-year retake." [19] By 2024, "some elite universities (e.g., Harvard, Yale, Dartmouth) reinstated testing requirements." [3]

## PRO Argument 3: International Competitiveness (PISA Scores, TIMSS)

### Thesis Statement
International standardized assessments like PISA and TIMSS provide critical benchmarks for national educational competitiveness, driving improvements in human capital that directly correlate with economic growth, and nations with strong testing cultures consistently outperform those with weaker systems.

### Supporting Points

**1. PISA 2022 results show East Asian dominance in international assessments.**

The PISA 2022 results, released on December 5, 2023, tested 690,000 15-year-old students from 81 countries/economies. [20] Top performers include Singapore (first in all three subjects with scores 575 in maths, 543 in reading, 561 in science), followed by other East Asian systems. [20] The average combined score across mathematics, science, and reading shows Singapore leading with 559.7 points, followed by Macao (China) at 535.0, Chinese Taipei and Japan both at 533.0, and South Korea at 523.3. [21]

**2. Japan achieved top-tier PISA 2022 results.**

According to the official MEXT report on Japan's PISA 2022 performance: "Japan's average score for Mathematical Literacy (536) continues to be world class. Among OECD member countries, Japan ranks 1st (range: 1st-2nd)." [22] Japan achieved 1st in mathematics (536 points), 2nd in reading (516 points), and 1st in science (547 points) among OECD countries. [22] "Japan was identified as one of four 'resilient' education systems (along with Korea, Lithuania, and Chinese Taipei) that maintained or improved performance, well-being, and equity during the COVID-19 pandemic." [22]

**3. The United States shows mixed PISA results.**

The U.S. PISA 2022 results show: "The U.S. average reading literacy score (504) was higher than the OECD average score (476). The U.S. average mathematics literacy score (465) was not measurably different from the OECD average score. The U.S. average science literacy score (499) was higher than the OECD average score (485)." [23] "In 2022, there were 5 education systems with higher average reading literacy scores for 15-year-olds than the United States, 25 with higher mathematics literacy scores, and 9 with higher science literacy scores." [23]

According to FutureEd (December 2023), "The only reason the U.S. rose is because academic performance in once higher ranking countries fell by even more. In math, the U.S. rose from 29th place to 28th place, still in the bottom half of economically advanced nations. American students scored 13 points lower than in 2018, equivalent to losing two-thirds of a year of education—the lowest U.S. math scores recorded in the history of the PISA math test. More than a third of U.S. 15-year-olds are considered to be low performers. Only seven percent of American students can do math at advanced levels." [24]

**4. PISA data shows an unprecedented drop in global performance.**

The OECD PISA 2022 report shows "an unprecedented drop in performance: OECD average scores fell by 10 points in reading and nearly 15 points in maths compared to 2018—the latter being nearly triple any prior consecutive change. About 25% of OECD 15-year-olds (16 million children) are now low performers (below Level 2 proficiency)." [20] "The dramatic fall in maths and reading scores points to a negative shock affecting many countries simultaneously. The COVID-19 pandemic seems an obvious factor... However, take a closer look at the data... Educational trajectories were negative well before the pandemic hit. This indicates that long term issues in education systems are also to blame for the drop in performance." [20]

**5. Test scores are strongly correlated with economic growth.**

Eric Hanushek's research demonstrates that "long-run growth in gross domestic product (GDP) is largely determined by the skills of a nation's population." [25] Using data from 1960-2000, "three-quarters of cross-country variation in GDP per capita growth is explained by these test scores, dwarfing the explanatory power of years of schooling alone." [25] The research projects that "modest improvements in basic skills (bringing all students to at least a minimal proficiency level) would yield enormous economic gains—e.g., average GDP gains of 13% for lower-middle-income countries over 80 years." [25]

**6. COVID-19 learning losses have severe economic consequences.**

Stanford economist Eric Hanushek estimates that "the average student in the pandemic cohort will lose 6% of lifetime earnings, and the nation's economy will incur $31 trillion in losses over the rest of the century—17 times the losses from the pandemic downturn itself." [26] Harvard's Tom Kane and Dartmouth's Douglas Staiger "calculated that the 48 million public school students in the 2020-21 school year face $900 billion in lost earnings; partial recovery (about one-third of math gaps) has reduced that estimate to $600 billion." [26] Hanushek stated: "The learning losses are huge. Economic growth depends on the skills of your labor force. We're going to have a lower skilled workforce. We're going to have fewer inventions. People with high achievement and strong academic skills are good for the economy." [26]

**7. The achievement gap between top-performing and lower-performing nations is equivalent to years of schooling.**

The gap between Singapore (highest) and Cambodia (lowest) in PISA 2022 is "223 points, the equivalent of roughly seven and a half years of schooling." [27] European students are "about one to two school years behind their peers in most advanced countries and regions, and the gap is increasing." [28]

**8. TIMSS provides additional international benchmarking.**

TIMSS, conducted by the IEA, "measures student achievement in mathematics and science at fourth and eighth grades. Measuring trends in achievement since 1995, TIMSS data have enabled countries to make evidence-based decisions to improve educational policies." [29] "TIMSS data are recognized by UNESCO as a solid evidence base for monitoring progress towards Sustainable Development Goal 4." [29] In TIMSS 2019, "U.S. 4th-graders scored 535 in math (above centerpoint, but 14 systems higher) and 539 in science (7 systems higher). U.S. 8th-graders scored 515 in math (10 systems higher) and 522 in science (10 systems higher)." [30]

**9. PISA has significant influence on national education policy.**

According to the Wikipedia article on PISA, "PISA, and similar international standardised assessments of educational attainment are increasingly used in the process of education policymaking at both national and international levels. The impact of these international standardised assessments in the field of educational policy has been significant, in terms of the creation of new knowledge, changes in assessment policy, and external influence over national educational policy more broadly." [31]

### Comparative Insights

**China:** China's top-performing provinces (Beijing, Shanghai, Jiangsu, Zhejiang) have historically scored very high in PISA but "did not report scores due to school closures during data collection" in 2022. [32] "China did participate in the 2022 testing, but did not release those results." [33] The aggregated PISA score for China is 1,605, second only to Singapore (1,679). [34]

**Japan:** Japan is one of the top performers globally. On the 2022 PISA, "only 12 percent of Japanese students were low performers in math, compared to the OECD average of 31 percent, and the overall impact of socio-economic status on student performance in that subject was also measured at 12 percent, below the OECD average of 15 percent. Scores in Japan also tend to show low levels of variance in performance across schools." [35] Japan's PISA 2022 scores: Math 536, Reading 516, Science 547. [36] "Japan was one of the few countries to defy the trends" of declining scores. [24]

**United States:** U.S. PISA 2022 scores: Math 465, Reading 504, Science 499. [36] The U.S. "rose from 29th place to 28th place" in math, "still in the bottom half of economically advanced nations." [24] "School closures explained only 11 percent of the variation in countries' test scores; nearly 90 percent is attributable to other, unclear reasons." [24] U.S. scores are "highly racialized, correlating with household income." [37]

## PRO Argument 4: Teacher and School Accountability Systems

### Thesis Statement
Standardized testing provides the objective data foundation for holding teachers, schools, and education systems accountable for student outcomes, driving improvements in instructional quality, resource allocation, and educational equity.

### Supporting Points

**1. Test-based accountability has improved outcomes for disadvantaged students.**

According to the CRPE report "Twenty Years of Testing" (2023), "The academic review supports what many educators, including myself, observed over time: Collecting and reporting disaggregated outcome data by student subgroups such as race, income and disability unmasked low performance and many hidden individual needs." [38]

The Fordham Institute's McDougald cites "Chester Finn's observation that 'we have solid evidence over thirty years in America and beyond that students learn more when they—and their schools—are held to account for what and how well they're learning.'" [2]

**2. Value-added models using test scores have been adopted by 40 states.**

Eric Hanushek's pioneering work on "using student test scores to measure teacher effectiveness (value-added models) has led to 40 states adopting such evaluations." [39] Hanushek notes: "Nobody had any idea what it was all about... I eventually got the data... and wrote a dissertation on it. I was one of the first people there to write a serious quantitative analysis clearly of education." [39]

**3. Disaggregated data reveals hidden inequities.**

The Center on Reinventing Public Education's report notes that "collecting and reporting disaggregated outcome data by student subgroups such as race, income and disability unmasked low performance and many hidden individual needs." [38] The authors "recommend that we learn from the unintended consequences of NCLB and policies like Race to the Top. They urge us to steer clear of the political traps that keep us from talking about meaningful ways to measure student growth and school improvement, and to consider system changes that could better engage students while also remaining accountable to taxpayers." [38]

**4. China's teacher accountability is tied to Gaokao performance.**

Teachers in China receive financial incentives based on student performance. For example, "teacher bonuses (50,000 yuan for Peking University admissions)" are tied to student Gaokao results. [13] "Teachers' financial well-being is tied to student performance, leading to teaching to the test." [14]

**5. Japan's teacher rotation system ensures equity.**

Japan has a systematic approach to teacher quality: "Teachers are highly respected, undergo rigorous preparation (including a national exam and induction), and are rotated among schools every 3-5 years to ensure equity. Lesson study is a key professional learning practice." [35] However, Japan's teachers face extreme challenges: "Japan's public school teachers average 96 hours of overtime per month, putting them at high risk of karoshi (death from overwork). A record 12,192 public school teachers in Japan took leave due to mental illness in 2022, while another 953 resigned for the same reason." [40]

**6. NCLB's test-based accountability showed mixed results.**

A study published in the *Economics of Education Review* (December 2023) by John J. Gregg and Stéphane Lavertu examined "whether the district-level accountability system under the No Child Left Behind Act (NCLB) succeeded in breaking through local politics to prioritize disadvantaged students." Using a regression discontinuity design with Ohio data from 1999-2011, they found that "failure to make AYP led to an immediate disenrollment of over 100 economically disadvantaged students per district (a 3% drop), a suspicious jump in test scores, and changes in collectively bargained teacher pay and personnel policies. However, there was no redistribution of financial resources toward disadvantaged students' schools." [41] The study concluded that "while NCLB created incentives for performance improvement, the mechanism was not a fundamental shift in local governance but rather gaming behaviors (e.g., student disenrollment, reclassification) and administrative changes, raising equity concerns." [41]

**7. Global teaching evaluation systems show a consensus toward standardized indicators.**

A 2023 study published in *Sustainability* (15(1), 818) examined "18 evaluation systems across seven countries (USA, Germany, China, Japan, Australia, Singapore, Chile)" and found "a consensus toward standardized evaluation indicators, a focus on teachers' professional development as the primary objective, a shift from summative to formative evaluation methods, reduced risk for teachers in evaluation outcomes, and the diagnostic function of evaluations in improving teaching." [42]

**8. The Shanker Institute emphasizes proper use of testing data.**

According to the Albert Shanker Institute, "Testing data by themselves cannot be valid or invalid; validity is a characteristic of how the data are interpreted." The page outlines three critical distinctions: "(1) School vs. student performance – absolute performance measures (e.g., proficiency rates) gauge student performance, while growth measures are needed to assess a school's contribution; (2) Growth vs. cohort changes – changes in proficiency rates over time often compare different groups of students, not actual growth of the same students; (3) Test scores vs. proficiency rates – converting scores into categorical proficiency rates simplifies communication but loses detail and can distort interpretations." [43]

### Comparative Insights

**China:** China's education system uses a centralized, standardized approach. "Using six dimensions including evaluation indicators, evaluation objectives, evaluation methods, interest relations, rights and roles, and accountability models," China's system is characterized by centralized control. [42] "In China, a 2023 survey found 33% of teachers work over 55 hours a week, and 92% work overtime daily. After China banned private tuition in 2021, 75% of Chinese teachers experienced moderate to severe anxiety, and 28% of middle school teachers reported high risk of depression." [40]

**Japan:** Japan's system is "highly centralized with a 6-3-3-4 structure" under MEXT. [36] "Governance is shared between MEXT (national curriculum, certification, pay scales), prefectures (teacher appointments, funding), and municipalities (daily operations)." [35] "A national curriculum is revised every decade, with recent additions of English and computer programming. Assessment includes the National Assessment of Academic Ability (NAAA) for grades 6 and 9, and entrance exams for upper secondary and university." [35]

**United States:** The U.S. system is "highly decentralized with state and local control." [36] "The US spends 4.9% of GDP on education" compared to Japan's 3.4%. [36] The U.S. Department of Education has "approved two waiver requests (Iowa and Louisiana) but neither altered assessment systems, leaving uncertainty about how federal officials will respond to legislation that conflicts with ESSA's comparability and accountability requirements." [12] Virginia Secretary of Education Aimee Guidera has noted that "'actionable data is the foundation for improving performance and holding our schools accountable.'" [44]

## PRO Argument 5: Identifying Learning Gaps and Educational Inequality

### Thesis Statement
Standardized testing provides the only consistent, objective source of data about disparities in educational outcomes across student subgroups, making it an essential tool for identifying learning gaps and targeting resources to address educational inequality.

### Supporting Points

**1. Civil rights organizations rely on standardized test data to identify disparities.**

"A letter signed by 12 civil rights organizations including the NAACP and the American Association of University Women, explained, 'Data obtained through some standardized tests are particularly important to the civil rights community because they are the only available, consistent, and objective source of data about disparities in educational outcomes, even while vigilance is always required to ensure tests are not misused.'" [3]

**2. The U.S. has wider equity gaps than many other countries.**

According to NCES Fast Facts, "the U.S. average reading literacy score (504) was higher than the OECD average score (476)" but "the U.S. average mathematics literacy score (465) was not measurably different from the OECD average." [30] The webpage "also reports score gaps (90th vs. 10th percentile) as equity indicators, with U.S. gaps generally larger than many other systems." [30]

**3. PISA 2022 reveals persistent socio-economic gaps.**

"Socio-economically advantaged students scored 93 points more in mathematics than disadvantaged students on average across OECD countries." [45] "Disadvantaged students are more likely to be low performers, but some countries (e.g., Macao, Peru, Qatar) improved low performers without harming high achievers." [20]

**4. Standardized testing helps identify pandemic-related learning loss.**

The Nation's Report Card (NAEP) 2022 results showed "the largest declines in reading and math scores since 1990. Math scores dropped 7 points for 4th graders and 8 points for 8th graders; reading scores dropped 3 points for 4th graders and 3 points for 8th graders." [46] Without standardized testing, these declines would have remained invisible, and recovery efforts would lack a baseline. The National Assessment of Educational Progress (NAEP) "has tracked student achievement in the U.S. since 1969, providing the only consistent measure of what American students know." [46]

**5. Standardized testing data enables targeted interventions.**

The Center for American Progress report notes that "tests can identify which students are falling behind, which subjects need more attention, and which schools are struggling." [1] This data allows for "targeted interventions, resource allocation, and support for students who need it most." [1]

**6. NAEP data reveals persistent racial and socioeconomic achievement gaps.**

NAEP data consistently shows that "Black and Hispanic students score lower than White and Asian students, and students from low-income families score lower than students from higher-income families." [46] These gaps "have persisted for decades, despite numerous reform efforts." [46] Without standardized testing, these disparities would be harder to measure and address.

**7. Standardized testing supports evidence-based policy decisions.**

The Council of Chief State School Officers (CCSSO) argues that "high-quality assessments are essential for understanding student learning and informing instruction." [47] The data from standardized tests "provides the evidence base for policy decisions about curriculum, funding, and school improvement." [47]

### Comparative Insights

**China:** The Gaokao reveals stark regional disparities. "Students in developed coastal provinces have significantly higher pass rates than those in inland rural areas." [14] "Maotongchan School's 90% pass rate vs. 30% provincial average" demonstrates how schools can succeed through the system. [13] However, the Gaokao's meritocratic promise is nuanced: "the gap in college attendance between rich and poor students is large... because the gaokao is the only criterion, and the opportunities to prepare for it are so unevenly distributed across regions and income levels." [9]

**Japan:** Japan's PISA results show "low levels of variance in performance across schools," indicating relatively high equity. [35] "The overall impact of socio-economic status on student performance in math was measured at 12 percent, below the OECD average of 15 percent." [35] However, the Common Test has faced criticism for "potentially widening educational inequality between well-resourced and rural schools." [10]

**United States:** The U.S. has "wider equity gaps than many other countries." [30] "The U.S. average mathematics literacy score (465) was not measurably different from the OECD average," but the gaps between high and low performers are larger. [30] U.S. scores are "highly racialized, correlating with household income." [37] The Opportunity Insights study shows that "children from the wealthiest 1% of American families are 13 times more likely to score 1300+ on SAT/ACT than children from low-income families." [48]

## PRO Argument 6: Learning Motivation, Study Discipline, and Work Ethic

### Thesis Statement
Standardized testing systems create structured incentives that motivate student effort, develop study discipline and work ethic, and establish clear academic goals that help students—particularly those from disadvantaged backgrounds—focus their learning and achieve measurable progress.

### Supporting Points

**1. The Gaokao motivates extraordinary student effort and achievement.**

The Gaokao's "centralized hierarchical tournament" structure creates powerful incentives for student effort. As described in *The Highest Exam*, "the competition between students is centralized because it is the one standard of success, and it is hierarchical because success is defined in relative terms, by outcompeting peers." [9] This structure motivates students to study intensively, with many students reporting study schedules of 12-14 hours per day in their final year of high school. [49]

**2. Clear goals and benchmarks improve student motivation.**

Research in educational psychology consistently shows that "clear, specific, and challenging goals improve performance more than vague or easy goals." [50] Standardized tests provide these clear goals: students know exactly what they need to learn and what standard they need to meet. This is particularly important for "students from disadvantaged backgrounds who may lack external motivation and support systems." [50]

**3. Standardized testing creates a culture of academic seriousness.**

In Japan, the "examination hell" (shiken jigoku) culture, while criticized, creates a "culture of academic seriousness where students understand the importance of education and are motivated to work hard." [10] Japan's consistently high PISA scores (536 in math, 516 in reading, 547 in science) demonstrate that this culture produces strong academic outcomes. [22] "Only 12 percent of Japanese students were low performers in math, compared to the OECD average of 31 percent." [35]

**4. The SAT/ACT motivate college preparation and readiness.**

The SAT and ACT, despite their flaws, "motivate students to prepare for college-level work and provide a benchmark for college readiness." [17] In 2025, "2 million students took the SAT," and "41% met the math readiness benchmark." [17] While these numbers are low, they represent students who are actively preparing for college and who have a clear target for their preparation.

**5. Frequent testing is positively associated with performance.**

The 2026 study in *Studies in Educational Evaluation* found that "frequent testing was positively associated with performance, particularly benefiting low-SES students." [4] This suggests that regular, low-stakes testing can motivate consistent effort and learning, rather than last-minute cramming.

**6. Standardized testing provides valuable feedback for student self-assessment.**

Standardized tests provide "objective feedback that students can use to assess their own learning and identify areas for improvement." [8] ETS Global notes that "standardized tests provide data that helps teachers identify where students are thriving and where they need more support," [8] but this data also helps students understand their own strengths and weaknesses.

**7. The discipline of test preparation develops transferable skills.**

The process of preparing for high-stakes exams like the Gaokao, Common Test, or SAT develops "discipline, time management, and perseverance—skills that are valuable in higher education and the workforce." [51] These "non-cognitive skills" are increasingly recognized as important predictors of success in life. [51]

**8. Testing creates accountability that motivates all stakeholders.**

As Chester Finn noted, "we have solid evidence over thirty years in America and beyond that students learn more when they—and their schools—are held to account for what and how well they're learning." [2] This accountability creates motivation not just for students, but for teachers, administrators, and policymakers to prioritize student learning.

### Comparative Insights

**China:** The Gaokao creates intense motivation. Students study "5:30 a.m. to 10:00 p.m. study schedules," [49] and the competition is fierce—only "2% gain entry to the 39 highest-ranking universities, and only 5 in 10,000 secure a place at Tsinghua or Peking University." [49] This motivates extraordinary effort, but at a cost: "One is self-harm, which comes from depression—there are a lot of depressed people." [49] The Double Reduction Policy (2021) was a direct government acknowledgment that exam-oriented education was causing excessive study time, with Chinese students averaging "55 hours of study per week (vs. 44 international average)" and "87.6% of junior/senior high school students sleeping under 8 hours." [52]

**Japan:** Japan's "examination hell" creates similar motivation. "Over 37% of Japanese students attended juku" (cram schools) in 2018, with sessions typically running from 5 p.m. to 8 p.m., often extending later. [53] Naohiko Harata, an 18-year-old student, reported studying until "2:00 a.m. or 3:00 a.m. after returning from juku." [53] The 2021 Common Test reform aims to shift from "rote memorization" to "critical thinking, data interpretation, and logical expression," [10] but the intense competition remains.

**United States:** The U.S. system has less intense exam pressure, but SAT/ACT preparation still motivates millions of students. "SAT participation rose from 1.5 million in 2021 to over 2 million in 2025." [17] However, the U.S. also has higher rates of "test anxiety," with "48% of U.S. students citing feelings of anxiety about performance" and "37% indicating that they feel overwhelming pressure regarding grades." [54] The U.S. approach to test motivation is less centralized but also less effective at driving high performance, as evidenced by U.S. PISA scores that are "still in the bottom half of economically advanced nations." [24]

# Part II: Con Arguments Against Standardized Testing

## CON Argument 1: Mental Health Crisis—Exam-Induced Stress, Anxiety, and Depression

### Thesis Statement
High-stakes standardized testing systems in China, Japan, and the United States inflict severe and measurable psychological harm on students, including elevated rates of depression, anxiety, and stress-related disorders, with mounting evidence from 2021–2026 confirming a causal relationship between testing pressure and declining adolescent mental health.

### Supporting Points

**1. China: The Gaokao creates severe psychological distress.**

A 2025 critical review in *Language Testing in Asia* synthesizes research on the social impact of the Gaokao and finds that 81% of Chinese children worry "a lot" about exams, of which 63% are afraid of being punished by teachers. Over one-third of students report psychosomatic symptoms weekly. Only 30% of children study out of genuine interest, while 46.5% study to please parents, and 71% of seniors are unaware of their own interests. [55]

A 2023 epidemiological study in *BMC Psychiatry* investigated depressive disorders among Chinese youth transitioning from the Gaokao to college. A two-stage cross-sectional survey of 6,922 incoming freshmen (mean age 18.6, 71.4% female) found that the sex-adjusted lifetime prevalence of depressive disorders was 7.5%, and the 9-month cumulative incidence of new-onset depressive disorders during the Gaokao-to-college transition was 2.3%. Critically, over one-third (36.5%) of depressed youth had their first onset during this critical period. The adjusted lifetime treatment rate was only 8.7%. [56]

A former Henan high school teacher (using the nickname "Lucy") described the situation to Radio Free Asia (2024): "I can only describe it as tragic. [The Gaokao] really is torture for students. It's a very extreme exam." She added: "One is self-harm, which comes from depression—there are a lot of depressed people. Another is finding a way to take it out on others through bullying. Bullying that leads to death and disability is particularly common." Students often destroy their books after the exam: "As soon as the Gaokao is done, they tear up all their books and go wild. They destroy all of it immediately, and never want to look at it again, ever. [The whole thing] is so destructive to the human spirit." [49]

**2. China: High school students show alarming mental health indicators.**

A 2025 cross-sectional study in *Nature Scientific Reports* examined the relationship between physical activity and mental health among 1,668 Chinese high school students (aged 15-18) in Xiamen, Fujian Province. Only 30.9% of students met the WHO-recommended 60 minutes of daily moderate-to-vigorous physical activity. Logistic regression identified Grade 11 students as having the highest risk of mental health problems (OR=2.421, 95% CI: 1.893-3.096), while being female (OR=0.630, 95% CI: 0.502-0.791) and MVPA non-compliance (OR=0.792, 95% CI: 0.736-0.851) were additional risk factors. [57]

**3. Japan: The "examination hell" culture takes a heavy toll.**

Japan's "examination hell" (shiken jigoku) culture creates intense pressure. "Over 37% of Japanese students attended juku" (cram schools) in 2018, with sessions typically running from 5 p.m. to 8 p.m., often extending later, leaving little free time and causing sleep deprivation and eye fatigue. [53] Ikuo Amano, University of Tokyo sociology professor, stated: "Jukus are harmful to Japanese education and children." [53]

A 2021 study in the *Hungarian Educational Research Journal* with 211 Japanese senior high school students and 145 university students found that the vast majority of respondents (88% of high school, 80% of university students) had attended or currently attend cram schools. While most students evaluated shadow education positively, a minority held negative views, citing forced attendance, harmful emotional consequences, or social inequality concerns. [58]

**4. United States: Rising test anxiety and stress.**

According to 2018 PISA data, 48% of U.S. students cited feelings of anxiety about performance, with 37% indicating that they feel overwhelming pressure regarding grades. [54] Student-reported anxiety linked to testing rose from 22% in 2015 to 40% in 2019. [54] The resulting pressure creates a "pressure cooker environment" leading to anxiety, stress, and burnout. A poor test score can lead to a fixed mindset, where students believe their intelligence is static rather than malleable. [54]

A study cited by Ivy Coach shows that 87% of students feel that standardized tests amplify their stress. The article argues that the high-stakes nature of standardized testing subconsciously illuminates competitiveness that pins students against one another, potentially correlating to self-deprecation and mental health struggles. [59]

**5. United States: Testing time and frequency contribute to stress.**

According to the National Library of Medicine, "Between 10-40% of all students experience some level of test anxiety, with these rates varying, depending on factors such as gender, race and socioeconomic status." [60] One teacher from the Association of Texas Professional Educators stated: "We cannot rely on data from one test, on one day of the year. Some students do not test well, others get sick on testing day, some are tired, and some just plain don't care about how they do on their tests." [60]

WellbeingPort reports that "Standardized testing causes headaches, sleep problems, depression, anxiety, stress and attendance issues." Figlio and Meer documented that students' level of the stress hormone cortisol rises by about 15 percent on average in the week when high-stakes standardized tests are given. [61]

**6. The psychological impact is worse for disadvantaged students.**

Students from disadvantaged backgrounds often score lower and face higher stress levels, amplifying feelings of inadequacy. [54] The pressure to succeed on high-stakes exams is particularly intense for students who see education as their only path to social mobility. This creates a "double burden" of academic pressure and socioeconomic anxiety. [54]

### Comparative Insights

**China:** The mental health crisis is most acute in China due to the Gaokao's singular importance. A 2020 study of over 8,000 middle and high school students found that 44% suffered from mild to severe depression and 37% had symptoms of anxiety during the COVID-19 pandemic. [62] The Chinese government's Double Reduction Policy (2021) was a direct response to these concerns, aiming to reduce homework burden and off-campus training. [52] However, the policy's effectiveness is debated, and academic pressure remains intense.

**Japan:** Japan's mental health challenges are compounded by teacher burnout. "Japan's public school teachers average 96 hours of overtime per month, putting them at high risk of karoshi (death from overwork). A record 12,192 public school teachers in Japan took leave due to mental illness in 2022." [40] The rotation system ensures equity but also adds stress to teachers who must adapt to new schools regularly.

**United States:** The U.S. system has the highest rates of reported test anxiety, but the stakes are lower than in China or Japan. The NYSUT "More Teaching Less Testing" Task Force Report (2025) argues that current standardized tests, particularly in grades 3-8, are overly frequent, developmentally inappropriate, and cause undue stress on students, parents, and educators, while failing to provide significant educational value. [63]

## CON Argument 2: Teaching to the Test and Curriculum Narrowing

### Thesis Statement
Standardized testing systematically narrows the curriculum across all three nations by incentivizing teachers to prioritize tested subjects and rote memorization over deeper learning, with devastating effects on non-tested subjects like arts, music, social studies, and science.

### Supporting Points

**1. United States: Extensive evidence of curriculum narrowing.**

The NYSUT "More Teaching Less Testing" Task Force Report (2025), composed of 65 educators including 19 New York State Teachers of the Year, argues that current standardized tests are overly frequent, developmentally inappropriate, and cause undue stress while failing to provide significant educational value. It cites that U.S. students take an average of 112 standardized tests from pre-K to 12th grade. The report concludes that the current overemphasis on high-stakes testing narrows the curriculum, promotes "teaching to the test," and fails to prepare students with 21st-century skills like critical thinking, collaboration, and creativity. One teacher is quoted: "We are dropping Science and Social Studies, and because of these tests, our students' knowledge gap is actually getting wider." Another says: "Kids are not just a number and should not say things like, 'I am a high 2.'" [63]

**2. Meta-analysis confirms widespread curriculum narrowing.**

Au's (2007) meta-synthesis, still widely cited in 2021-2026 literature, found that about 70% of studies reported curriculum narrowing, 50% reported fragmented knowledge, and 65% reported increased teacher-centered pedagogy. [64]

**3. Post-NCLB data shows significant shifts in instructional time.**

A 2012 study by Farkas Duffett Research Group, sponsored by the Ford Foundation and American Federation of Teachers, surveyed 1,001 3rd-to-12th grade public school teachers. Two-thirds of teachers (66%) report that other subjects "get crowded out by extra attention being paid to math or language arts." Math (55%) and language arts (54%) are the only two subjects getting more attention, while about half say art (51%) and music (48%) get less, and 40% say foreign language and 36% say social studies get less. This narrowing is most prevalent in elementary schools (81% of elementary teachers report crowding out). Teachers overwhelmingly attribute the shift to state tests: 93% of those who see crowding out say it is driven by state tests. [65]

**4. The impact on teaching quality and teacher professionalism.**

The NCTE Policy Research Brief identifies three primary impacts: (1) Changing the nature of teaching—teachers spend 60 to 110 hours per year on testing-related institutional tasks (data collection, grouping students, aligning curricula), reducing instructional time, and often face mandated scripted curricula that de-professionalize them. (2) Narrowing the curriculum—subjects like music, art, foreign languages, and social studies are squeezed out; in ELA, instruction shifts to low-level reading skills over writing, and machine scoring prioritizes mechanics over substance. (3) Limiting student learning—tests ignore non-cognitive skills (curiosity, perseverance, sociability), which are crucial for life success; they foster negative self-perceptions; create a binary proficient/not-proficient label that discourages learners; and disproportionately harm poor, minority, and English language learner populations. [66]

**5. The distinction between "curriculum teaching" and "item teaching."**

Assessment expert W. James Popham makes a clear distinction between "curriculum-teaching" (aligning instruction to broad standards) and "item-teaching" (drilling on specific test question clones), deeming the latter unethical and counterproductive. [67] A landmark Chicago study (Newmann, Bryk, and Nagaoka) showed that classrooms using authentic intellectual work—assignments requiring complex problem-solving, elaborated communication, and real-world application—produced 20% higher gains on standardized tests than classrooms relying on drill-and-kill methods. The researchers concluded: "Fears that students will score lower on conventional tests due to teacher demands for more authentic intellectual work appear unwarranted." [67]

**6. China: The Gaokao's "distorted education."**

The *Language Testing in Asia* (2025) critical review identifies "distorted education (teaching to the test)" as one of five categories of "collateral damage" from the Gaokao. The review traces the Gaokao's history and finds that the massive scale of the exam (10 million test-takers annually, only 40% entering college, 1.6% top-tier) creates powerful incentives for teaching to the test. [55] A Scribd comparative analysis notes that "China mandates nine years of education and has a rigorous exam system for high school and college admissions, while the U.S. offers more flexibility and holistic grading. Criticism of the Chinese system focuses on its emphasis on testing, which some argue produces 'robot students' rather than fostering creativity and well-rounded learning." [68]

**7. Japan: Entrance exam focus encourages superficial learning.**

The National Academies report (1999) on Japanese engineering education noted that "the focus on entrance examination scores in Japan encourages students to find the quickest and easiest way to answer examination questions, often to the detriment of deeper understanding." [69] The report notes that U.S. and Japanese practices concerning university entrance "are quite different, with major implications for other aspects of engineering education." [69]

**8. Japan's 2021 Common Test reform acknowledges the problem.**

Japan's 2021 Common Test reform aims to measure "the ability of students to discover and solve problems for themselves. Emphasis will be put on their ability to think, make judgements, and express themselves." [70] However, the government decided against using private English tests, leaving the English section unclear. [70] Kazuo Maruyama (Benesse Research Corporation) said: "Japanese higher education has traditionally been rooted in developing academics who are top researchers rather than becoming innovators in the workplace." [71]

### Comparative Insights

**China:** The Double Reduction Policy (2021) was a direct government acknowledgment that exam-oriented education was causing excessive study time and curriculum narrowing. Background factors include Chinese students averaging 55 hours of study per week (vs. 44 international average), 45% of Shanghai students attending math tutoring, and 87.6% of junior/senior high school students sleeping under 8 hours. [52] Policy measures include homework limits (no written homework for grades 1-2, ≤60 minutes for grades 3-6, ≤90 minutes for middle school), strict regulation of private tutoring, and bans on ranking students and frequent exams. [52]

**Japan:** Japan's curriculum is described as "standardized, teacher-centered" compared to the U.S.'s "flexible, student-centered" approach. "Japanese classrooms often employ a teacher-centered approach, while U.S. classrooms favor a student-centered approach encouraging interactive learning and critical thinking." [72] The 2021 Common Test reform attempts to address these concerns by emphasizing critical thinking, but the basic structure of the system remains exam-focused.

**United States:** The U.S. has the most extensive evidence of curriculum narrowing, with the most research and policy attention focused on this issue. The NYSUT report recommends "revising federal testing mandates by returning to grade-span testing or representative sampling, fixing the grades 3-8 assessment system by revising exam content, and reimagining high school graduation requirements by allowing alternative pathways such as interdisciplinary capstone projects, project-based learning, performance-based assessments, dual-enrollment courses, and CTE/apprenticeships." [63]

## CON Argument 3: Socioeconomic Bias and Inequality in Testing Outcomes

### Thesis Statement
Standardized tests across all three nations function as instruments of social reproduction, systematically advantaging students from wealthier families through unequal access to test preparation resources, tutoring, and enrichment, while penalizing low-income and rural students.

### Supporting Points

**1. United States: Stark income-based disparities in SAT/ACT scores.**

The Opportunity Insights study (2023) by economists Raj Chetty, John Friedman, and David Deming reveals stark income-based disparities. Children from the wealthiest 1% of American families are 13 times more likely to score 1300+ on SAT/ACT than children from low-income families. Only 2.5% of low-income test-takers (bottom 20% income) score 1300+, compared to about 17% of top-quintile test-takers. Among all students, one-third from the highest-income households score 1300+, compared to less than 5% of middle-class students and only 0.6% from the lowest quintile (where only one in five even takes the test). [48]

The disparities begin early in childhood (vocabulary, readiness, health) and widen primarily through unequal out-of-school opportunities (summer camps, travel sports, extracurriculars). Deming emphasizes that while schools can do more, the inequality largely stems from what happens outside the classroom. He argues against eliminating the SAT because it remains a key tool for academically talented low-income students to be noticed: "Getting rid of the test doesn't make the disparity go away. It just makes it invisible in the eyes of the public." He also notes: "The problem isn't the test. The problem is everything that happens before the test." [48]

**2. New York Times analysis confirms the pattern.**

The New York Times (2023) analysis of Opportunity Insights data reports: "Test takers whose families were in the top 20 percent of earners were seven times as likely as those in the bottom 20 to score at least 1300. The children of the richest 1 percent were 13 times as likely as the poorest students to score this high." The gap widened to 13 times for children of the top 1%, and even higher for the top 0.1% (average parental income $11 million). [73]

Sean Reardon (Stanford): "Kids in disadvantaged neighborhoods end up behind the starting line even when they get to kindergarten." Nate G. Hilger: "K-12 schools only manage 10 percent of children's time, and they do it pretty equitably. The other 90 percent of nonschool time masks all the most important inequality of opportunity." [73]

**3. Racial gaps in SAT scores persist.**

The Brookings Institution (2017) analysis by Richard V. Reeves and Dimitrios Halikias examines persistent racial gaps in SAT math scores using 2015 College Board data for nearly 1.7 million test-takers. The mean math scores are 428 for Black, 457 for Latino, 534 for White, and 598 for Asian students. Gaps are stark at the extremes: among top scorers (750-800), 60% are Asian and 33% White, while only 5% are Latino and 2% Black. At the low end (300-350), 37% are Latino, 35% Black, 21% White, and 6% Asian. The black-white gap has remained virtually unchanged for fifteen years (about 0.88 standard deviations). [74]

**4. The NEA highlights the racist origins of standardized testing.**

The NEA (2023) article "The Racist Beginnings of Standardized Testing" argues that standardized testing has racist origins and continues to perpetuate racial and socioeconomic inequality. It traces the history to early 20th-century eugenicists like Carl Brigham, who developed the SAT and believed tests proved the superiority of the "Nordic race group," and Lewis Terman, who created the Stanford-Binet IQ test. [75]

**5. China: Regional and rural-urban disparities in Gaokao preparation.**

The Gaokao reveals stark regional disparities. "Students in developed coastal provinces have significantly higher pass rates than those in inland rural areas." [14] A review of *The Highest Exam* notes: "In the US, socioeconomic status is highly correlated with college admissions, and the gap in college attendance between rich and poor students is large. But the Chinese system is even more unequal because the gaokao is the only criterion, and the opportunities to prepare for it are so unevenly distributed across regions and income levels." [9]

The Gaokao's meritocratic promise is thus a "myth" in some respects. A 2022 study in the *Journal of China-ASEAN Studies* by Ye Liu finds that "the Gaokao's impact on 'changing destiny' is nuanced and context-dependent, with higher education's effects varying widely across different life trajectories." [13] "I believe the majority of people still believe in meritocracy in China," Ye Liu says, "because despite its flaws, the national test is still seen as 'the fairest, most democratic institution in China.'" [16]

**6. Japan: The juku industry exacerbates inequality.**

Japan's cram school (juku) industry creates a two-tiered system where wealthier students can afford additional preparation. "Over 37% of Japanese students attended juku" in 2018, [53] and the cost of juku can be substantial. The Hungarian Educational Research Journal study found that "educational experts' and sociologists' common criticism—that shadow education has a detrimental effect on fairness or equal chances in education—was 'hardly at all expressed' by students themselves." However, a minority of students (especially university boys) held negative views, citing forced attendance, harmful emotional consequences, or social inequality concerns. [58]

**7. The test preparation industry is a multi-billion dollar business.**

The global K-12 testing and assessment market size was valued at USD 13.6 billion in 2025 and is projected to grow to USD 26.83 billion by 2034. [5] This market is driven by test preparation, and the costs are disproportionately borne by families who can afford them. In China, the private tutoring industry (buxiban) was so large and problematic that the government banned it in 2021 through the Double Reduction Policy. [52]

### Comparative Insights

**China:** The Gaokao's regional disparities are the most extreme. "About 13.35 million students registered for the 2025 Gaokao," [14] but the resources available to students vary dramatically by province. Students in Beijing and Shanghai have access to better schools, more qualified teachers, and more preparation resources than students in rural Henan or Gansu. The Double Reduction Policy (2021) attempted to address this by banning private tutoring, but the policy's effectiveness is debated.

**Japan:** Japan's juku system creates a parallel education system that reinforces existing inequalities. "Only about 3 in 10 students gain admission to their university of choice," [76] and the competition for spots at prestigious universities is intense. The 2021 Common Test reform aims to reduce the emphasis on rote memorization, but the basic structure of the system remains competitive and unequal.

**United States:** The U.S. has the most extensive research on socioeconomic bias in standardized testing. The Opportunity Insights data is the most comprehensive analysis to date, showing that "the disparities begin early in childhood and widen primarily through unequal out-of-school opportunities." [48] The test-optional movement gained momentum during COVID-19, with "~90% of US colleges no longer requiring SAT/ACT after pandemic-era test-optional policies." [16] However, some elite schools (Yale, MIT, Dartmouth) have reinstated testing, arguing that test scores can help identify high-potential students from under-resourced backgrounds. [3]

## CON Argument 4: The Shadow Education Industry and Cram School Culture

### Thesis Statement
High-stakes standardized testing systems have spawned massive shadow education industries in all three countries—juku in Japan, buxiban in China, and test prep in the US—that exacerbate educational inequality, increase student stress, and undermine the integrity of the formal education system.

### Supporting Points

**1. Japan: The juku industry is deeply embedded in education.**

Japan's cram school (juku) industry is the most developed. "Over 37% of Japanese students attended juku" in 2018, with sessions typically running from 5 p.m. to 8 p.m., often extending later. [53] "40% of primary school final-year students and over 65% of high school final-year students attend cram schools." [76] The industry is for-profit and creates a two-tiered system where wealthier students can afford additional preparation.

Ikuo Amano, University of Tokyo sociology professor, stated: "Jukus are harmful to Japanese education and children. It's not healthy for kids to have so little free time. It is not healthy to become completely caught up in competition and status at such a young age." [53]

**2. China: The buxiban industry was so large it required government intervention.**

China's private tutoring industry (buxiban) was a multi-billion dollar sector that employed millions of tutors. The Double Reduction Policy (2021) was a direct response to the excesses of this industry. Background factors include Chinese students averaging 55 hours of study per week (vs. 44 international average), 45% of Shanghai students attending math tutoring, and 87.6% of junior/senior high school students sleeping under 8 hours. [52]

The policy banned for-profit tutoring in core academic subjects, limited homework, and restricted off-campus training. However, the policy has had mixed results. "After China banned private tuition in 2021, 75% of Chinese teachers experienced moderate to severe anxiety, and 28% of middle school teachers reported high risk of depression." [40] The policy also drove some tutoring underground, creating a black market for test preparation.

**3. United States: The for-profit test prep industry.**

The U.S. has a large for-profit test preparation industry, including companies like Kaplan, The Princeton Review, and Khan Academy (non-profit). "SAT participation rose from 1.5 million in 2021 to over 2 million in 2025," [17] and the test prep market is a significant industry. The cost of test preparation can be substantial, with private tutoring costing hundreds of dollars per hour. This creates a system where wealthier students can afford better preparation, further exacerbating existing inequalities.

**4. The shadow education industry undermines formal education.**

The shadow education industry "mirrors and reinforces the formal education system, but it is private, for-profit, and often unregulated." [77] This creates a system where students who can afford additional tutoring have a significant advantage over those who cannot. The industry also creates pressure on students to attend cram schools in addition to their regular schooling, leading to longer study hours and increased stress.

**5. China: The Double Reduction Policy's impact on the shadow education industry.**

The Double Reduction Policy (2021) aimed to address the excesses of the shadow education industry. The policy "banned for-profit tutoring in core academic subjects, limited homework, and restricted off-campus training." [52] However, the policy has had mixed results. "75% of Chinese teachers experienced moderate to severe anxiety, and 28% of middle school teachers reported high risk of depression" after the policy was implemented. [40] The policy also drove some tutoring underground, creating a black market for test preparation.

**6. Japan: The juku industry's role in "examination hell."**

Japan's juku industry is deeply embedded in the "examination hell" (shiken jigoku) culture. A 2021 study in the *Hungarian Educational Research Journal* found that 88% of Japanese high school students and 80% of university students had attended or currently attend cram schools. [58] The industry is for-profit and creates a two-tiered system where wealthier students can afford additional preparation. Critics argue that jukus are "too exam-focused," though some are shifting to holistic learning. [76]

### Comparative Insights

**China:** The shadow education industry in China was the largest and most heavily regulated. The Double Reduction Policy (2021) was a direct response to the industry's excesses, but its effectiveness is debated. The policy has had unintended consequences, including increased teacher anxiety and the creation of a black market for tutoring. [52]

**Japan:** Japan's juku industry is the most deeply embedded in the education system. "Over 37% of Japanese students attended juku" in 2018, [53] and the industry is a significant part of the "examination hell" culture. The 2021 Common Test reform aims to reduce the emphasis on rote memorization, but the juku industry is likely to adapt to the new test format.

**United States:** The U.S. test prep industry is the most market-driven. "SAT participation rose from 1.5 million in 2021 to over 2 million in 2025," [17] and the test prep market is a significant industry. The test-optional movement during COVID-19 reduced the importance of SAT/ACT for college admissions, but some elite schools have reinstated testing requirements, [3] potentially reviving the test prep industry.

## CON Argument 5: Suppression of Creativity, Critical Thinking, and 21st-Century Skills

### Thesis Statement
Standardized testing systems across all three countries systematically suppress creativity, critical thinking, and other 21st-century skills by prioritizing rote memorization, recall of factual knowledge, and test-taking strategies over deeper learning, problem-solving, and innovative thinking.

### Supporting Points

**1. The Gaokao produces "robot students" rather than creative thinkers.**

A Scribd comparative analysis notes that "Criticism of the Chinese system focuses on its emphasis on testing, which some argue produces 'robot students' rather than fostering creativity and well-rounded learning." [68] The Gaokao's emphasis on memorization and recall creates students who "may excel at memorization but feel uncertain when asked to form independent opinions." [78]

Think Academy (2025) argues that "traditional tests rely heavily on rote memorization and recall, neglecting higher-order skills like analysis, evaluation, and synthesis. The result is a generation of learners who may excel at memorization but feel uncertain when asked to form independent opinions." [78]

**2. Japan's entrance exam focus encourages superficial learning.**

The National Academies report (1999) on Japanese engineering education noted that "the focus on entrance examination scores in Japan encourages students to find the quickest and easiest way to answer examination questions, often to the detriment of deeper understanding." [69] This creates a system where students prioritize test-taking strategies over genuine learning and understanding.

**3. Japan's 2021 Common Test reform acknowledges the need for change.**

Japan's 2021 Common Test reform aims to measure "the ability of students to discover and solve problems for themselves. Emphasis will be put on their ability to think, make judgements, and express themselves." [70] The reform was designed to shift from "the ability to memorize knowledge to the ability to think, judge, and express oneself." [10] However, the test has faced criticism for "still relying heavily on multiple-choice formats, increasing pressure on students, and potentially widening educational inequality between well-resourced and rural schools." [10]

**4. The U.S. system's emphasis on test-taking skills.**

The NYSUT "More Teaching Less Testing" Task Force Report (2025) argues that the current overemphasis on high-stakes testing "fails to prepare students with 21st-century skills like critical thinking, collaboration, and creativity." [63] Teachers report that the pressure to improve test scores leads to a focus on test-taking strategies rather than deeper learning.

**5. Research shows that standardized tests do not measure creativity.**

Dr. Robert Sternberg of Cornell adds: "Standardized tests are necessarily narrow in scope... they do not assess creative skills, practical skills, or wisdom-based ethical skills." [79] The NCTE Policy Research Brief notes that "tests ignore non-cognitive skills (curiosity, perseverance, sociability), which are crucial for life success." [66]

**6. The "item-teaching" approach undermines genuine learning.**

Assessment expert W. James Popham's distinction between "curriculum-teaching" and "item-teaching" is crucial here. "Item-teaching fosters rote memorization, as opposed to a true understanding of concepts." [80] A landmark Chicago study (Newmann, Bryk, and Nagaoka) showed that classrooms using authentic intellectual work—assignments requiring complex problem-solving, elaborated communication, and real-world application—produced 20% higher gains on standardized tests than classrooms relying on drill-and-kill methods. The researchers concluded: "Fears that students will score lower on conventional tests due to teacher demands for more authentic intellectual work appear unwarranted." [67]

**7. The U.S. system's decentralized nature allows for more innovation.**

The U.S. system's "highly decentralized with state and local control" [36] allows for more innovation in assessment. The Center for American Progress report identifies three innovative approaches: "matrix sampling, through-year assessments, and performance-based assessments." [1] These approaches aim to address the limitations of traditional standardized tests and promote deeper learning.

### Comparative Insights

**China:** The Gaokao's emphasis on memorization and recall is the most extreme. "71% of seniors are unaware of their own interests" [55] because the system does not encourage or reward exploration of personal interests. The 3+1+2 reform model aims to give students more choice, but "the issue of solely relying on test scores persists. It is necessary to strengthen the reform of comprehensive quality evaluation." [14]

**Japan:** Japan's 2021 Common Test reform is a direct acknowledgment that the old system suppressed creativity and critical thinking. The reform aims to shift from "the ability to memorize knowledge to the ability to think, judge, and express oneself." [10] However, the test's continued reliance on multiple-choice formats limits its effectiveness in achieving this goal.

**United States:** The U.S. system has the most potential for innovation, with its decentralized structure allowing for experimentation. However, the U.S. also has the most extensive evidence of curriculum narrowing and teaching to the test. The NYSUT report recommends "alternative pathways such as interdisciplinary capstone projects, project-based learning, performance-based assessments, dual-enrollment courses, and CTE/apprenticeships." [63] These alternatives could help address the suppression of creativity and critical thinking.

## CON Argument 6: Cheating, Gaming the System, and the Integrity Crisis

### Thesis Statement
High-stakes standardized testing systems in all three countries create perverse incentives for cheating, score manipulation, and system gaming that undermine the validity of test results, erode public trust, and disproportionately harm disadvantaged students.

### Supporting Points

**1. United States: NCLB accountability led to gaming behaviors.**

A study published in the *Economics of Education Review* (December 2023) by John J. Gregg and Stéphane Lavertu found that "failure to make AYP led to an immediate disenrollment of over 100 economically disadvantaged students per district (a 3% drop), a suspicious jump in test scores, and changes in collectively bargained teacher pay and personnel policies. However, there was no redistribution of financial resources toward disadvantaged students' schools." [41] The study concluded that "while NCLB created incentives for performance improvement, the mechanism was not a fundamental shift in local governance but rather gaming behaviors (e.g., student disenrollment, reclassification) and administrative changes, raising equity concerns." [41]

**2. The Atlanta Public Schools cheating scandal.**

The most notorious U.S. cheating scandal was the Atlanta Public Schools cheating scandal (2009-2011), where teachers and administrators were found to have systematically altered student test scores. The scandal "resulted in criminal convictions for 11 educators, including the former superintendent." [81] The scandal revealed the intense pressure that high-stakes testing creates and the willingness of some educators to cheat to meet targets.

**3. China: Gaokao cheating scandals.**

The Gaokao has been plagued by cheating scandals. In 2020, "a candidate was caught using a hidden camera to take photos of the exam paper and upload them to a chat group for answers." [82] In 2021, "a candidate was caught using a smartphone to search for answers during the exam." [82] These scandals are rare but high-profile, and they undermine public trust in the system.

**4. The Gaokao's high stakes create incentives for cheating.**

The Gaokao's status as "the central meritocratic channel for social advancement" [9] creates intense pressure to succeed, and this pressure can lead to cheating. The exam is "designed to be cheat-proof, with metal detectors, jammers, and surveillance cameras," [82] but cheating still occurs. The high stakes also create incentives for "proxy test-takers" who take the exam for others.

**5. Japan: The 2021 Common Test and cheating concerns.**

The 2021 Common Test reform was designed to address concerns about the old system, but it has also faced cheating concerns. In 2021, "a student was caught using a smartphone to take photos of the test paper and upload them to a chat group." [83] The incident raised concerns about the security of the new test format.

**6. The integrity crisis erodes public trust.**

The cheating scandals in all three countries erode public trust in the testing system. When test scores are manipulated, the validity of the entire system is called into question. This is particularly damaging in systems like China's, where the Gaokao is seen as "the fairest, most democratic institution in China." [16] Cheating scandals undermine this perception of fairness.

**7. The "teaching to the test" phenomenon is a form of gaming.**

The "teaching to the test" phenomenon, while not technically cheating, is a form of gaming the system. Teachers and schools focus on test-taking strategies and specific test content at the expense of broader learning. This undermines the validity of test scores as measures of genuine learning. [64]

**8. The COVID-19 pandemic created new opportunities for cheating.**

The shift to online learning and testing during the COVID-19 pandemic created new opportunities for cheating. "In an era of AI-assisted essays and inflated transcripts, a standardized score is the closest thing colleges have to a signal that a machine cannot easily fake." [16] However, online proctoring has also raised privacy concerns and has been criticized for being biased against students of color and low-income students. [84]

### Comparative Insights

**China:** The Gaokao's cheating scandals are rare but high-profile. The exam is "designed to be cheat-proof, with metal detectors, jammers, and surveillance cameras," [82] but cheating still occurs. The high stakes of the exam create powerful incentives for cheating, and the system's reliance on a single test score makes the consequences of cheating particularly severe.

**Japan:** Japan's 2021 Common Test reform aimed to address concerns about the old system, but it has also faced cheating concerns. The 2021 incident where a student used a smartphone to take photos of the test paper raised concerns about the security of the new test format. [83]

**United States:** The U.S. has the most extensive evidence of system gaming, with the Atlanta Public Schools cheating scandal being the most notorious example. The NCLB-era gaming behaviors (student disenrollment, reclassification) are well-documented in the academic literature. [41] The test-optional movement during COVID-19 reduced the stakes of standardized testing for college admissions, but the reinstatement of testing requirements by some elite schools [3] suggests that the integrity crisis is not resolved.

# Part III: Conclusion and Synthesis

## Summary of Key Findings

The evidence on whether standardized tests improve K-12 education is deeply contested, with compelling arguments on both sides. The six pro arguments demonstrate that standardized testing provides essential accountability, meritocratic selection, international competitiveness, teacher accountability, identification of learning gaps, and motivation for student effort. The six con arguments reveal that standardized testing causes mental health crises, curriculum narrowing, socioeconomic bias, shadow education industries, suppression of creativity, and cheating and gaming behaviors.

## Comparative Analysis Across the Three Countries

**China** exemplifies the most extreme version of a test-driven system. The Gaokao's singular importance creates powerful incentives for student effort and achievement, and China's PISA scores are among the highest in the world. However, the system also produces the most severe mental health consequences, the most extensive curriculum narrowing, and the most extreme regional disparities. The Double Reduction Policy (2021) represents a significant attempt to address these problems, but its effectiveness is debated.

**Japan** represents a middle ground. The Common Test reform (2021) represents a significant attempt to shift from rote memorization to critical thinking, but the "examination hell" culture persists. Japan's PISA scores are among the highest in the world, and the system has relatively low levels of variance in performance across schools. However, the juku industry is deeply embedded, and teachers face extreme working conditions.

**United States** represents the most decentralized and diverse system. The U.S. has the most extensive evidence of curriculum narrowing and socioeconomic bias, but also the most potential for innovation. The test-optional movement during COVID-19 reduced the stakes of standardized testing for college admissions, but some elite schools have reinstated testing requirements. The U.S. PISA scores are mediocre, particularly in mathematics, where the U.S. ranks in the bottom half of OECD countries.

## Policy Implications

The evidence suggests that the debate over standardized testing is not simply about whether to test, but about how to design and use assessments. The 2026 study in *Studies in Educational Evaluation* found that "frequent testing was positively associated with performance, particularly benefiting low-SES students; conversely, high-stakes accountability was linked to lower achievement, especially among high-SES students. These patterns held across high- and low-SES economies, indicating that policy design matters beyond national wealth. The study argues for context-sensitive policies that use testing as diagnostic feedback to support learning and equity." [4]

Key policy implications include:

1. **Use testing as diagnostic feedback, not high-stakes punishment.** The evidence suggests that frequent, low-stakes testing can improve learning, while high-stakes accountability creates perverse incentives.

2. **Invest in alternative assessment methods.** Matrix sampling, through-year assessments, and performance-based assessments offer promising alternatives to traditional standardized tests.

3. **Address the shadow education industry.** The Double Reduction Policy in China and the Common Test reform in Japan represent attempts to address the excesses of the shadow education industry, but more work is needed.

4. **Invest in mental health support.** The mental health crisis caused by high-stakes testing requires significant investment in counseling, stress management, and support services.

5. **Address socioeconomic inequality.** The evidence shows that standardized tests systematically advantage wealthier students. Addressing this inequality requires investment in early childhood education, out-of-school opportunities, and targeted support for disadvantaged students.

6. **Promote a balanced curriculum.** The evidence of curriculum narrowing is extensive. Policies should protect non-tested subjects like arts, music, social studies, and science.

## Final Assessment

The evidence does not support a simple yes or no answer to whether standardized tests improve K-12 education. The answer depends on how the tests are designed, how they are used, and what the alternatives are. The most promising approach is to use testing as one tool among many, with a focus on diagnostic feedback, formative assessment, and performance-based evaluation. The three countries studied—China, Japan, and the United States—each face unique challenges and opportunities in reforming their testing systems, and each can learn from the others' experiences.
