# Comprehensive Review: Recurrent Lateral Ankle Sprains in 16–30-Year-Old Basketball Players (2020–2026)

## Executive Summary

This comprehensive review synthesizes evidence from 2020–2026 on recurrent lateral ankle sprains in recreational and collegiate basketball players aged 16–30 with season-to-season instability. The review covers four domains: (1) biomechanical and neuromuscular factors most implicated in recurrence, (2) patterns documented across international literature including case reports and case series, (3) comparative outcomes of rehabilitation programs versus surgical stabilization, and (4) return-to-play criteria and prevention protocols that most effectively reduce re-injury risk. Key findings include that peroneal reaction time deficits (effect size 0.72–1.24), proprioceptive angular errors exceeding 6.0° (OR=2.87), and cavovarus foot alignment are strongly associated with recurrence. Exercise-based rehabilitation reduces reinjury risk by approximately 40% (OR=0.60), while surgical stabilization—particularly arthroscopic Broström-Gould with internal-brace augmentation—offers accelerated return-to-play timelines (4.2 weeks faster). The Ankle-GO score (<8 points conferring 9-fold increased recurrence risk) and the PAASS framework represent the most validated return-to-play decision-making tools. Prevention protocols combining bracing (69% risk reduction) with neuromuscular training (50–60% risk reduction) are most effective.

## 1. Biomechanical and Neuromuscular Factors Most Implicated in Recurrent Lateral Ankle Sprains

### 1.1 Peroneal Muscle Weakness and Delayed Reaction Time

Peroneal muscle dysfunction is among the most consistently identified neuromuscular factors in recurrent ankle sprains. A ten-year retrospective cohort study (2013–2022) of 1,524 professional male soccer players found that peroneal strength asymmetries exceeding 15% were associated with an adjusted odds ratio of 2.34 (95% CI: 1.52–3.61) for recurrent ankle sprains. Neuromuscular deficits persisted up to 18 months post-injury in players who later re-injured, and combined deficits (proprioception + strength) showed a near-multiplicative risk increase (OR=6.73) [1].

A systematic review with meta-analysis of 23 studies on peroneal reaction time (PRT) found that previous ankle sprain is associated with moderate-to-strong PRT deficits, with an overall effect size of 0.67 (95% CI: 0.37–0.95, P<0.001). Patients classified as having chronic ankle instability demonstrated large-magnitude PRT deficits in between-groups comparisons (ES=0.72, 95% CI: 0.29–1.14) and side-to-side comparisons (ES=1.24, 95% CI: 0.70–1.79) [28].

A 2022 study on peroneal electromechanical delay (EMD) in healthy athletes found that fatigue caused a significant increase in EMD by 10–15 ms (p=0.003), suggesting that improving resistance to fatigue may add another layer of protection against ankle sprain recurrence [29]. A 2026 study further suggests that mechanoreceptor signaling delay, rather than nerve conduction velocity, is the primary contributor to delayed peroneal reaction time in chronic ankle instability [30].

### 1.2 Proprioceptive Deficits

Proprioceptive deficits—encompassing joint position sense, force sense, and kinesthesia—are strongly implicated in recurrent instability. In the same cohort of 1,524 professional soccer players, proprioceptive deficits exceeding 6.0° angular error showed the strongest independent association with recurrence (adjusted OR=2.87, 95% CI: 1.91–4.31). The composite neuromuscular deficit scoring system achieved a C-statistic of 0.847 [1].

A 2020 study of 80 track-and-field athletes (aged 18–25) found that athletes with chronic ankle sprain demonstrated statistically significant deficits in proprioception (mean error 3.46° vs. 1.25° in healthy controls), along with mild pain (mean VAS 3.90 cm). The authors concluded that deficits in foot proprioception, static and dynamic balance, even one year after the ankle sprain, could be the reason for limitations in the dynamic defense system of the joint, predisposing to recurrent injury and instability [16].

The 2021 clinical practice guideline from the Academy of Orthopaedic Physical Therapy provides a Grade A recommendation: "Clinicians should prescribe proprioceptive and neuromuscular therapeutic exercise to improve dynamic postural stability and patient-perceived stability during function in individuals with CAI" [22].

### 1.3 Cavovarus Foot Alignment

Cavovarus foot alignment is a significant structural contributor to recurrent lateral ankle instability. A 2020 study from Yonsei Medical Journal evaluated outcomes of the modified Broström procedure combined with corrective osteotomies for chronic lateral ankle instability (CLAI) in patients with subtle cavovarus deformity. Fifteen patients (all male, mean age 25.7 years) were followed for a mean of 58.5 months. Additional procedures included calcaneal lateral closing wedge osteotomy (46.7%), first metatarsal dorsiflexion osteotomy (73.3%), and gastrocnemius lengthening (13.3%). Clinical outcomes (VAS, AOFAS, Karlsson-Peterson scores) improved significantly (all p<0.001), and no recurrent instability was reported. The authors concluded that cavovarus deformity is an anatomical risk factor for CLAI, and simultaneous correction with MBP leads to good outcomes and prevents recurrence [17].

A 2026 study published in the Journal of Orthopaedic Research found that individuals with CAI demonstrated cavus-like morphology, indicating that bony alignment may be a structural contributor to recurrent instability [18]. A 2020 literature review notes that subtle cavovarus foot affects up to 25% of the population and is characterized by a high medial arch, hindfoot varus, and plantarflexed first ray, leading to altered gait biomechanics, lateral foot overload, and increased risk of chronic foot and ankle pathologies including recurrent lateral ankle sprains [19].

A 2025 study of 81 patients with CLAI and subtle cavus foot found that those receiving arthroscopic modified Broström procedure plus calcaneal osteotomy had significantly better AOFAS scores at 3 and 24 months (P<0.01) compared to those receiving Broström alone, with lower complication rates (6.52% vs. 11.43%). The authors concluded that correcting hindfoot alignment while stabilizing the ankle joint is necessary to enhance function and reduce recurrence [23].

### 1.4 Talar Tilt and Anterior Drawer Laxity (Mechanical Instability)

Mechanical instability, as measured by talar tilt and anterior drawer testing, is a well-established factor. A cross-sectional study of 106 elite male football and basketball players found that positive anterior drawer and talar tilt tests were significantly more common in athletes with acute and recurrent LAS, particularly in the left ankle. The authors concluded that lateral ankle ligament laxity, impaired balance, and reduced plantarflexion may be intrinsic risk factors for LAS [3].

A 2025 mini-review of 45 studies (3,474 participants) evaluated the anterior drawer test (ADT) for diagnosing mechanical ankle instability. The ADT shows good to excellent intra-rater reliability but poor to moderate inter-rater reliability. The minimal detectable change (MDC) for manual ADT is 1.995 mm, while instrumented ADT has a higher MDC of 6.153 mm. The authors recommend standardized protocols and population-specific thresholds [24].

A 2024 narrative review summarizes that stress radiography quantifies laxity but has high variability and radiation exposure, while ultrasonography provides dynamic, non-invasive assessment with high sensitivity (up to 97.7%) for ATFL injury. MRI is essential for preoperative evaluation of associated osteochondral lesions, present in 23–50% of CLAI patients [25]. Up to 40–70% of patients can develop chronic lateral ankle instability after an initial sprain, with a 25% overall prevalence (46% in those with prior sprain) [20].

### 1.5 Postural Control and Dynamic Balance Deficits

Deficits in dynamic postural control, as measured by the Star Excursion Balance Test (SEBT) and Y-Balance Test (YBT), are consistently documented. The 2021 Y-Balance Test Lower Quarter systematic review and meta-analysis (57 studies) demonstrated high reliability (intra-rater ICCs 0.85–0.91, inter-rater ICCs 0.81–1.00). Sport-specific comparisons revealed that basketball athletes had significantly lower composite scores (92.8 %LL) compared to soccer (97.6 %LL) and baseball (97.4 %LL) athletes. For injury prediction, anterior reach asymmetry (≥4 cm) was associated with increased injury risk, with an odds ratio of 2.20 [32].

The 2021 clinical commentary on the SEBT provides updated recommendations: use the modified version (mSEBT) with three directions (anterior, posteromedial, posterolateral); perform four familiarization trials and three recorded trials per direction; normalize reach distances to lower limb length. Smallest detectable changes for normalized scores are approximately 5.9% (ANT), 7.8% (PM), and 7.6% (PL). Cutoff scores for injury risk include composite <94% (females 6.5x risk, males 3x) and anterior asymmetry ≥4 cm or >4.5% [57].

### 1.6 Proximal Neuromuscular Control (Hip and Knee)

Emerging evidence highlights the role of proximal neuromuscular control. A 2024 RCT on hip-strengthening in 30 patients with CAI found that targeted hip strengthening improved dynamic balance and patient-reported outcomes [35]. A 2024 study on core stabilization in basketball players with CAI demonstrated that CAI negatively affects core stabilization, dynamic balance, and agility [34].

A 2026 study of 55 CAI and 55 matched controls found that individuals with CAI demonstrate altered landing mechanics, including reduced hip and knee flexion angles, which may increase the risk of recurrent sprains and secondary injuries [13]. The shift toward a hip-dominant strategy for postural control in CAI patients suggests that rehabilitation should target both whole-body coordination and ankle-specific adaptation [36].

## 2. International Literature Patterns

### 2.1 Geographic Distribution of Research

The international literature on recurrent lateral ankle sprains in basketball players shows distinct geographic patterns:

**United States** produces the highest volume of research globally. Key institutions include the University of Kentucky (Gribble), University of Virginia (Hertel), Duke University (Gross, Goodloe, Nunley), and UNC Chapel Hill. The NBA epidemiological data show that ankle sprains affect approximately 26% of NBA players per season, accounting for 14.7% of all NBA injuries. NCAA data show an injury rate of 1.34 per 1,000 athlete exposures in men's college basketball [2][5].

**Japan** is the second most active research hub for basketball-specific ankle injury epidemiology. Sekine et al. (2022) found that the overall injury rate in Japanese collegiate men's basketball was 1.55 to 1.64 times higher than NCAA data, and the ankle sprain rate was 2.10 times higher. Severe injuries (time loss >21 days) were also more frequent in Japan (IRR=2.23). Kikumoto et al. (2026) surveyed 2,747 youth basketball players and found ankle sprain history in 61.2% of U-15 players and 39.0% of U-12 players, with CAI prevalence of 12.4% in both age groups. Medical consultation rates were concerningly low (56.6% in U-15 vs. 80.1% in U-12), and many players returned to play despite pain (~54%) [21].

**Taiwan** reported the highest CAI prevalence in basketball. Lin et al. (2022) studied 388 basketball athletes (243 men, 145 women; mean age 22.3) and found that 97% had experienced an ankle sprain, 26% had unilateral CAI, 50% had bilateral CAI, and only 24% had no CAI. Women had significantly higher prevalence than men (85% vs. 70%), particularly at the collegiate level (p=0.001) [2].

**Netherlands/Belgium** produce high-impact systematic reviews. Wagemans et al. (2022) conducted a systematic review with meta-analysis of 14 RCTs (2,182 participants) showing that exercise-based rehabilitation reduces reinjury prevalence at 12 months (OR=0.60; 95% CI: 0.36 to 0.99). Wagemans et al. (2023) audited 177 exercises from 14 RCTs and found that only 31% of exercises were multiplanar and only 18% included a flight phase, concluding that current rehabilitation exercises are generic and do not reflect the pathomechanics of non-contact LAS [6][7][8].

**Other regions** with emerging research include South Korea (Park et al., 2025—hop-stabilization training in adolescent female basketball players), China (comprehensive review on CAI management, January 2025), Australia (basketball injury epidemiology showing 3.85 ankle injuries per 1,000 athlete-participations), the UK (PAASS framework development via Delphi consensus with 155 experts from 19 countries), Spain (FC Barcelona 22-season analysis showing ankle injuries constitute 10.1% of all injuries), and Pakistan (19% CAI prevalence in collegiate recreational athletes, predominantly functional rather than mechanical instability) [5][9][21].

### 2.2 Case Reports and Case Series

**USA**: A 2021 case report by Paswan documented a 14-year-old African American basketball player with chronic ankle instability resulting from recurrent lateral ankle sprains. The patient presented one month after an acute sprain that had not healed normally, with primary concerns of lateral ankle pain, decreased range of motion and strength, and impaired balance. The patient successfully completed rehabilitation, returning to sport with external bracing and improved balance and joint stability [5].

**South Korea**: Park et al. (2025) conducted a double-blind, cluster-randomized controlled trial comparing hop-stabilization training with traditional balance training in 32 adolescent female basketball players with CAI. The hop training group showed significantly greater improvements in dynamic performance tests (T-test, lateral hop, figure-8 hop) and ankle dorsiflexion and eversion strength compared to the balance training group [21].

**Indonesia**: A 2026 cross-sectional study of 43 active basketball players in Kupang, Indonesia, found that most participants had poor agility (60.5%) but normal ankle flexibility (88.4%) and a low mean FAOS of 80.03. No significant relationship was found between agility score and ankle injury risk (p=0.954) or between ankle flexibility and injury risk (p=0.794), suggesting that factors like proprioception and prior injury history play a larger role [21].

**India**: A 2025 cross-sectional study in Gujarat found that 73% of all basketball athletes had experienced recurrent ankle sprain, and 59% of these athletes had significant disability. A 2016 perspective article from the Sancheti Institute College of Physiotherapy noted that the injury rate is as high as 7.9 injuries per 1,000 athlete exposures in females and 7.1 in males, with more than 90% of elite athletes reporting a history of at least one ankle injury [21].

**Multi-country Female Sport Study**: A 2025 cross-sectional study of 578 female athletes (netball, soccer, basketball, volleyball) across Australia, New Zealand, the UK, and USA found that ankle sprains were the most common injury (77%), with 73% reporting CAI of the left ankle and 54% of the right ankle. The CAI group had significantly lower FAAM-S scores (p<0.05) and reduced HRQOL scores compared to potential copers and uninjured individuals [21].

Coaches cited lack of knowledge and limited practice time as barriers [21].

## 3. Comparative Outcomes: Rehabilitation Programs vs. Surgical Stabilization

### 3.1 Balance and Proprioceptive Training

Balance and proprioceptive training programs are the cornerstone of conservative management for recurrent ankle sprains. A systematic review published in *Sports* (2024) evaluated 8 RCTs on rehabilitation methods for CAI, including balance training, proprioceptive exercises, whole-body vibration, BAPS board training, and stroboscopic vision training. All interventions improved dynamic balance and subjective stability, but no single method was consistently superior. The review concluded that personalized programs combining multiple approaches are recommended [22].

A review of systematic reviews found that balance training is the most effective standalone intervention for CAI, improving functionality, instability, dynamic balance, and health-related quality of life. Whole-body vibration is a useful adjunct, and joint mobilization is effective in increasing ankle dorsiflexion and improving balance. Despite the frequent use of external support, it has not been supported as a sole intervention [6].

The APTA clinical practice guideline (2021) provides a Grade A recommendation for proprioceptive and neuromuscular therapeutic exercise to improve dynamic postural stability and patient-perceived stability in individuals with CAI [22].

### 3.2 Neuromuscular and Perturbation Training

A 2022 RCT comparing 8 weeks of neuromuscular training (NG) vs. strength training (SG) vs. no intervention in 67 physically active athletes with CAI found that both intervention groups showed significant within-group improvements. Between-group comparisons revealed significant differences favoring NG and SG over controls in posteromedial SEBT, posterolateral SEBT, FAAM-ADL, and FAAM-SPORT, but no significant differences were found between NG and SG. The study concluded that both neuromuscular and strength training are effective for CAI, but neither is superior [40].

A 2024 RCT (Level 2 evidence) investigated the effect of adding neuromuscular training to conventional training in 34 individuals with CAI. The experimental group (conventional plus neuromuscular training) showed significant improvements in tibialis anterior muscle cross-sectional area, posteromedial Y-balance reach, and FAOS composite, sport, and ADL subscales (effect sizes small to moderate, Hedges g 0.40–0.73) compared to conventional training alone [38].

A study comparing neuromuscular adaptation to repeated mediolateral perturbations between 22 individuals with CAI and 23 healthy controls found that healthy controls decreased TA-PL co-contraction with practice, while CAI showed no significant change. CAI individuals exhibited improved margin of stability and increased hip torque coordination across sessions, indicating a shift toward hip strategy. The authors concluded that rehabilitation for CAI should target both whole-body coordination and ankle adaptation exercise since the ankle joint appears refractory to change in CAI individuals [36].

### 3.3 Peroneal Strengthening Protocols

Peroneal strengthening is a critical component of rehabilitation. The UCSF Orthopaedic Institute provides an ankle strengthening protocol designed to improve muscular strength, regain balance and proprioception, and increase functional abilities over 6–8 weeks. Exercises include ankle eversion, dorsiflexion, inversion, and calf raises (3 sets x 15 reps, 1–2 times/day), along with balance exercises (single-leg balancing, single-leg squats, half-circle taps, and floor touches) [52].

The AAOS Foot and Ankle Conditioning Program (4–6 weeks, 3–5 days/week) includes stretching exercises (Heel Cord Stretch, Golf Ball Roll, Towel Stretch) and strengthening exercises (Calf Raises, Ankle Range of Motion, Marble Pickup, Towel Curls, Ankle Dorsiflexion/Plantar Flexion using elastic band, Single Leg Balance) [65][67].

E3 Rehab recommends ankle-specific strength exercises including eversion with bands, dorsiflexion with bands, and heel raises (3 sets of 25 reps for bands, 3–4 sets of 6–12 for weighted heel raises) [34].

### 3.4 Bracing and Taping

External supports are highly effective for preventing recurrent ankle sprains. A 2024 RCT compared a novel adaptive ankle brace (Betterguards) to conventional ankle taping in 13 young subelite male soccer players after acute LAS. The adaptive brace selectively resists high-velocity inversion but allows full range of motion at normal speeds. Median RTS was 52.5 days for the brace group vs. 79.5 days for the taping group, but the difference was not statistically significant (P=0.109; large effect size deltaMAD=0.94). No re-injuries occurred in either group. User surveys showed 100% rated the brace comfortable/very comfortable, and 71% rated the experience better than previous ankle taping [12][14].

Taping and bracing have been shown to decrease the risk of ankle sprains by 50–70% in those with a history of ankle sprains [22]. A 2022 meta-analysis found that taping improves gait functions, reduces inversion-eversion range of motion, decreases peroneus longus muscle activation, reduces postural sway, and increases ankle stabilization, with no evidence of weakening [27].

The APTA clinical practice guideline (2021) notes that for acute LAS, using external supports and progressive weight-bearing is a Grade A recommendation. For CAI, external support alone is not recommended (Grade B) [8].

### 3.5 Multi-Modal Rehabilitation Programs

A systematic review update with meta-analysis (PROSPERO: CRD42020210858) assessed whether exercise-based rehabilitation reduces reinjury after acute lateral ankle sprain. Fourteen RCTs with 2,182 participants were included. Pooled data showed a significant reduction in re-injury prevalence at 12 months favoring exercise-based rehabilitation over usual care (OR: 0.60; 95% CI: 0.36 to 0.99), equating to a 6% absolute risk reduction (22% vs. 16% reinjury rate). However, re-injury incidence results were not significant. Meta-regression found no significant association between training volume and odds of re-injury. The authors conclude that exercise-based rehabilitation reduces reinjury risk, but optimal exercise content remains unclear [2].

A systematic review published March 2023 in *Physical Therapy in Sport* analyzed 177 exercises from 14 RCTs. Results showed that 44% of exercises addressed neuromuscular function (postural control), 23% performance tasks (plyometrics, jumping, running), 20% muscle strength, and 13% range of motion. Only 31% incorporated multiplanar movements, and only 18% of exercises included a flight phase. The review concluded that current rehabilitation exercises are generic, simplistic, and do not reflect the pathomechanics of non-contact ankle sprains. Future interventions should incorporate more open chain joint position sense training, multiplanar single limb challenges, and jumping and landing exercises [6][7][8].

The Massachusetts General Brigham Sports Medicine non-operative rehabilitation protocol for lateral ankle sprain is both time-based and criterion-based: Phase I (1–2 weeks) focuses on protection and pain/edema reduction; Phase II (3–6 weeks) on normalizing gait and improving ROM and single-leg balance; Phase III (7–10 weeks) on advancing strength and balance with plyometrics/agility; and Phase IV (11–16 weeks) on full strength and return to sport. Return-to-sport criteria include ≥90% on Y-balance and hop tests, with no pain/swelling during plyometrics [39][60].

### 3.6 Surgical Stabilization: Broström-Gould Repair

**Open vs. Arthroscopic Techniques**

A systematic review and meta-analysis (8 studies, 408 patients) comparing open versus arthroscopic Broström surgery for chronic lateral ankle instability found that the arthroscopic approach showed statistically significant superior outcomes at 6 months and 1 year postoperatively in AOFAS functional scores (mean difference >11 points) and VAS pain scores. Time to weightbearing was significantly shorter in the arthroscopic group (9 vs. 14.25 weeks). Wound complications were significantly lower with arthroscopy (1.5% vs. 9.2%). There were no significant differences in overall complication rates, nerve complications, or return to sport. The authors conclude that while arthroscopic Broström is technically more demanding, it offers superior short-term functional outcomes and faster recovery, but long-term data are lacking [37].

A retrospective study comparing mid-term outcomes (minimum 48-month follow-up) of arthroscopic vs. open Broström-Gould repair in 99 patients found that both groups showed significant improvements in ADT, VAS, AOFAS, K-P, and Tegner activity scores. The arthroscopic group had significantly higher AOFAS and K-P scores at 6 months postoperatively, but no significant differences were found at final follow-up. Complication rates were similar (16.3% vs. 14%), with arthroscopic showing more superficial peroneal nerve injuries and open showing more wound infections [38].

A more recent systematic review and meta-analysis (14 studies, 875 patients, published 2026) comparing arthroscopic vs. open Broström-Gould procedures found no statistically significant differences in surgical time, AOFAS scores, Tegner activity scores, or complication rates. The arthroscopy group achieved significantly better K-P scores (MD=0.78, p=0.04), VAS scores (MD=−0.65, p=0.02), and time to return to sports (MD=−3.91 weeks, p=0.03). However, these differences were small and below the minimal clinically important difference (MCID). The authors conclude that both techniques show broadly comparable outcomes [43].

**Gould Modification**

A systematic review and meta-analysis (10 studies, 635 patients, mean follow-up 19.5 months) compared the Broström procedure with vs. without the Gould modification (inferior extensor retinaculum reinforcement). No significant differences were found between groups in AOFAS scores, Karlsson scores, talar tilt, or anterior talar translation. Complication rates were also comparable. The authors conclude that the Gould modification does not provide additional clinical benefit, potentially simplifying surgical protocols for CAI [2].

### 3.7 Internal-Brace Augmentation

Internal-brace augmentation (suture tape augmentation) represents a significant advancement in surgical stabilization. A review article from AAOS Now (March 2026) reports that suture tape augmentation reinforces the modified Broström-Gould repair with high-strength tape, providing biomechanical load-to-failure of 250–315 N (vs. native ATFL ~154 N) and stiffness of 21–31 N/mm (vs. native 14.5 N/mm). Clinical evidence shows accelerated return to play: a randomized trial (Kulwin et al., 119 patients) found return to preinjury activity at 13.3 weeks vs. 17.5 weeks (P=0.001). Systematic reviews report that athletes undergoing augmented repairs often return to sport at 8 to 12 weeks when following aggressive rehabilitation protocols, compared to the historical time frame of 12 to 24 (or more) weeks for nonaugmented repairs [8].

A multicenter, prospective randomized controlled trial of 119 patients with chronic lateral ankle instability found that patients treated with InternalBrace augmentation returned to their previous activity level an average of 4.2 weeks sooner than those treated with standard Broström repair alone (13.3 weeks vs. 17.5 weeks, p<0.001). At 26 weeks, only 3.6% of InternalBrace patients had failed to return to activity, compared to 12.5% in the standard repair group [19].

A biomechanical study comparing the Broström technique to suture anchor repair and to IB augmentation found the IB group had a larger torque at failure of 11.2 Nm compared to 8.0 Nm for suture anchor group and 5.7 Nm for the Broström alone group [20].

A retrospective cohort study (ISAKOS 2023) comparing Broström repair with suture tape augmentation vs. Broström repair alone (104 patients, minimum 2-year follow-up) found no significant differences in postoperative FAAM ADL, FAAM Sport, SF-12 PCS, or Tegner activity scale. The authors conclude that both procedures yield similar clinical outcomes, but the augmented repair offers biomechanical advantages and allows earlier return to full weightbearing and faster rehabilitation [23].

### 3.8 Return-to-Play Timelines: Surgical vs. Non-Surgical

**Non-Surgical Timelines:**
- Grade I LAS: ~7 days (Loiacono, NBA PT); 5–14 days (Mass General)
- Grade II LAS: ~15 days; 2–3 weeks (Mass General)
- Grade IIIA LAS: ~31 days; 3–12+ weeks (Mass General)
- Grade IIIB LAS: ~55 days
- Most athletes return to full training in 1–3 weeks for mild sprains; 6–8 weeks for moderate sprains; 12+ weeks for severe sprains

**Surgical Timelines:**
- Modified Broström (open): Mean time to return to personal training 1.9 months, to team training 2.9 months, to competitive play 3.9 months. RTP 83.3% at 4 months and 100% at 8 months postoperatively [56]
- Arthroscopic Broström: Time to return to sports MD=−3.91 weeks faster than open (p=0.03) [43]
- InternalBrace augmentation: Return to preinjury activity at 13.3 weeks vs. 17.5 weeks for standard repair (p=0.001) [19]
- Systematic review of 10 studies (343 professional athletes): 89% returned to pre-injury level, mean time to return to sports 16 weeks (median 77 days, range 56–178 days) [2]

## 4. Return-to-Play Criteria and Prevention Protocols

### 4.1 Functional Testing Thresholds

**Hop Tests**

The Side Hop Test (SHT) has the largest effect size of all functional tests for identifying CAI (Hedges g = -2.314, p=0.001) per Rosen et al. (2017) meta-analysis [29]. The threshold for passing is <10 seconds for 10 repetitions, with a Limb Symmetry Index (LSI) ≥90% as the standard minimum across all hop tests [28]. Flore et al. (2024) recommend using direction-corrected LSI (LSIdc) with interval limits of ≥90% to ≤110%, as the conventional LSI can miss deficits on the "better" side [15].

The Figure-of-8 Hop Test requires <12 seconds for the course (two cones 5m apart) with LSI ≥90% (average LSI in healthy adults = 96.1%) [27][75]. The 6-Meter Timed Hop has a cutoff of LSI ≥88% as the strongest predictor—patients below 88% were over five times more likely to have impaired function [28].

**SEBT/Y-Balance Test**

The anterior reach cutoff is >4 cm asymmetry between limbs, which constitutes a failed test [7][26]. The composite score threshold is ≥94% (VBG Return to Competition protocol) [21]. Specific cutoffs include anterior asymmetry <4.5% or 4 cm, and posteromedial and posterolateral >91% [25]. The dorsiflexion lunge test (knee-to-wall) should be within 2 cm of the uninjured side—"If you're off by at least two centimeters compared to your other side you increase your risk for injury significantly" [11][12].

**Heel Rise Test**

The single leg heel raise test requires ≥25 full-range repetitions at 60 BPM metronome, with LSI ≥85–90% of the uninjured side [21][33]. The test is terminated if form breaks [7][26].

### 4.2 Isokinetic Strength Testing Criteria

The normative evertor/invertor strength ratio is 88.8% (da Fonseca et al., 2025), meaning evertors are normally approximately 89% as strong as invertors [1][2][4]. The dorsiflexors/plantar flexors ratio is 36.1% [1][2][4]. Restoration of this normal evertor/invertor strength ratio through an isokinetic ankle strengthening program is recommended [2][7].

Side-to-side comparisons require LSI ≥90% for all strength measures for final RTS (Phase III), with Phase II requiring ≥75% LSI for gastroc/soleus MVIC. The single-leg press should be ≥1.5× body weight for Phase II progression [24].

### 4.3 Patient-Reported Outcome Measure Thresholds

The **Cumberland Ankle Instability Tool (CAIT)** cutoff is ≥28 for adequate stability, with <28 indicating chronic instability risk [21]. The **Foot and Ankle Ability Measure (FAAM)** requires ≥95% for both ADL and Sports subscales [25]. The **Foot and Ankle Ability Measure — Sport (FADI Sport)** requires ≥29/32 score [21].

The **Ankle Ligament Reconstruction-Return to Sport after Injury (ALR-RSI)** has a validated cutoff of >63 points for RTS. The returned to sport group scored 68.8 points (range 56.5–86.5) vs. the non-returned group at 45.0 points (31.3–55.8), p=0.02 [19][29]. The ALR-RSI has excellent psychometrics (Cronbach's α=0.96, ICC=0.92) and is strongly correlated with Karlsson score (r=0.79) and AOFAS score (r=0.8) [19]. A short version (6 items) was validated in 2025 by Mahieu et al. [26][28].

The **Ankle-GO Score** is the first validated objective criterion for RTS after LAS. It combines 4 functional tests (single leg stance, mSEBT, Side Hop Test, Figure-8 Test) with 2 questionnaires (FAAM, ALR-RSI) for a maximum of 25 points [75]. Key cutoffs:
- **≥11 points**: 69% probability of becoming a coper vs. 6.8% if <11 [73]
- **<8 points at 2 months**: 9-fold increased risk of recurrence (OR=8.6; 95% CI: 2–37.2, p=0.001) [70]
- **≥8 points for RTS at preinjury level at 4 months**: 72% sensitivity, 66% specificity
- **<7 points for no RTS**: 67% sensitivity, 92% specificity [75]

The **I-PRRS Scale** cutoff is ≥50 (range 0–100), suggesting psychological readiness to return to sports [21].

### 4.4 The PAASS Framework

The PAASS framework (Pain, Ankle impairments, Athlete perception, Sensorimotor control, Sport/functional performance) was developed via Delphi consensus involving 155 health professionals from 19 countries working in elite field/court sports. Sixteen assessment items reached consensus (>70% agreement) for inclusion. No expert recommended time from injury as a criterion. The framework provides clinicians with expert-recommended domains to assess when determining an athlete's readiness to return to unrestricted training and competition [6][10].

Key findings from the 2026 Physical Therapy in Sport review of elite team protocols: only one StARRT domain (specific tests) was universally assessed. Key deficiencies identified included psychological readiness, proprioception, contextual modifiers, and ability to complete a full training session. The review recommends shifting from reductionist, single-time-point testing toward a complex-systems approach embedded across the RTS continuum, integrating progressive on-field rehabilitation and continuous monitoring [9].

### 4.5 Prevention Protocols

**External Supports**

Ankle bracing reduces recurrent ankle sprain risk by 69–71% (odds ratio ~0.31) [4][37]. Lace-up braces reduce first-time and recurrent sprains by 40–50% [44]. The JOSPT 2021 Clinical Practice Guideline provides a Grade A recommendation: "Clinicians should recommend the use of prophylactic bracing to reduce the risk of a first-time or recurrent LAS" [23][39]. Bracing is recommended for at least 6 months post-injury [21].

Taping is effective initially but loosens after 20–40 minutes of activity [40]. It requires a trained provider to apply and is more expensive than bracing (several hundred dollars per season). The choice between bracing and taping should be guided by personal preference [42]. Both are effective; NATA 2025 recommends personal preference [41].

**Neuromuscular and Proprioceptive Training**

Exercise-based prevention programs reduce ankle sprain rates by 50–60% if performed 2–5 times per week for 10–30 minutes per session [44]. Balance training alone reduces ankle injury rates by 68% in soccer players [19]. Balance board training provides approximately 50% reduction in recurrence [14].

The FIFA 11+ program reduces lower-limb injuries in basketball when done consistently [17]. The SHRED Injuries Basketball study recommends a neuromuscular training warm-up program including dynamic running, sideways shuffles, cutting drills, skipping, skate jumps, plyometric hopping, and static balance/strengthening [11].

The protective effect of balance training takes 6 weeks to 3 months to develop [44]. The Sure Steps systematic review (14 studies, 2024) found that neuromuscular training programs combining different contents (strength, stability, mobility, agility) are the most effective for both general injuries and ACL injuries. For ankle sprains specifically, the most effective measures are training programs based on analytical ankle stability exercises combined with the use of ankle braces [36].

**Peroneal Strengthening for Prevention**

Peroneal strengthening is critical because the peroneals are the primary dynamic stabilizers preventing inversion sprains. Recommended exercises include banded ankle eversion, resisted eversion with resistance bands (3 sets of 12–15 reps, 3–4 days/week), with an emphasis on eccentric control [34]. Peak and end-range eccentric evertor/concentric invertor ratios are important for comprehensive prevention [9].

**Workload and Load Management**

Emerging evidence supports the role of workload management in preventing recurrent ankle sprains. The acute:chronic workload ratio concept, while more established for other lower extremity injuries, is increasingly recognized as relevant for ankle sprain prevention. The 2026 Physical Therapy in Sport review recommends integrating continuous monitoring across a broader range of risk and readiness factors, including training load, and aligning with the RTS continuum (return to participation → return to performance) [9].

A 2023 study of 534 youth basketball athletes found that age category was the only significant predictor of lower limb injury history (p=0.529 for WBLT asymmetry, p=0.105 for LESS score), suggesting that developmental factors and cumulative load exposure are important considerations [35].

## 5. Integrated Recommendations for Clinical Practice

Based on the evidence synthesized above, the following integrated recommendations emerge for managing 16–30-year-old recreational and collegiate basketball players with recurrent lateral ankle sprains and season-to-season instability:

1. **Comprehensive Assessment**: Evaluate for all biomechanical and neuromuscular factors—peroneal strength (LSI >90%, evertor/invertor ratio ~89%), proprioception (angular error <6°), cavovarus alignment (clinical exam including Coleman block test, Silfverskiöld test), mechanical instability (ADT, talar tilt), and dynamic postural control (SEBT composite ≥94%, anterior asymmetry <4 cm).

2. **Rehabilitation First**: Initiate a progressive, multi-modal rehabilitation program incorporating balance training, neuromuscular exercise, peroneal strengthening, and sport-specific plyometrics. Ensure exercises are multiplanar, include flight phase, and reflect the reactive, multidirectional demands of basketball. The protective effect of balance training requires 6 weeks to 3 months to develop.

3. **External Support**: Use prophylactic ankle bracing (lace-up or semi-rigid) for at least 6 months after return to sport. Bracing provides 69–71% risk reduction for recurrent sprains. Taping is an acceptable alternative but requires skilled application and is less cost-effective.

4. **Surgical Consideration**: If 3–6 months of structured rehabilitation fails to resolve instability, surgical intervention is indicated. Arthroscopic Broström-Gould repair with internal-brace augmentation offers the fastest return to play (8–12 weeks for augmented repairs vs. 12–24+ weeks for nonaugmented) while maintaining excellent long-term outcomes. Correct underlying cavovarus deformity if present.

5. **Objective Return-to-Play Criteria**: Use the Ankle-GO score (≥11 for coper status, <8 conferring 9-fold recurrence risk) and the PAASS framework. Minimum thresholds include: hop test LSI ≥90%, SEBT composite ≥94%, anterior asymmetry <4 cm, heel rise ≥25 reps, CAIT ≥28, FAAM ≥95%, ALR-RSI ≥63, and sport-specific movement assessment.

6. **Prevention Protocols**: Implement neuromuscular training warm-up programs (FIFA 11+ or similar) 2–5 times per week, combined with prophylactic bracing. Address coach education gaps, particularly in settings where medical support is limited. The combined approach (bracing + exercise) provides the best risk reduction.

7. **Psychological Readiness**: Assess psychological readiness using the ALR-RSI or I-PRRS scale. The ALR-RSI cutoff of ≥63 points distinguishes those likely to return to sport from those who are not.
