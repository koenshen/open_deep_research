# Systematic Review: Comparative Efficacy of Training and Nutrition Interventions for Body Recomposition in Recreationally Active Adults Aged 18–35

## Executive Summary

This systematic review synthesizes international peer-reviewed literature and consensus guidelines published between January 2015 and August 2026 to determine the comparative efficacy of specific training and nutrition interventions for reducing adiposity while preserving or increasing lean body mass in recreationally active adults aged 18–35. The evidence base includes landmark meta-analyses, randomized controlled trials, and position stands from the American College of Sports Medicine (ACSM), the International Society of Sports Nutrition (ISSN), and other professional organizations.

Key findings indicate that successful body recomposition—simultaneous fat loss and lean mass preservation or gain—requires the strategic integration of multiple interventions. Protein intake of 1.6–2.4 g/kg/day during caloric restriction, combined with structured resistance training, provides the foundational nutritional support. Resistance training periodization models (linear, undulating, and block) produce comparable hypertrophy outcomes, though undulating models may offer modest advantages for strength development in trained individuals. High-intensity interval training (HIIT) and moderate-intensity continuous training (MICT) yield similar fat mass reductions when energy expenditure is matched, with HIIT offering superior time efficiency. Caloric deficits of 200–400 kcal/day (approximately 10–15% below maintenance) optimize muscle retention during fat loss. Time-restricted feeding protocols (16:8) can reduce body fat when combined with resistance training but offer no advantage over daily caloric restriction alone. Long-term maintenance requires sustained physical activity (≥250 minutes/week of combined aerobic and resistance exercise), continued higher protein intake (≥1.2 g/kg/day), and behavioral strategies including self-monitoring and habit formation.

## 1. Protein Intake Targets for Body Recomposition

### 1.1 General Protein Requirements for Active Adults

The landmark meta-analysis by Morton et al. (2018), published in the *British Journal of Sports Medicine*, analyzed 49 randomized controlled trials with 1,863 participants and established that total protein intakes above approximately 1.6 g/kg/day provided no additional benefit for fat-free mass gains during resistance training. The study demonstrated that protein supplementation significantly increased one-repetition-maximum strength by 2.49 kg, fat-free mass by 0.30 kg, and muscle fiber cross-sectional area by 310 µm², with effects more pronounced in resistance-trained individuals [1].

The Nunes et al. (2022) systematic review and meta-analysis of 74 RCTs confirmed that higher protein intake (≥1.6 g/kg/day in younger adults) leads to small but significant gains in lean body mass (SMD 0.22) and lower-body strength (SMD 0.40) during resistance exercise. The dose–response relationship was evident, with younger adults under 65 years benefiting from intakes of 1.6 grams per kilogram of body weight per day or higher [2,13].

The ISSN Position Stand on Protein and Exercise (2017) recommends daily protein intake of 1.4–2.0 g/kg body weight for most exercising individuals. Per serving, 0.25 g/kg or 20–40 g of high-quality protein is recommended to maximize muscle protein synthesis (MPS), with each dose containing 700–3,000 mg of leucine and a balanced array of essential amino acids. Protein should be evenly distributed every 3–4 hours throughout the day. The anabolic effect of exercise lasts at least 24 hours, making pre- or post-workout timing a matter of tolerance rather than a critical window [3].

### 1.2 Protein Intake During Caloric Deficit

During energy restriction, protein requirements increase substantially to offset the catabolic effects of the caloric deficit. The Longland et al. (2016) landmark randomized controlled trial, published in the *American Journal of Clinical Nutrition*, demonstrated that overweight young men consuming 2.4 g/kg/day of protein during a 40% calorie deficit gained 1.2 kg of lean mass and lost 4.8 kg of fat over four weeks, while those consuming 1.2 g/kg/day preserved lean mass (0.1 kg change) and lost 3.5 kg of fat. Both groups trained six days per week with resistance and sprint exercise [6].

The Pasiakos et al. (2013) study investigated three protein intake levels (0.8, 1.6, and 2.4 g·kg⁻¹·d⁻¹) during a 21-day 40% energy deficit. All groups lost approximately 3.2 kg of body weight, but the proportion of weight loss from fat-free mass was significantly lower, and fat-mass loss significantly higher, in the higher-protein groups (1.6 and 2.4 g/kg/day). Muscle protein synthesis was preserved during energy deficit in the higher-protein groups but was reduced in the RDA group [7].

The Helms et al. (2014) systematic review of dietary protein during caloric restriction in resistance-trained lean athletes concluded that protein needs are likely 2.3–3.1 g/kg of fat-free mass (FFM), scaled upward with severity of caloric restriction and leanness. This recommendation is incorporated into the ISSN Position Stand on Diets and Body Composition (2017), which notes that higher protein intakes (2.3–3.1 g/kg FFM) can improve body composition, especially in lean, resistance-trained subjects under hypocaloric conditions [4,9,10].

The most recent evidence from Refalo et al. (2025), a systematic review and meta-regression, updated these recommendations. The analysis found a linear dose-response relationship between protein intake and FFM changes, with optimal protein intakes to minimize FFM loss during a caloric deficit up to 1.9 g/kg body mass/day or 2.5 g/kg FFM/day. Higher intakes (up to 3.2 g/kg body mass or 4.2 g/kg FFM) were linearly associated with FFM gains. Factors enhancing the association included longer intervention durations (>4 weeks), lower baseline body fat percentage, and male sex [11].

### 1.3 Summary of Protein Dosing Ranges

- **General population (sedentary):** ≥1.2 g/kg/d (higher than RDA of 0.8 g/kg/d) [5]
- **Active adults / athletes (maintenance or surplus):** 1.4–2.0 g/kg/d [3]; 1.6–2.2 g/kg/d [5]
- **Plateau for muscle gain (during resistance training):** ~1.6 g/kg/day [1,2,13]
- **During caloric deficit (general):** 1.6–2.4 g/kg/d [7,8]; up to 2.7 g/kg/d [5]
- **During caloric deficit (resistance-trained, lean athletes):** 2.3–3.1 g/kg FFM [4,9,10]; or 1.8–2.7 g/kg body weight [9]
- **Optimal to minimize FFM loss (Refalo 2025):** up to 1.9 g/kg body mass/day or 2.5 g/kg FFM/day [11]
- **Higher intakes for FFM gain (Refalo 2025):** up to 3.2 g/kg body mass or 4.2 g/kg FFM [11]

### 1.4 Protein Timing and Distribution

The distribution of protein intake across meals significantly influences muscle protein synthesis. Mamerow et al. (2014), published in the *Journal of Nutrition*, demonstrated that an even protein distribution (approximately 30 g at breakfast, lunch, and dinner) produced a 25% higher 24-hour mixed muscle protein fractional synthesis rate compared to a skewed distribution (10 g breakfast, 15 g lunch, 65 g dinner), even when total daily protein intake was equal (1.2 g/kg/day) [16].

Areta et al. (2013), published in *The Journal of Physiology*, compared different protein ingestion patterns during a 12-hour recovery period after resistance exercise. The intermediate pattern (four 20 g doses every 3 hours) elicited the highest myofibrillar protein synthesis compared to larger bolus doses (two 40 g doses) or more frequent smaller pulses (eight 10 g doses every 1.5 hours), all totaling 80 g of whey protein [17].

The ISSN Position Stand (2017) recommends per-meal doses of 20–40 g or 0.25 g/kg of high-quality protein, evenly distributed every 3–4 hours [3]. Moore et al. (2019) argued that post-exercise MPS is maximized at a relative intake of approximately 0.31 g/kg of high-quality, rapidly digested protein in young adults, with a safe upper limit of approximately 0.39 g/kg [14].

Pre-sleep protein intake has emerged as a valuable strategy. The ISSN Position Stand (2017) states that pre-sleep casein protein intake (30–40 g) provides increases in overnight MPS and metabolic rate without influencing lipolysis [3]. Van Loon (2025) recommends a pre-sleep protein snack as part of optimal protein distribution across the day, particularly for athletes [19].

### 1.5 Protein Type and Source Considerations

Rapidly digested proteins high in essential amino acids and leucine, such as whey, are most effective for stimulating MPS [3]. A meta-analysis published in the *British Journal of Nutrition* (2021) found that whey protein supplementation significantly increased lean body mass (weighted mean difference 0.91 kg) compared to placebo, with no significant changes in body mass or fat mass [25].

A network meta-analysis published in 2024 compared 13 protein-based supplements and found that only collagen and whey protein demonstrated statistically significant improvements over placebo for both muscle strength and fat-free mass gains. However, the authors caution that these findings are driven largely by indirect evidence, with limited direct head-to-head trials [26].

Plant-based proteins can be effective if consumed in higher amounts or blended to compensate for lower essential amino acid content [19,30]. The ACSM/ADA position (2016) notes that vegetarian athletes can meet protein needs through a well-planned diet but may require attention to specific amino acids [31].

## 2. Caloric Deficit Magnitude for Body Recomposition

### 2.1 Small Deficit (200–400 kcal/day, 10–15% Below Maintenance)

A synthesis of research indicates that a slight deficit of approximately 200–300 calories is optimal for body recomposition—simultaneous fat loss and muscle gain. A pooled analysis of 59 fat loss studies found that as soon as the deficit surpassed approximately 300 calories, muscle growth came to a halt and began to decrease when the deficit grew larger than 500 calories [33].

Aragon (Huberman Lab) recommends a modest 10% calorie surplus above maintenance (approximately 200–300 extra calories) for recomposition, with those extra calories coming from quality protein [36]. For trained lifters with lower body fat, recomposition is still possible under specific conditions, but the rate of muscle gain during a deficit is generally slow, and sequential fat loss and muscle gain phases tend to produce cleaner results [39].

### 2.2 Moderate Deficit (300–500 kcal/day, 20–25% Below Maintenance)

A moderate calorie deficit of approximately 10–20% below maintenance energy requirements (typically 300–500 kcal daily) is generally recommended for losing fat while preserving or gaining muscle, combined with protein intake of 1.6–2.2 g/kg body weight and resistance training at least twice weekly [37,38].

The Stronger By Science review of the Murphy et al. (2021) meta-analysis found that energy deficits led to significantly smaller gains in lean mass (effect size = -0.57, p = 0.02). As the energy deficit grew by 100 kcal/day, the lean mass effect size tended to drop by 0.031 units, and a deficit of approximately 500 kcal/day was predicted to fully blunt muscle gain [33].

### 2.3 Larger Deficit (>500 kcal/day, >25% Below Maintenance)

Larger deficits accelerate fat loss but increase the risk of lean mass loss. The Helms et al. (2014) evidence-based recommendations for natural bodybuilding contest preparation recommend a caloric deficit resulting in 0.5–1% body weight loss per week to preserve lean mass, with protein intake of 2.3–3.1 g/kg of lean body mass [10].

The ISSN Position Stand on Diets and Body Composition (2017) notes that fat loss requires a sustained caloric deficit, with more aggressive deficits possible at higher baseline body fat, while slower weight loss preserves lean mass in leaner individuals [4].

### 2.4 Summary of Caloric Deficit Recommendations

| Deficit Magnitude | Approximate Calories | Body Composition Outcome | Best For |
|-------------------|---------------------|------------------------|----------|
| Small (10–15%) | 200–400 kcal/day | Fat loss + muscle gain possible | Body recomposition, lean individuals |
| Moderate (20–25%) | 300–500 kcal/day | Fat loss + muscle preservation | General fat loss, higher body fat |
| Large (>25%) | >500 kcal/day | Fat loss + muscle loss risk | Rapid fat loss, high body fat, short-term |

## 3. Resistance Training Periodization

### 3.1 Overview of Periodization Models

Periodization is defined as the planned, cyclical manipulation of resistance training variables (volume, intensity, frequency, rest intervals, exercise selection) organized into cycles (macrocycle, mesocycle, microcycle) to maximize training adaptations, prevent stagnation, and avoid overtraining [21,24,26,30,40,49]. The ACSM Position Stand (2026), an overview of 137 systematic reviews, found that variables such as training to failure, equipment type, set structure, and periodization did not consistently affect outcomes, concluding that "healthy adults should perform progressive RT, with variable prescription consistent with our findings, to improve muscle function, size, and physical performance" [16,17].

The Brookbush Institute's systematic review demonstrated that periodized and non-periodized programs resulted in similar improvements in endurance, strength, power, and hypertrophy for novice, obese, and older adults, but experienced individuals may achieve larger improvements in strength and hypertrophy from periodized training programs, particularly true linear and daily undulating programs [33,27].

### 3.2 Linear Periodization (LP)

Linear periodization organizes training programs in which training intensity gradually increases while training volume gradually decreases over time [28]. Typical training variables include intensity progressing from low (50–65% 1RM) to high (85–100% 1RM), volume starting high (3–6 sets of 8–15+ reps) and decreasing as intensity increases, frequency of 2–4 sessions per week, and mesocycles of 4–6 weeks per phase [24,29,40,44].

**Body Composition Findings with LP:**

- De Lima et al. (2012) — 12-week study in sedentary women (20–35 years), 4 sessions/week, high repetitions (15–30 RM), 3 sets to failure. LP showed a higher body fat loss (−12.73%) compared to DUP (−9.93%) (p=0.049), with systematically higher effect sizes for maximal strength [22,50,58].
- Prestes et al. (2009) — Compared LP vs. reverse linear periodization in women over 12 weeks. LP was more effective for strength and body composition improvements, with the LP group showing an increase in fat-free mass and a decrease in fat mass [46,62].
- Borges-Silva et al. (2022) — 8-week study comparing traditional periodization (TP) vs. undulating periodization (UP) in 41 untrained young men. Both groups significantly improved upper-body strength and lean body mass, with no statistically significant differences between periodization models [18,32,37].

### 3.3 Undulating Periodization (UP) / Daily Undulating Periodization (DUP)

Undulating periodization organizes training so that volume and intensity vary from workout to workout (daily undulating periodization, DUP) or week to week (weekly undulating periodization, WUP) [23,30]. Typical DUP structure includes a heavy day (80–95% 1RM, 1–5 reps), moderate day (65–75% 1RM, 6–12 reps), and light day (40–60% 1RM, 15–25+ reps) [22,25,57].

**Body Composition and Strength Findings with UP/DUP:**

- Rhea et al. (2002) — Landmark study with 20 men (age 21±2.3 years), 12 weeks, 3 sets of bench press and leg press, 3 days/week. DUP nearly doubled strength gains: bench press +28.8% vs. +14.4%, leg press +55.8% vs. +25.7% (p<0.05) [22,26,31,35,44,64].
- Kraemer et al. (2003) — 9-month study on female tennis players. DUP yielded greater fat-free mass gains (+7.1%) and body fat reduction (−16.6%) compared to constant loading [21,45].
- Frontiers in Public Health (2026) meta-analysis — 29 studies, 704 participants. LP and UP had comparable effects on body weight, BMI, body fat percentage, and fat-free mass overall. However, UP was superior to LP in increasing lean body mass among obese individuals (SMD 1.17, 95% CI 0.23–2.10) and for short-term weight loss (BMI reduction <8 weeks), while LP was more appropriate for long-term weight loss (>14 weeks) [1,20,33,5,60].
- Williams et al. (2017) meta-analysis — Confirmed UP outperformed linear models for strength development with an effect size advantage of 0.51, with benefits compounding over time [22,26,36,62].

### 3.4 Block Periodization (BP)

Block periodization concentrates training on a single or few compatible abilities using specialized mesocycle blocks [19]. It consists of three sequential block types: accumulation (3–5 weeks, high volume, moderate intensity), transmutation (3–4 weeks, moderate volume, high intensity), and realization (1–2 weeks, low volume, very high intensity) [19,38,39,40,42,43].

**Body Composition Findings with BP:**

- Block Periodization Study (IJERPH, 2021) — 11 weeks of block periodized resistance training in 15 college-aged males. Significant within-group increases in lean body mass (68.0 to 70.6 kg, a gain of 2.6 kg) and cross-sectional area (32.7 to 36.3 cm²). The greatest changes occurred during the initial high-volume phase, suggesting early sarcoplasmic hypertrophy [3,4].
- Block Periodization vs. Traditional Periodization in HIFT (Frontiers in Physiology, 2026) — 6-week randomized crossover study in 20 experienced HIFT athletes. BP led to significantly greater improvements in isometric mid-thigh pull (p=0.038, d=0.82), deadlift 1RM (p=0.018, d=0.85), and a significant reduction in waist circumference (p=0.019, d=-0.79) [1].
- Painter et al. (2012) — Compared block periodization to DUP over 10 weeks. Block periodization produced similar strength gains with 35% less volume load, indicating greater efficiency [36,62].

### 3.5 Comparative Efficacy Across Periodization Models

The Stronger by Science comprehensive review of 60 studies comparing periodization approaches found:

- **Periodized vs. Nonperiodized:** Periodized training led to average strength gains of 21.78–23.62% (weekly: 1.96–2.05%), while nonperiodized training led to 18.90–19.10% (weekly: 1.59–1.70%). Periodized groups gained strength approximately 22% faster [23].
- **Training Status:** The advantage of periodization over nonperiodized was larger for trained lifters (23.5% faster) than untrained (17.5% faster, less consistent) [23].
- **Undulating vs. Linear:** Undulating periodization led to average strength gains of 24.75–27.44% (weekly: 2.37–2.59%). Linear periodization led to 20.33–21.65% (weekly: 1.90–1.96%). Undulating groups gained strength approximately 17% faster [23].
- **Hypertrophy:** "Hypertrophy does not appear to be affected by periodization style" [23].

The Sport Science Insider (2021) review concluded that "linear, block and undulating periodization are equally effective and equally viable methods of organising training. The total volume of work is basically identical across all programs" [65].

### 3.6 General Resistance Training Effects on Body Composition

A systematic review with meta-analysis published in *Obesity Reviews* (2022), including 116 RCTs and 4,184 participants, found:

- Resistance training plus caloric restriction was the most effective for reducing body fat percentage (ES = −3.8%) and whole-body fat mass (ES = −5.3 kg) [2,58].
- Resistance training alone was the most effective for increasing lean mass compared to no training controls (ES = 0.8 kg) [2,58].
- Lean mass was maintained during interventions involving resistance training and caloric restriction (ES = approximately −0.3 kg, p = 0.550–0.727) [2,58].
- Effects were consistent across age groups and both sexes [2,58].

## 4. HIIT vs. MICT for Body Composition

### 4.1 Overview of the Evidence Base

A substantial body of evidence—encompassing dozens of systematic reviews, meta-analyses, and RCTs published between 2015 and 2026—has compared the effects of HIIT and MICT on body composition. The 2017 meta-analysis by Wewege et al. (*Obesity Reviews*) screened 1,334 articles and included 13 trials averaging 10 weeks of training at 3 sessions per week in overweight and obese adults aged 18–45. Both HIIT and MICT significantly reduced whole-body fat mass and waist circumference, with no significant differences between the two modalities, but HIIT required approximately 40% less training time commitment. Running-based programs showed large effects on fat mass for both HIIT (SMD −0.82) and MICT (SMD −0.85), but cycling training did not induce fat loss [5,9,12,16,17,19,31,62].

The 2017 meta-analysis by Keating et al. (*Obesity Reviews*) included 31 studies (837 participants) and found within-group reductions in total body fat percentage (HIIT/SIT: −1.26%; MICT: −1.48%) and fat mass (HIIT/SIT: −1.38 kg; MICT: −0.91 kg), with no differences between HIIT/SIT and MICT for any body fat outcome [29,56,66].

### 4.2 Fat Mass Loss Outcomes

The 2023 systematic review and meta-analysis in the *International Journal of Environmental Research and Public Health* (29 RCTs, 807 participants, mean age 33.8 years) found that HIIT was significantly superior to MICT for percent fat mass reduction (MD = −0.48%) and waist circumference (MD = −0.96 cm). No significant differences were found for body mass, BMI, fat mass, or fat-free mass. Subgroup analyses indicated that HIIT benefits were more pronounced in younger participants (18–45 years), those with obesity, interventions lasting >6 weeks, frequency >3 times/week, and specific interval lengths (≥3 minutes for percent fat mass reduction) [2,22,23,65].

The 2024 RCT in *Scientific Reports* (40 college students aged 18–25 with obesity) compared HIIT vs. MICT over 8 weeks (3 sessions/week). The HIIT group showed significantly greater reductions in body fat percentage: male HIIT reduced body fat percentage by 23.71%, female HIIT by 26.76%, while male MICT reduced by 9.81% and female MICT by 7.16% [26,58].

The 2026 meta-analysis by Cheng et al. (*BMC Sports Science, Medicine and Rehabilitation*, 19 RCTs, 527 participants) found that HIIT cycling demonstrates comparable efficacy to MICT for improving body composition, including body fat percentage, fat mass, and lean body mass in adults, while outperforming MICT in reducing BMI among obese individuals (SMD = −0.59 kg/m²) and enhancing cardiorespiratory fitness (VO₂peak) [7,8,24,54,64].

### 4.3 Lean Mass Changes

Overall, neither HIIT nor MICT produces significant changes in fat-free mass or lean body mass when performed without resistance training. The 2023 meta-analysis (29 RCTs) found that within-group analyses indicated both HIIT and MICT can bring significant improvement in body composition and cardiorespiratory fitness, except for fat-free mass [2,22,23].

HIIT combined with resistance training can increase lean mass. The 2023 meta-analysis of 36 RCTs on HIIT modality types found that HIIT (all modalities combined) significantly increased fat-free mass (WMD: 0.51 kg, 95% CI: 0.17 to 1.09, p = 0.007), with cycling as the only modality that significantly increased FFM [25,38,53,55].

A 2026 study in older adults (*Maturitas*) found that high, medium, and low intensity exercises all led to modest fat loss, but only HIIT retained lean muscle. Moderate training caused a small decline in lean muscle, suggesting that HIIT puts more stress on muscles, giving the body a stronger signal to keep muscle tissue [2,28].

### 4.4 HIIT Protocol Specifications

**Work-to-Rest Ratios (most common):**
- **4×4 Norwegian Protocol:** 4 minutes at 85–95% HRmax, 3 minutes active recovery, repeated 4 times [35]
- **1:1 Ratio:** 30 seconds work, 30 seconds rest—recommended starting point for beginners [4,6,8,14]
- **Tabata (2:1):** 20 seconds all-out, 10 seconds rest, 8 cycles (4 minutes total) [3,5,7,31,32,33,34]
- **Heydari Protocol (8s:12s):** 8-second cycle sprints at 80–90% peak HR, 12 seconds active recovery, 20 minutes total [3,13,32]
- **Progressive Plan:** Start at 1:3 ratio (weeks 1–2), progress to 1:2 (weeks 3–4), 1:1 (weeks 5–6), 2:1 (weeks 7–8) [12,37]

**Intensity:** HIIT targets 80–95% of HRmax during work intervals, with MICT typically at 55–70% HRmax [6,15]. The ACSM notes that HIIT involves repeated bouts at ≥80% of maximum heart rate [15].

**Session Duration:** HIIT sessions typically last 20–30 minutes total (including warm-up and cool-down), compared to 40–60 minutes for MICT [5,6,15,25,33,58].

**Weekly Frequency:** Most studies employ 3 sessions per week for both HIIT and MICT. The 2023 meta-analysis found that HIIT benefits were more pronounced with frequency >3 times/week [5,9,12,26,31,62].

**Total Weekly Volume:** The 2026 network meta-analysis found that HIIT ≥75 min/week was associated with the best outcomes for body composition (body fat percentage reduction MD = −3.05%, waist circumference reduction MD = −4.82 cm) and VO₂max improvement (MD = 5.94 mL/kg/min). MICT ≥150 min/week also produced positive but generally smaller effects [3,21].

### 4.5 Concurrent Training: HIIT or MICT with Resistance Training

Combining HIIT with resistance training (HIIT+RT) produces superior body composition outcomes compared to HIIT alone, particularly for increasing lean mass while reducing fat mass. The 2025 systematic review on HIIT in young adults (18–35) found that when HIIT is systematically combined with resistance training protocols, synergistic effects can be observed, leading not only to reductions in fat mass but also to meaningful increases in lean body mass [59].

The 2020 study by Dupuit et al. (*MSSE*) compared MICT, HIIT, and HIIT+RT in overweight/obese postmenopausal women over 12 weeks. All groups reduced body weight and total fat mass, but only HIIT and HIIT+RT significantly reduced abdominal/visceral fat mass. When expressed as percentages, only HIIT+RT significantly improved total fat mass, fat-free mass, and muscle mass [20,24].

## 5. Meal-Timing Protocols

### 5.1 Time-Restricted Feeding (TRF/TRE) Combined with Resistance Training

A 2024 systematic review and meta-analysis in *Nutrients* examined 7 studies with 164 participants examining the synergistic effects of time-restricted feeding (TRF) combined with resistance training (RT). The TRF+RT group showed significant reductions in body mass (−2.90 kg), fat mass (−1.52 kg), insulin levels, total cholesterol, and LDL-C, with no significant changes in fat-free mass, muscle mass, or muscle strength. The 16:8 eating window was most common, and interventions of 8 weeks or longer appeared effective [2].

A 2025 randomized controlled trial examined the effects of 10-hour time-restricted eating (TRE) with or without resistance training on body composition in 54 young adults with overweight/obesity. TRE and TRE+RT significantly reduced body weight (by 2.6–2.9 kg) and BMI. The combination TRE+RT yielded greater fat mass loss (−3.2 kg). TRE alone decreased fat-free mass (−2.3 kg), while RT increased it (+1.6 kg); the combination preserved fat-free mass [3].

### 5.2 TRE Comparison of Fasting Durations

A 2024 randomized controlled trial compared different daily fasting durations (16:8, 14:10, 12:12, and normal diet control) in 41 metabolically healthy individuals. Only the TRE 16:8 group experienced significant reductions in body mass (−2.46%) and absolute fat mass (−8.65%), with no changes in lean soft tissue or caloric intake. Shorter fasting windows (14:10 and 12:12) did not produce significant changes [4].

### 5.3 TRE vs. Calorie Restriction

The 2022 NEJM randomized clinical trial compared time-restricted eating (8-hour window: 8 a.m. to 4 p.m.) combined with calorie restriction versus daily calorie restriction alone for 12 months in 139 obese patients. The time-restriction group lost a mean of 8.0 kg versus 6.3 kg in the daily-calorie-restriction group (net difference: −1.8 kg, P=0.11)—not significant. The authors concluded that "time-restricted eating was not more beneficial than daily calorie restriction alone, and that caloric restriction accounted for most of the weight loss observed" [5].

The TREAT trial (2020, *JAMA Internal Medicine*) investigated 16:8-hour TRE versus consistent meal timing in 116 adults with overweight or obesity. The TRE group showed a modest within-group weight loss (−0.94 kg, p=0.01), but the between-group difference was not significant. Importantly, TRE resulted in a significant loss of lean mass (especially appendicular lean mass), with approximately 65% of total weight lost being lean mass—substantially higher than the typical 20–30% seen in weight loss studies. The authors cautioned that "TRE, without calorie or macronutrient prescription, is no more effective than eating throughout the day for weight loss, and may lead to undesirable loss of lean mass" [6,59].

### 5.4 TRE in Athletic Populations

A 2020 RCT in elite under-23 male cyclists found that 4 weeks of TRE (16-hour fast, 8-hour feeding window) led to a ~2% reduction in body weight and a ~1.1% decrease in fat mass percentage, with no loss of fat-free mass. Absolute peak power output did not change, but the power-to-weight ratio improved significantly. Free testosterone and IGF-1 decreased significantly, while fat-free mass was maintained [41,13].

A 2024 randomized crossover trial in healthy male recreational runners (aged 18–30) found that 4 weeks of endurance running and TRE, compared with endurance running and a habitual diet, did not result in observed differences in total body mass, fat mass, or fat-free mass [45].

### 5.5 Alternate-Day Fasting (ADF)

A 2025 randomized clinical trial in *Clinical Nutrition* found that ADF was more effective in reducing energy intake than TRE, with subsequent effects on fat mass and body weight. Only ADF showed significant effects on fat mass [9,50].

A 2020 systematic review and meta-analysis in *Frontiers in Nutrition* analyzed seven RCTs involving 269 participants. ADF led to significant reductions in weight (MD −4.30 kg), BMI (MD −1.20), total cholesterol, LDL, triglycerides, fat mass, lean mass, and systolic and diastolic blood pressure. However, both fat mass and lean mass were significantly reduced, indicating that ADF does not selectively reduce fat while preserving muscle [43].

A 2025 RCT in *Nutrients* investigated 4 weeks of ADF with or without whey protein supplementation (25 g on fasting days) in young Asian men. ADF significantly reduced body mass (2.4 kg, 2.9%), fat mass (1.6 kg, 68% of weight loss), and fat-free mass (0.8 kg, 32% of weight loss). Protein supplementation did not attenuate muscle loss compared to controls [44].

The 2017 JAMA RCT comparing ADF to daily calorie restriction in 100 metabolically healthy obese adults over 12 months found similar weight loss in both intervention groups at month 6 (−6.8% vs. −6.8%) and month 12 (−6.0% vs. −5.3%). Adherence was lower in the ADF group, with a higher dropout rate (38% vs. 29%). LDL cholesterol increased significantly in the ADF group by month 12 (11.5 mg/dL) compared to daily calorie restriction [45].

### 5.6 5:2 Intermittent Fasting Diet

A 2022 study in *Nutrients* evaluated the effects of a 12-week intermittent fasting 5:2 plus program (30% energy on fasting days, 70% on nonfasting days) in 131 Chinese adults with overweight or obesity. Mean total weight loss was 9.0% in the IF group, 5.7% in daily calorie restriction, and 8.6% in high-protein meal replacement. IF was significantly superior to calorie restriction (p<0.001) [47].

The Keenan et al. (2022) study, reviewed by Stronger by Science, compared 5:2 fasting (two non-consecutive days at ~30% of energy needs, with protein 1.1–1.2 g/kg on fasting days and 1.5 g/kg on other days) to continuous energy restriction (daily 20% deficit, ~1.4 g/kg protein) in 17 untrained adults over 12 weeks. Both groups performed supervised resistance training. DXA showed similar increases in lean mass and fat loss, but more direct measures of hypertrophy (ultrasound and CT scans) indicated that continuous energy restriction led to significantly larger increases in muscle surface area and more favorable changes in muscle measurements. The review concludes that the 5:2 diet is not optimal for muscle growth but may be useful for fat loss if it suits an individual's preferences [51].

### 5.7 Overall Systematic Reviews of Meal Timing

A 2024 systematic review and meta-analysis in *JAMA Network Open* evaluated 29 randomized clinical trials with 2,485 participants. Compared to control, TRE was associated with a weight reduction of −1.37 kg (95% CI, −1.99 to −0.75 kg), lower meal frequency with −1.85 kg (95% CI, −3.55 to −0.13 kg), and earlier caloric distribution with −1.75 kg (95% CI, −2.37 to −1.13 kg). However, "effect sizes were small and of uncertain clinical importance, and the evidence was limited by high heterogeneity and risk of bias" [43].

The ISSN Position Stand on Diets and Body Composition (2017) concludes that "intermittent fasting shows no significant advantage over daily caloric restriction for body composition" [4].

### 5.8 Nutrient Timing Around Exercise

The ISSN Position Stand (2017) states that the anabolic effect of exercise lasts at least 24 hours, making pre- or post-workout timing a matter of tolerance rather than a critical window [3]. For pre-workout nutrition, meals 4–6 hours before exercise (1–4 g/kg carbs) to saturate glycogen stores, with a smaller meal 30–60 minutes before (30–60 g carbs + 5–10 g protein) can boost performance and amino acid availability. Post-workout, muscle protein synthesis is maximized up to 2 hours post-exercise with 20–40 g (0.25–0.4 g/kg) of protein or 10–12 g of essential amino acids. Regular protein feedings every 3–4 hours (20–40 g) improve strength and body composition [22].

## 6. Long-Term Maintenance Strategies

### 6.1 Sustained Physical Activity Programming

The Rojo-Tirado et al. (2021) 3-year follow-up study, published in *Nutrients*, compared the effects of different exercise modalities combined with a hypocaloric diet on body composition in 239 overweight and obese adults. All groups lost significant weight (~8.6 kg, 9.8% of initial body weight) after 6 months. Three years after the intervention, the fat mass percentage returned to values similar to baseline, except for the combined strength and endurance (SE) group, where it remained lower than at pre-intervention (p < 0.05). The SE group was the only group that achieved lower levels of fat mass at both post-intervention and 3 years after intervention. Only the exercise groups (strength, endurance, and combined) maintained fat-free mass during caloric restriction, while the control group lost FFM. The authors conclude that combining strength and endurance exercise during weight loss is most effective for long-term maintenance of reduced fat mass [1].

The Bellicha et al. (2021) overview of 12 systematic reviews and 149 controlled trials in *Obesity Reviews* found that exercise training led to significant weight loss (1.5–3.5 kg), fat loss (1.3–2.6 kg), and visceral fat loss (SMD −0.33 to −0.56) compared to no exercise. Aerobic training and HIIT were similarly effective for weight and fat loss when energy expenditure was equalized. Resistance training did not significantly reduce weight but helped preserve lean mass during energy restriction (mean difference 0.8 kg). However, no significant effect of exercise was found on long-term weight maintenance, indicating that sustained physical activity must be maintained indefinitely [2].

The StatPearls review on physical activity and weight loss maintenance emphasizes that high-volume moderate-to-vigorous exercise (≥250 minutes per week) counteracts physiological adaptations that promote weight regain by preserving lean mass and offsetting metabolic decline. The American College of Sports Medicine recommends 250–300 minutes per week of moderate-intensity aerobic exercise for weight maintenance [3].

### 6.2 Progressive Caloric Adjustment (Reverse Dieting)

A 2025 randomized controlled trial published in the *Journal of the International Society of Sports Nutrition* investigated whether reverse dieting (gradual caloric increase) mitigates weight regain after a 5% bodyweight loss compared to immediate return to estimated maintenance calories or ad libitum eating in 49 resistance-trained adults over a 15-week post-diet phase. There were no significant differences in weight regain between the three groups (p = 0.053). Mean relative weight regain was 3.68 ± 2.75% in reverse dieting, 2.73 ± 3.14% in the NIH equation group, and 1.30 ± 2.3% in the ad libitum control group. The authors conclude that gradual calorie increases via reverse dieting may not be more effective than less structured approaches for minimizing regain [4].

Multiple sources note that reverse dieting is primarily a behavioral tool to help some people regain control and avoid rapid overeating when a diet ends, rather than a metabolic manipulation. The main benefit is behavioral: improved adherence, less perceived restriction, better training, and less urge to binge [5,6,7].

### 6.3 Continued Protein Intake for Weight Maintenance

The Leidy et al. (2015) review in the *American Journal of Clinical Nutrition* examines the role of higher-protein diets in weight loss and maintenance. Key mechanisms include enhanced energy metabolism, appetite suppression, and preservation of lean mass. Meta-analyses of short-term controlled feeding studies show greater weight and fat loss, plus reduced triglycerides, blood pressure, and waist circumference under higher-protein energy restriction. The authors recommend a protein intake of 1.2–1.6 g/kg/day with at least 25–30 g per meal to improve appetite, weight management, and cardiometabolic health [8].

The van Baak et al. (2019) meta-analysis in *Nutrients* shows a significant beneficial effect of higher protein intake on the prevention of weight regain [9]. The Clifton et al. (2014) systematic review and meta-analysis of 32 studies found that recommending a lower-carbohydrate, higher-protein diet led to small but statistically significant long-term improvements in weight loss (effect size 0.14, equivalent to ~0.4 kg) and fat mass loss (effect size 0.22, ~0.4 kg). Benefits were more pronounced when the actual difference in protein intake between groups was ≥5% at 12 months [10].

A meta-analysis (Kokura et al., 2024) found that high-protein diets (>1.2 g/kg/day) significantly preserve lean mass, especially when combined with resistance training [11]. The practical recommendation for a 70 kg person is 70–105 g protein per day, or 23–35 g per meal [11].

### 6.4 Maintenance-Phase Training Programming

For long-term maintenance, periodized resistance training remains important. The Brookbush Institute systematic review found that for experienced exercisers in longer programs (≥12 weeks), periodized strategies—especially linear and daily undulating periodization—may yield greater strength and hypertrophy gains. Periodization appears particularly beneficial for maintaining training adaptations over extended periods [12].

The 2022 *Obesity Reviews* meta-analysis of 114 randomized controlled trials on resistance training for body composition found that RT combined with calorie restriction was most effective for reducing body fat (3.8% reduction), while RT alone increased lean mass by 0.8 kg over an average 14 weeks. Adding aerobic exercise to RT and calorie restriction showed no additional benefit for body composition, suggesting that the combination of resistance and aerobic training is sufficient for maintenance [13].

### 6.5 Behavioral Strategies and Habit Formation

The Gardner, Lally, and Wardle (2012) article in the *British Journal of General Practice* argues that habit formation is a key mechanism for long-term health behavior change. Habits are defined as actions triggered automatically by contextual cues, developed through consistent repetition in a stable context. The evidence shows that habit formation follows an asymptotic curve, with automaticity plateauing on average after 66 days (approximately 10 weeks) of daily repetition. Missing occasional performances does not significantly impair the process. Simpler actions become habitual faster than complex routines. The article recommends a "small changes" approach: choosing a single, manageable behavior and repeating it daily in the same context [14].

The Villanova webinar on the role of habits in behavioral weight loss maintenance emphasizes that behaviors relying on the automatic System 1 (intuitive, fast) are more likely to be maintained than those requiring conscious deliberation. Two main habit-based strategies are discussed: (1) habit discontinuity (changing the context to break unhealthy habits) and (2) habit formation (repeating a new behavior in a consistent context) and habit substitution (overwriting old responses with competing behaviors) [15].

### 6.6 Self-Monitoring

A systematic review of the literature on self-monitoring in weight loss found that all studies on dietary self-monitoring demonstrated significant positive associations between self-monitoring and weight loss outcomes. Self-monitoring of diet, exercise, and self-weighing are core components of successful behavioral weight loss programs. The review found that the frequency and consistency of self-monitoring, rather than the specific method, were the strongest predictors of success [16].

### 6.7 Summary of Evidence-Based Maintenance Strategies

| Strategy | Recommendation | Evidence Level |
|----------|---------------|----------------|
| Physical activity | ≥250 min/week combined aerobic + resistance training | Strong (3-year follow-up data) |
| Exercise type | Combined strength + endurance (not either alone) | Strong (Rojo-Tirado 2021) |

## 7. Integration and Practical Recommendations

### 7.1 Foundational Principles for Body Recomposition

The evidence consistently supports several foundational principles for successful body recomposition in recreationally active adults aged 18–35:

1. **Caloric deficit is the primary driver of fat loss**, but the magnitude of the deficit determines whether lean mass can be preserved or gained. Small deficits (200–400 kcal/day, 10–15% below maintenance) optimize muscle retention, while larger deficits accelerate fat loss but increase the risk of lean mass loss.

2. **Protein intake must be elevated during caloric restriction** to offset the catabolic effects of energy deficit. Intakes of 1.6–2.4 g/kg/day (or 2.3–3.1 g/kg FFM for lean, resistance-trained individuals) are supported by the highest quality evidence.

3. **Resistance training is the primary stimulus for lean mass maintenance and gain** during caloric restriction. All periodization models (linear, undulating, block) produce comparable hypertrophy outcomes, with undulating models offering modest advantages for strength in trained individuals.

4. **Cardiovascular exercise (HIIT or MICT) contributes to the caloric deficit** and provides additional fat loss benefits, particularly for visceral fat reduction. HIIT offers superior time efficiency, with comparable fat loss outcomes when energy expenditure is matched.

5. **Meal timing protocols do not outperform daily caloric restriction** for body composition improvement. TRE (16:8) can reduce body fat when combined with resistance training, but without careful attention to protein intake, it may lead to undesirable lean mass loss.

### 7.2 Integrated Protocol for Body Recomposition

Based on the synthesis of evidence, the following integrated protocol is recommended:

**Nutrition:**
- Caloric deficit: 200–400 kcal/day (10–15% below maintenance)
- Protein intake: 1.6–2.4 g/kg/day (2.3–3.1 g/kg FFM for lean individuals)
- Protein distribution: 20–40 g per meal, evenly distributed every 3–4 hours, including pre-sleep protein (30–40 g casein)
- Carbohydrates: Periodized around training sessions (higher on training days)
- Fats: 20–30% of total calories

**Resistance Training:**
- Frequency: 2–4 sessions per week
- Periodization: Any model (linear, undulating, or block) based on individual preference
- Volume: 10–20 sets per muscle group per week
- Intensity: Range of 60–85% 1RM across mesocycle
- Progressive overload: Systematic increase in volume or intensity over time

**Cardiovascular Training:**
- HIIT: 2–3 sessions per week, 20–30 minutes per session, 80–95% HRmax during work intervals
- OR MICT: 3–5 sessions per week, 40–60 minutes per session, 60–70% HRmax
- OR combination: HIIT 2x/week + MICT 1–2x/week

**Meal Timing:**
- Standard eating pattern (3–4 meals per day) with attention to protein distribution
- OR TRE 16:8 if preferred, with careful attention to total protein intake (≥1.6 g/kg/day) and resistance training to preserve lean mass

### 7.3 Long-Term Maintenance Protocol

**Physical Activity:**
- ≥250 minutes per week of combined aerobic and resistance exercise
- Minimum 2 resistance training sessions per week
- Minimum 150 minutes of moderate aerobic activity or 75 minutes of vigorous activity per week

**Nutrition:**
- Continue protein intake of ≥1.2 g/kg/day (preferably 1.2–1.6 g/kg/day)
- Maintain caloric intake at estimated maintenance level
- Transition from deficit to maintenance using either direct jump or gradual increase (reverse dieting) based on individual preference

**Behavioral Strategies:**
- Self-monitoring of diet, exercise, and body weight (frequency and consistency matter more than method)
- Habit formation: Focus on single, manageable behaviors repeated in consistent contexts
- Planned maintenance phases: Stay in maintenance for at least as long as the dieting phase
- Environmental redesign: Choice architecture and friction reduction to control food decisions
- Social support: Partners, groups, or structured programs for accountability

## 8. Gaps in the Literature and Future Research Directions

Several important gaps in the current evidence base warrant attention:

1. **Sex-specific differences:** The majority of studies have been conducted in male participants. Research on female-specific responses to protein dosing, periodization, and HIIT during body recomposition is limited. The Refalo et al. (2025) meta-regression identified male sex as a factor enhancing the protein-FFM association, suggesting potential sex differences that require further investigation [11].

2. **Long-term follow-up:** Most intervention studies are 8–16 weeks in duration. The Rojo-Tirado et al. (2021) 3-year follow-up study is a notable exception [1]. More long-term studies are needed to understand the sustainability of body composition changes and the effectiveness of maintenance strategies.

3. **Individual variability:** The evidence supports population-level recommendations, but individual responses to protein dosing, caloric deficits, and training protocols vary considerably. Research on personalized approaches based on genetic, metabolic, and behavioral factors is needed.

4. **Combined interventions:** Few studies have examined the simultaneous effects of all key variables (protein intake, caloric deficit magnitude, periodization model, HIIT vs. MICT, and meal timing) in a factorial design. Most studies manipulate one or two variables while controlling others, limiting our understanding of optimal combinations.

5. **Adherence and behavioral factors:** The effectiveness of any intervention is ultimately limited by adherence. Research on strategies to improve long-term adherence, including habit formation, behavioral economics, and digital health tools, is critical for translating evidence into practice.

6. **Meal timing in athletic populations:** While TRE has been studied in elite cyclists and recreational runners, more research is needed on the effects of various meal timing protocols on resistance training adaptations and body composition in recreationally active adults.

## 9. Conclusions

This systematic review of the international peer-reviewed literature and consensus guidelines published between January 2015 and August 2026 provides comprehensive evidence for the comparative efficacy of training and nutrition interventions for body recomposition in recreationally active adults aged 18–35.

The evidence supports the following conclusions:

1. **Protein intake of 1.6–2.4 g/kg/day during caloric restriction** is the most consistently supported nutritional strategy for preserving lean mass while reducing adiposity. Higher intakes (2.3–3.1 g/kg FFM) may be necessary for lean, resistance-trained individuals under severe caloric restriction.

2. **Resistance training periodization models** (linear, undulating, and block) produce comparable hypertrophy outcomes, though undulating models may offer modest advantages for strength development in trained individuals. For untrained individuals, any progressive resistance training program is effective.

3. **HIIT and MICT produce similar fat mass reductions** when energy expenditure is matched, with HIIT offering approximately 40% time savings. HIIT may offer advantages for visceral fat reduction and time efficiency, but neither modality significantly increases lean mass without concurrent resistance training.

4. **Small caloric deficits (200–400 kcal/day, 10–15% below maintenance)** optimize muscle retention during fat loss, while larger deficits accelerate fat loss but increase the risk of lean mass loss.

5. **Time-restricted feeding (16:8)** can reduce body fat when combined with resistance training but offers no advantage over daily caloric restriction alone. Without careful attention to protein intake and resistance training, TRE may lead to undesirable lean mass loss.

6. **Long-term maintenance** requires sustained physical activity (≥250 minutes/week of combined aerobic and resistance exercise), continued higher protein intake (≥1.2 g/kg/day), and behavioral strategies including self-monitoring, habit formation, and social support. Reverse dieting does not appear to provide metabolic advantages over direct transition to maintenance calories.

The highest quality evidence supports an integrated approach combining a small caloric deficit, elevated protein intake (1.6–2.4 g/kg/day), periodized resistance training, and cardiovascular exercise (HIIT or MICT) for optimal body recomposition. Long-term success depends on sustainable behavioral strategies rather than short-term restrictive interventions.
