# Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis: Comparative Effectiveness of HIIT, MICT, and Hybrid Protocols in Adults Aged 30–70 with Limited Training Time (≤150 min/week)

## Executive Summary

This comprehensive systematic review synthesizes evidence from randomized controlled trials, meta-analyses, and official guidelines published between 2018 and 2026. The research compares high-intensity interval training (HIIT, including low-volume and sprint-interval variants), moderate-intensity continuous training (MICT), and hybrid HIIT+MICT protocols in adults aged 30–70 with limited weekly training time (≤150 min/week, with a subgroup analysis for ≤60 min/week). Primary outcomes examined are cardiorespiratory fitness (VO₂max/VO₂peak), cardiometabolic markers (HbA1c, fasting glucose, HOMA-IR, blood pressure, lipids, adiposity/liver fat), and patient-centered outcomes (adherence, enjoyment, adverse events). The analysis incorporates supportive context from observational studies and major guidelines (ACSM, WHO), examines progression/maintenance plans over ≥6–12 months, and considers moderators including sex, age band, baseline fitness, cardiometabolic risk, and medication status.

**Key overarching finding:** HIIT consistently produces comparable or superior improvements in cardiorespiratory fitness compared to MICT while requiring 40–60% less weekly training time. For cardiometabolic outcomes, the evidence is more nuanced—both modalities are effective, with HIIT showing advantages for certain outcomes (e.g., VO₂max, post-challenge glucose, HDL-C) and MICT for others (e.g., HbA1c in some populations, visceral fat reduction). Hybrid HIIT+MICT protocols may offer synergistic benefits, particularly for cardiac rehabilitation. Adherence is similar between modalities in supervised settings, though enjoyment may favor HIIT acutely. Adverse events are rare with appropriate screening and supervision. The minimal effective dose for VO₂max improvement appears to be as low as 20 min/week of REHIT or ≤60 min/week of low-volume HIIT.

## 1. Definitions and Protocol Evaluation Methods

### 1.1 High-Intensity Interval Training (HIIT)

HIIT is defined as a training method involving repeated bouts of high-intensity exercise (≥80% HRmax, ≥80% VO₂max, or ≥80% HRR) interspersed with periods of rest or active recovery at lower intensity [1][2]. The work intervals range from 10 seconds to 5 minutes, with work-to-rest ratios typically between 1:1 and 1:4.

**Intensity thresholds:**
- ≥80% of maximal heart rate (HRmax) [1]
- 80–95% of peak heart rate [2]
- ≥80% of maximal oxygen uptake (VO₂max) [3]
- ≥80% of heart rate reserve (HRR) [1]
- Rating of Perceived Exertion (RPE) 14–17 on Borg 6–20 scale [4]

**Representative protocols:**
- **4×4 protocol (Norwegian model):** 4 minutes at 85–95% HRmax, 3 minutes active recovery, repeated 4 times [5]
- **10×1 protocol:** 60 seconds at 95% HRmax, 75 seconds recovery, 8–12 rounds [6]
- **Tabata protocol:** 20 seconds work/10 seconds rest, 8 rounds (4 minutes total), at 170% VO₂max [7]

### 1.2 Low-Volume HIIT

Low-volume HIIT is defined as HIIT with total high-intensity exercise time ≤5 minutes per session and/or total weekly time ≤60 minutes [8]. The most extreme variant is Reduced-Exertion High-Intensity Interval Training (REHIT), involving two 20-second all-out sprints within a 10-minute session, performed 2–3 times per week (20–30 min/week total) [9].

**Representative protocols:**
- **REHIT:** 10-minute sessions with two 20-second all-out sprints, 2–3 sessions/week [9]
- **10-20-30 method:** 30 seconds low, 20 seconds moderate, 10 seconds maximal effort, repeated [6]
- **Low-volume HIIT (≤5 min high-intensity work):** Various protocols with total session duration ≤15 minutes [8]

### 1.3 Sprint Interval Training (SIT)

SIT is defined as "all-out" supramaximal efforts (≥100% VO₂max) typically lasting ≤30 seconds, with extended recovery periods at least 5× the work duration [10]. SIT involves maximal or near-maximal effort that cannot be sustained for longer durations.

**Representative protocols:**
- **Classic Wingate protocol:** 30-second all-out sprints, 4 minutes recovery, 4–6 repetitions [10]
- **REHIT (also classified as SIT):** Two 20-second all-out sprints within 10 minutes [9]

### 1.4 Moderate-Intensity Continuous Training (MICT)

MICT is defined as continuous aerobic exercise performed at moderate intensity (40–59% HRR, 46–63% VO₂max, or 64–76% HRmax) without rest intervals [11]. Sessions typically last 30–60 minutes.

**Intensity thresholds:**
- 40–59% of heart rate reserve (HRR) [11]
- 46–63% of maximal oxygen uptake (VO₂max) [11]
- 64–76% of maximal heart rate (HRmax) [11]
- RPE 11–13 on Borg 6–20 scale [4]

### 1.5 Hybrid HIIT+MICT Protocols

Hybrid protocols combine HIIT and MICT within the same training program, either within individual sessions or across the weekly schedule. The ACE IFT Model uses a personalized approach combining ventilatory threshold-based MICT with one weekly HIIT session [12]. The combined HIIT+MICT approach in cardiac rehabilitation typically involves 3 days/week HIIT plus 2 days/week MICT [13].

### 1.6 Evaluation Methods for Cardiorespiratory Fitness

VO₂max/VO₂peak is typically assessed via graded exercise tests (GXT) using:
- **Treadmill protocols:** Bruce protocol, modified Bruce protocol, or ramp protocols [14]
- **Cycle ergometer protocols:** Ramp protocols with incremental workload increases [14]
- **Primary criteria for VO₂max attainment:** Plateau in oxygen consumption despite increasing workload, RER ≥1.10, HR within 10 bpm of age-predicted maximum, RPE ≥18 [14]

VO₂max is expressed in absolute terms (L/min) or relative to body mass (mL·kg⁻¹·min⁻¹). The latter is the most common metric in training studies.

## 2. Cardiorespiratory Fitness (VO₂max/VO₂peak)

### 2.1 HIIT vs. MICT: Overall Comparison

The evidence consistently demonstrates that HIIT produces superior improvements in VO₂max compared to MICT, with the magnitude of difference ranging from small to moderate depending on population and protocol.

**Poon et al. (2024) umbrella review** [15] synthesized evidence from 24 systematic reviews (429 primary studies, 12,967 participants). HIIT significantly increased VO₂max compared to MICT (SMD: 0.18–0.99; WMD: 0.52–3.76 mL/kg/min). The effect was consistent across multiple populations including healthy adults, overweight/obese individuals, older adults, and athletes.

**The 115-trial meta-analysis (2026)** [16] (3,196 participants, age 8–68 years) found HIIT produced significantly greater improvements than MVICT in relative VO₂max (g=0.39, 95% CI [0.27, 0.51]) and absolute VO₂max (g=0.29, 95% CI [0.15, 0.43]). HIIT was particularly superior for inactive individuals, recreationally active, and elite athletes.

**Wen et al. (2019)** [17] meta-analysis of 53 RCTs found that HIIT significantly improved VO₂max across all populations compared to control (SMD = 0.41–1.81). When compared to MICT, only long-interval (≥2 min), high-volume (≥15 min), and moderate-to-long-term (≥4–12 weeks) HIIT showed beneficial effects (SMD = 0.65–1.07). Short-interval, low-volume, and short-term HIIT also produced clear benefits versus control, confirming time-efficiency.

### 2.2 Low-Volume HIIT and REHIT

**Low-volume HIIT meta-analysis (2023, 21 studies, 849 participants)** [8] found that LV-HIIT (≤5 min high-intensity work within ≤15 min session) significantly improved CRF compared to control (SMD = 1.19 [0.87, 1.50]). Despite requiring only 14–47% of the time of MICT, there were no significant differences between LV-HIIT and MICT for any outcome. Dose–response analysis showed that supramaximal intensity drove greater CRF gains than submaximal, while increasing sprint repetitions or session duration did not augment improvements.

**REHIT (Reduced-Exertion HIIT):** Studies by Metcalfe and Vollaard demonstrate that REHIT (two 20-second all-out sprints within a 10-minute session, 2–3 sessions/week) improves VO₂max by 10–15% after 6 weeks [9][18]. A randomized trial found no significant difference between 2, 3, or 4 sessions/week, suggesting that 2 sessions/week are sufficient [19].

### 2.3 SIT vs. HIIT

**IJERPH meta-analysis (2021, 19 studies)** [20] found no significant difference between HIIT (effort bouts >60 s) and SIT (effort bouts ≤60 s, all-out) for VO₂max improvement (SMD = 0.150; 95% CI = −0.038 to 0.338; p = 0.119). The authors conclude: "HIIT and SIT protocols promote similar gains in cardiorespiratory fitness. Thus, the choice of the protocol can be made for convenience."

**Frontiers in Physiology (2025, 9 RCTs, n=666)** [21] confirmed that both HIIT and SIT significantly improved CRF (SMD = 1.54; 95% CI: 0.89–2.18; p < 0.00001) compared to control. Subgroup analyses revealed HIIT was more effective for CRF in overweight/obese individuals, while SIT was more effective for fat loss in trained populations.

### 2.4 Efficiency: Improvement per Minute of Training Time

A key advantage of HIIT is its efficiency. **Scribbans et al. (2016)** [22] demonstrated that while training intensity alone may not affect the magnitude of VO₂max increase, similar adaptations can be achieved with lower training doses at higher intensities. Low-volume HIIT requires only 14–47% of the time commitment of MICT while producing comparable or superior results [8].

**Cycling-based HIIT meta-analysis (2025, 19 RCTs, 527 participants)** [23] found HIIT significantly improved VO₂peak in sedentary populations (SMD = 6.21 mL·kg⁻¹·min⁻¹, 95% CI: 4.68–7.74, p<0.001) and obese populations (SMD = 0.23 mL·kg⁻¹·min⁻¹, 95% CI: 0.01–0.45, p=0.041).

### 2.5 Optimal Protocols for VO₂max Improvement

**For older adults (≥55 years):** Yang et al. (2023) [24] meta-analysis (30 studies, 1,973 subjects) found HIIT significantly improved VO₂max compared to MICT (WMD = 1.80 mL/kg/min, 95% CI: 1.25–2.35, p<0.001). The most effective HIIT protocol was: training period <12 weeks, frequency 3 times/week, 20 min per session, intensity <80% VO₂peak, ≤60s work intervals, ≤90s rest.

**For elderly (≥60 years):** Chu et al. (2026) [25] meta-analysis (16 RCTs, n=1,434) found HIIT superior to MICT (MD = 1.17, 95% CI: 0.52–1.82). Optimal HIIT protocol: intervention duration >3 to <6 months, 4 sessions/week, 21–39 minutes/session. Optimal MICT: similar duration, 3 sessions/week, ≥60 minutes/session.

**For athletes:** BMC Sports Science network meta-analysis (2025, 51 studies, 1,261 athletes) [26] found optimal HIIT benefits at work duration of 140 seconds and work-to-recovery ratio of 0.85. For SIT, improvements were not significant when recovery exceeded 97 seconds.

## 3. Cardiometabolic Markers

### 3.1 Glycemic Control (HbA1c, Fasting Glucose, HOMA-IR)

**Type 2 Diabetes:** The Frontiers in Endocrinology (2026) meta-analysis [27] (21 RCTs, 831 patients) found both HIIT and MICT significantly reduced FBG and increased VO₂max compared to control. HIIT significantly reduced HbA1c, BMI, and increased HDL, while MICT did not show significant effects on these parameters. Direct comparison revealed HIIT was significantly superior to MICT in lowering FBG (SMD = -0.28) and improving VO₂max (SMD = 1.59).

However, a meta-analysis of 55 studies [28] found MICT was superior to HIIT for HbA1c improvement (d = -0.27, P < 0.05). The Diabetes Research and Clinical Practice (2022) meta-analysis [29] (19 RCTs) found HIIT significantly reduced HbA1c compared to no exercise (−0.34%, P < 0.001), but differences between HIIT and MICT were not statistically significant.

**Prediabetes:** The Frontiers in Endocrinology (May 2026) meta-analysis [30] (13 RCTs) found no significant differences for HbA1c between HIIT and MICT. MICT had a small advantage for fasting glucose (MD 0.12 mmol/L), while HIIT significantly improved 2-hour plasma glucose (MD −0.16 mmol/L). The authors conclude: "HIIT may be more favorable for post-challenge glycemic control and cardiorespiratory fitness, whereas MICT may offer a modest advantage for fasting glucose."

**Metabolic Syndrome:** A 12-week RCT in middle-aged adults (45–65 years) with metabolic syndrome (n=90) [31] found HIIT (4×4-minute intervals at 85–95% HRmax) showed significantly greater reductions in HbA1c (−0.72%) compared to MICT and control. HIIT also showed greater reductions in FBG (−0.68 mmol/L) and TG (>1.0 mmol/L reduction).

**Insulin Resistance (HOMA-IR):** The Diabetes Research and Clinical Practice meta-analysis [29] found HIIT significantly reduced HOMA-IR compared to control (−0.88, P = 0.005), with no significant difference between HIIT and MICT. The F1000Research (May 2026) meta-analysis [32] (4 RCTs, n=162) found no significant differences between HIIT and MICT for HOMA-IR.

### 3.2 Blood Pressure

**Systolic Blood Pressure:** Meta-analyses show mixed results. The meta-analysis of 55 studies [28] found no significant differences between HIIT and MICT. However, Yang et al. (2023) [24] in older adults found HIIT significantly reduced SBP compared to MICT (WMD = −2.59 mmHg, P < 0.04). An 8-week RCT in 60 sedentary adults (aged 25–45) [33] found HIIT yielded significantly greater reductions in SBP (−11.4 ± 3.2 mmHg vs. −6.7 ± 2.9 mmHg) and DBP (−7.2 ± 2.5 mmHg vs. −4.1 ± 2.3 mmHg) compared to MICT.

**Diastolic Blood Pressure:** The PLoS ONE (2025) meta-analysis in CAD patients [34] found HIIT showed a significant increase in DBP compared to MICT (WMD = 3.43 mmHg, 95% CI 1.09–5.76), an unexpected finding requiring further investigation. In overweight/obese adults, a meta-analysis of 13 studies (446 participants) [35] found HIIT caused a significant decrease in DBP (WMD = −3.33 mmHg).

**Hypertension:** A 12-week RCT in 100 adults with hypertension (mean age 54) [36] found combined strength+MICT achieved the largest BP reductions (SBP −13.4 mmHg, DBP −6.8 mmHg), superior to both strength+HIIT and MICT alone.

### 3.3 Lipid Profile

**Total Cholesterol and LDL-C:** The BMJ Open Sport & Exercise Medicine meta-analysis (2019, 26 RCTs, 823 participants) [37] concluded: "Neither HIIT nor MICT is superior for altering TC, TRG, or LDL-C, or TC-HDL-C ratio." Aerobic physical activity volume (~1200 kcal/week) rather than intensity appears to be the key driver of lipid improvements.

**HDL-C:** HIIT may offer a small advantage for raising HDL-C. The BMJ meta-analysis found HIIT produced a statistically significant improvement in HDL-C compared with MICT (mean difference 0.07 mmol/L, P < 0.0001), though this result was sensitive to removal of one study [37].

**Triglycerides:** Results are mixed. The metabolic syndrome RCT [31] found HIIT reduced TG by >1.0 mmol/L, significantly greater than MICT. However, in prediabetes [38] and animal models, MICT was superior for TG reduction.

### 3.4 Adiposity and Body Composition

**Body Fat Percentage:** The network meta-analysis (2026, 18 RCTs, 952 overweight/obese adults) [39] found HIIT ≥75 min/week was associated with significant reductions in BFP (MD = −3.05%, 95% CrI: −4.69 to −1.42), ranking first by SUCRA (84.74%). A meta-analysis of 31 studies (837 participants) [40] found both HIIT/SIT and MICT significantly reduced body fat percentage (HIIT/SIT: −1.26%; MICT: −1.48%), with no significant between-group differences.

**Visceral Adipose Tissue:** The JCEM (2022) meta-analysis [41] (19 RCTs, 745 participants) found both HIIT and MICT significantly reduced liver fat compared to control (HIIT: −2.85%; MICT: −3.14%), with no significant difference between modalities. A meta-analysis of 39 studies (617 subjects) [42] found HIIT significantly reduced total, abdominal, and visceral fat, with running more effective than cycling.

**Waist Circumference:** The young and middle-aged adults meta-analysis (29 RCTs, 807 participants) [43] found HIIT was significantly superior to MICT for reducing WC (MD = −0.96 cm, P = 0.037). The network meta-analysis [39] found HIIT ≥75 min/week reduced WC by MD = −4.82 cm.

### 3.5 Liver Fat Content

The most comprehensive evidence comes from the **JCEM (2022) systematic review and meta-analysis** [41] (19 RCTs, 745 participants). Both HIIT and MICT significantly reduced liver fat (HIIT: −2.85%, 95% CI −4.86 to −0.84, P = 0.005; MICT: −3.14%, 95% CI −4.45 to −1.82, P < 0.001). There was no significant difference between HIIT and MICT (−0.34%, 95% CI −2.20 to 1.52, P = 0.721). Neither total exercise volume nor energy expenditure were related to changes in liver fat, suggesting that both modalities are effective regardless of time commitment.

The Physiology & Behavior (2021) meta-analysis [44] (10 RCTs, 333 participants) confirmed that HIIT significantly reduced liver fat compared to no exercise (SMD −0.51, 95% CI −0.85 to −0.17, P = 0.003), with no significant difference between HIIT and MICT, and the improvement occurred independently of weight loss.

## 4. Patient-Centered Outcomes

### 4.1 Adherence

**Santos et al. (2023) systematic review and meta-analysis** [45] (188 studies, n=8,928 participants) is the most comprehensive analysis of adherence to HIIT vs. MICT. Weighted averages showed:
- Compliance to HIIT: 89.4% (SD: 11.8%)
- Compliance to MICT: 92.5% (SD: 10.6%)
- Adherence to HIIT: 63% (SD: 21.1%)
- Adherence to MICT: 68.2% (SD: 16.2%)

Meta-analysis of 65 studies found **no significant difference in compliance between HIIT and MICT** (Hedge's g = 0.015, 95%CI: −0.088–0.118, p = 0.78). Adherence meta-analysis (10 studies) also showed **no significant difference** (Hedge's g = −0.313, 95%CI: −0.681–0.056, p = 0.096).

**Long-term adherence (≥12 months):** The Ekkekakis and Biddle (2023) review [46] examined eight randomized controlled trials with follow-up periods of at least 12 months (SWIFT, Small Steps for Big Changes, SAINTEX-CAD, SMARTEX-HF, Generation 100, FITR, OptimEx-Clin, HITTS). Results consistently showed that **HIIT does not lead to superior adherence; in unsupervised settings, participants originally assigned to HIIT often exercised at lower intensities than prescribed, and no adherence advantage was found over MICT.**

**Generation 100 study (5-year RCT, 1,567 participants aged 70–77)** [47]: Adherence to prescribed exercise at Years 1, 3, and 5 was 50%, 49%, and 47% for HIIT, and 63%, 55%, and 51% for MICT. Men and women in HIIT had lower adherence compared with MICT at all follow-up times.

**Home-based delivery:** The home-based ERS study (Front. Physiol., 2021) [48] with 154 participants found adherence was poor and similar between groups (MICT 48±35%, Home-HIIT 39±36%, p=0.77). Despite low adherence, both interventions yielded clinically meaningful CRF improvements.

### 4.2 Enjoyment

**Frontiers in Public Health meta-analysis (2024, 16 RCTs, 537 participants)** [49] compared HIIT vs. MICT on enjoyment and affective responses in overweight/obese adults. Results showed:
- **HIIT elicited significantly higher enjoyment than MICT** (SMD = 0.47; 95% CI 0.12–0.81; p < 0.05), particularly in acute studies
- No significant difference for affective responses (FS) (SMD = −0.47; 95% CI −1.17–0.23)
- No significant difference for arousal (FAS) (SMD = −0.29; 95% CI −0.86–0.28)

**Thum et al. (2017)** [50] found significantly higher enjoyment for HIIT (PACES score 103.8 vs 84.2, p=0.013) and 92% preference for HIIT in 12 recreationally active adults. The authors attribute this to HIIT's time efficiency, alternating stimulus, and greater sense of accomplishment.

**Enjoyment over time:** The Frontiers meta-analysis [49] found the enjoyment advantage for HIIT was significant in acute studies but not in chronic studies, suggesting the novelty effect may diminish. Conversely, Heisz et al. (2016) [51] showed that "enjoyment for high-intensity interval exercise increases with chronic training." However, in overweight/obese female adolescents, MIIT showed progressively increasing pleasure over 12 weeks while HIIT showed decreasing pleasure.

### 4.3 Adverse Events and Safety

**Cardiovascular adverse events:** The Wewege et al. (2018) systematic review [52] (JAHA, 23 studies, 1,117 participants with CVD) found only one major cardiovascular adverse event during HIIT, equating to one event per 17,083 training sessions (11,333 training hours). The Frontiers in Cardiovascular Medicine (2022) review [53] (22 RCTs, 949 participants) found adverse events were rare: one minor cardiovascular event and four non-cardiovascular events in HIIT, versus six non-cardiovascular events in MICT.

**Absolute risk:** The Springer Nature editorial (2025) [54] provides comparative risk data: 1 event per 23,182 patient-hours for HIIT vs. 1 event per 129,456 patient-hours for MICT—5.6 times higher in HIIT. In session-based terms: 1 event per 3,417 sessions for HIIT vs. 1 per 7,134 for MICT. However, most RCTs studied low-risk, medically supervised patients.

**Musculoskeletal risks:** A systematic review (15 studies, 315 participants) [55] found HIIT protocols can promote changes in markers of exercise-induced muscle damage (CK, Mb, LDH, AST, ALT), with pain and muscle circumference increases observed immediately and 24 hours post-session. Even trained subjects exhibited significant muscle damage, indicating that high intensity can override training status.

**Screening and risk stratification:** The ACSM updated preparticipation health screening guidelines (2015) [56] now base clearance on: (1) current level of physical activity, (2) presence of signs/symptoms and/or known cardiovascular, metabolic, or renal disease, and (3) desired exercise intensity. CVD risk factor assessment is no longer part of the screening process per se, given the extremely low absolute risk of exercise-related sudden cardiac death (1 death per 396,000 hours of jogging).

**The ACSM risk stratification scheme** assigns participants to low, moderate, or high risk. Low-risk individuals require no medical workup for any intensity. Moderate-risk individuals require further workup only before vigorous activity. High-risk individuals require medical workup and exercise testing for both low-to-moderate and vigorous intensity.

## 5. Hybrid HIIT+MICT Protocols

### 5.1 Post-AMI Cardiac Rehabilitation

A landmark RCT published in the **American Heart Journal (2026)** [57] evaluated combining HIIT with MICT versus either modality alone in 204 post-PCI acute myocardial infarction patients. Results showed:
- Combined training: superior VO₂ peak improvement (5.6 ± 2.3 mL/kg/min)
- HIIT alone: 4.2 ± 1.8 mL/kg/min
- MICT alone: 2.7 ± 1.9 mL/kg/min
- All pairwise comparisons: P<.001
- 76.7% of combined group reached clinically meaningful improvement (≥3 mL/kg/min)
- LVEF increased by 8.6 ± 3.2% in combined group
- MACE incidence was low (2.9%) with no significant differences between groups

### 5.2 ACE IFT Model (Personalized MICT+HIIT)

The ACE-sponsored study (Byrd et al., 2019, IJERPH) [12] randomized 54 inactive, low-to-moderate risk adults (21–55 years) into control, individualized MICT+HIIT (based on ACE IFT Model), or standardized MICT-only. Key findings:
- MICT+HIIT group achieved significantly greater improvements in VO₂max and MetS z-score
- 100% of MICT+HIIT participants were responders based on MetS z-score vs. 56.25% in MICT-only
- 100% of MICT+HIIT showed positive VO₂max improvement (Δ > 4.9%) vs. 68.75% in MICT-only
- The study concludes that combined programs reduce the incidence of exercise non-responders

### 5.3 Post-Stroke Rehabilitation

A 24-week RCT (Cardiology, 2023) [13] compared HIIT (3 days/week) combined with MICT (2 days/week) versus MICT alone (5 days/week) in 47 post-stroke patients. HIIT led to significantly greater improvement in VO₂peak (mean difference 3.2 mL·kg−1·min−1, 95% CI, 1.5–4.8; P<0.001).

### 5.4 Hypertension

A 12-week RCT in 100 adults with hypertension (mean age 54) [36] compared MICT alone, strength training combined with MICT, and strength training combined with HIIT. The strength+MICT group achieved the greatest reductions (SBP −13.4 mmHg, DBP −6.8 mmHg, MAP −8.9 mmHg), superior to both strength+HIIT and MICT alone.

### 5.5 Clinical Recommendations

ACE Fitness (2022) [58] argues that HIIT and MICT should be viewed as complementary rather than mutually exclusive. Research shows HIIT is most effective for endothelial function and cardiorespiratory fitness, while MICT is better for lowering body fat, blood pressure, and lipids. ACE-supported research found that adding just one HIIT session per week to MICT led to 2.5 times greater improvements in cardiorespiratory fitness.

## 6. Progression and Maintenance Plans (≥6–12 Months)

### 6.1 Generation 100 Study (5-Year RCT)

The **Generation 100 study** [47] is the largest and longest randomized controlled trial on exercise training, involving 1,567 participants aged 70–77 from Trondheim, Norway, randomized to HIIT (~90% peak HR), MICT (~70% peak HR), or control (national guidelines).

**Mortality outcomes:**
- Overall survival: 95.4% across all groups
- HIIT: 3.0% died during study period
- MICT: 5.9% died
- Control: 4.7% died
- HIIT vs. MICT: HR 0.51 (95% CI 0.25–1.02), absolute risk reduction of 2.9 percentage points

**Adherence over 5 years:**
- HIIT: 50%, 49%, 47% at Years 1, 3, and 5
- MICT: 63%, 55%, 51% at Years 1, 3, and 5
- HIIT had lower adherence at all follow-up times

**Quality of life:** Both physical and mental quality of life were better in the high-intensity group after five years.

### 6.2 Long-Term Maintenance of VO₂max Gains

**Home-based HIIT vs. MICT (ERS Study, 2021)** [48]: VO₂peak increased significantly in both groups post-intervention (MICT: +3.9±6.0 mL/kg/min; Home-HIIT: +2.8±4.5 mL/kg/min, p<0.001) and **was maintained at 3-month follow-up**, with no between-group differences.

**Elderly populations (≥60 years):** Chu et al. (2026) [25] found that both HIIT and MICT significantly improved VO₂max, with HIIT superior (MD=1.17, 95% CI: 0.52–1.82). Optimal protocols for maintenance: HIIT—4 sessions/week, 21–39 min/session; MICT—3 sessions/week, ≥60 min/session.

### 6.3 Periodization and Progression

**HIIT 4×4 Norwegian Protocol** [5] progression model:
- Weeks 1–4: 2 sessions/week, 3–4 intervals, 4 min work/3 min recovery
- Weeks 5–8: 3 sessions/week, 4 intervals, maintain intensity
- Weeks 9–12: 3 sessions/week, 4 intervals, increase intensity to 90–95% HRmax
- Maintenance: 2–3 sessions/week, 4 intervals

**ACSM progression guidelines** [1]: Gradually increase time/duration first, manipulating only one variable at a time. For deconditioned individuals, start with lower intensity and shorter intervals, progressing to longer work intervals and higher intensity over 4–8 weeks.

### 6.4 Minimal Effective Dose

**For VO₂max improvement:** REHIT (20 min/week, 2 sessions) produces significant improvements [9]. Low-volume HIIT (≤60 min/week) produces comparable results to MICT requiring 150 min/week [8]. The dose–response analysis shows that higher intensity drives greater CRF gains, while increasing repetitions or duration does not augment improvements beyond a certain point.

**For cardiometabolic health:** The network meta-analysis [39] found HIIT ≥75 min/week was effective for fat loss and CRF improvement. The SMD analysis [8] found LV-HIIT (≤15 min/session, ≤5 min high-intensity work) significantly improved cardiometabolic health despite requiring only 14–47% of MICT time.

## 7. Major Guidelines

### 7.1 ACSM (American College of Sports Medicine)

**ACSM's Guidelines for Exercise Testing and Prescription (11th Edition)** [1] integrates HIIT information across most health conditions covered. The FITT framework for HIIT includes:
- **Frequency:** 2–3 days/week for HIIT, 5 days/week for moderate MICT
- **Intensity:** ≥80% HRmax for HIIT, 40–59% HRR for MICT
- **Time:** 20–30 min for HIIT, 30–60 min for MICT
- **Type:** Mode-specific, enjoyable activities

**ACSM Position Stand (2011)** [4] recommends: moderate-intensity exercise ≥30 min/day on ≥5 days/week (≥150 min/week), vigorous-intensity exercise ≥20 min/day on ≥3 days/week (≥75 min/week), or a combination. Resistance training 2–3 days/week for major muscle groups.

**ACSM 2026 Resistance Training Guidelines** [59] (first major update since 2009, based on 137 systematic reviews, 30,000+ participants) emphasizes: consistency over complexity, tailored load and volume to goals, and that nontraditional tools (bands, bodyweight, home routines) are highly effective. Adherence is identified as the true driver of long-term outcomes.

**ACSM Diabetes and Exercise Recommendations (2022)** [60] highlights: short bouts of physical activity throughout the day improve blood glucose; high-intensity resistance exercise is more beneficial than low-to-moderate intensity; being active after meals reduces blood glucose.

### 7.2 WHO (World Health Organization) 2020 Guidelines

The WHO 2020 guidelines [61] recommend:
- Adults: 150–300 minutes moderate-intensity OR 75–150 minutes vigorous-intensity aerobic physical activity per week
- Adults: muscle-strengthening activities on 2 or more days per week
- Children and adolescents: average 60 min/day of moderate-to-vigorous physical activity
- Reducing sedentary behaviour across all age groups
- "Every move counts" – any duration of physical activity is beneficial, with the old 10-minute bout requirement removed

The WHO noted that 31% of adults (1.8 billion) are physically inactive, with a 5 percentage point increase from 2010 to 2022. If trends continue, 35% of adults will be inactive by 2030.

### 7.3 AHA/ACC (American Heart Association/American College of Cardiology)

The AHA [62] recommends: at least 150 minutes/week of moderate-intensity or 75 minutes/week of vigorous-intensity aerobic activity, spread throughout the week, plus muscle-strengthening activities at least twice a week. Only about one in five adults and teens currently meet these targets.

The 2025 AHA/ACC scientific statement on competitive sports participation [63] marks a paradigm shift from paternalistic disqualification to a shared decision-making framework, incorporating recent evidence on lower-than-expected risks for athletes with hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, long QT syndrome, and ICDs.

### 7.4 ESC (European Society of Cardiology)

The 2020 ESC Guidelines [64] recommend at least 150 minutes of moderate or 75 minutes of vigorous-intensity PA per week. Regular PA reduces relative risk of cardiovascular mortality by 27%. The guidelines emphasize promoting moderate physical activity in all patients with CVD while balancing the small risk of exercise-triggered adverse events.

### 7.5 ADA (American Diabetes Association)

The ADA updated guidelines [65] recommend: most adults with diabetes should get at least 150 minutes of moderate-to-vigorous aerobic exercise per week, spread over at least 3 days with no more than 2 days between sessions, plus 2–3 sessions of resistance training on nonconsecutive days. A key change in 2016 was the recommendation to interrupt prolonged sitting every 30 minutes with light activity.

The ADA Standards of Care in Diabetes—2026 [66] includes expanded use of continuous glucose monitoring, new guidance on obesity pharmacotherapy, and emphasis on Mediterranean-style and low-carbohydrate eating patterns.

## 8. Moderators of Training Effects

### 8.1 Sex

**The 115-trial meta-analysis (2026)** [16] found that sex is a significant moderator, with HIIT particularly superior for males and mixed-sex groups. However, the magnitude is modest and individual variability often exceeds sex-based differences.

**Dr. Martin Gibala (2023)** [67] noted that subtle sex effects may exist (e.g., menstrual cycle phases, oral contraceptive use), but individual variation often outweighs biological sex. After repeating a study with proper controls (menstrual cycle phase, fat-free mass normalization), initial sex differences in SIT response disappeared.

**Physiological basis:** Women have approximately 12% lower hemoglobin levels than men, and maximum cardiac output is lower in women. When VO₂max is expressed per kilogram of fat-free mass, the gender difference is significantly reduced [68].

**Postmenopausal women:** HIIT is beneficial for cardiorespiratory, muscle, and cognitive advantages. A 12-week RCT in postmenopausal women with overweight/obesity [69] found both HIIT and concurrent training (HIIT+resistance) improved body composition and metabolic profile, with concurrent training showing greater reductions in body weight, BMI, and body fat percentage.

**Perceptual responses:** A 2024 RCT [70] found no significant differences between sexes in RPE, affective valence, or enjoyment during HIIT, despite higher blood lactate in men.

### 8.2 Age Band (30–49 vs. 50–70)

**HIIT is at least as effective in older adults (50–70) as in younger adults (30–49).** The Generation 100 study [47] (age 70–77) found HIIT associated with lower all-cause mortality (HR 0.51, 95% CI 0.25–1.02) compared to MICT. Yang et al. (2023) [24] (mean age ≥55) found HIIT significantly improved VO₂max compared to MICT (WMD = 1.80 mL/kg/min).

**Younger adults (18–45):** The 2022 meta-analysis [43] found HIIT benefits for fat loss and VO₂peak were more pronounced in younger adults (18–45 years), those with obesity, interventions lasting >6 weeks, and higher frequency (>3 times/week).

**Older adults (≥60 years):** The Chu et al. (2026) meta-analysis [25] found HIIT superior to MICT (MD = 1.17, 95% CI: 0.52–1.82). Both modalities improved VO₂max, with HIIT optimal for short-term, intensive interventions and MICT suitable for longer-term training.

**Progression in older adults:** A 2019 study [71] found that healthy older women can safely exercise and progress intensity at the same rate as young women, challenging recommendations for slower progression in older adults.

### 8.3 Baseline Fitness

**Low-fit/deconditioned individuals show the greatest absolute and relative improvements.** The 115-trial meta-analysis [16] found HIIT was particularly superior for inactive individuals. The cycling HIIT meta-analysis [23] found HIIT outperformed MICT in improving VO₂peak in sedentary populations (SMD = 6.21 mL·kg⁻¹·min⁻¹, p<0.001) and obese populations (SMD = 0.23 mL·kg⁻¹·min⁻¹, p=0.041).

**The 2018 Physical Activity Guidelines Advisory Committee** [72] found that HIIT-induced improvements in cardiometabolic risk factors are more likely to occur in adults at higher risk of CVD and diabetes than in healthy adults. Adults with overweight or obesity are more responsive than normal-weight individuals.

**Low-volume HIIT dose–response:** The SMD analysis [8] showed significant inverse dose–responses between CRF change and sprint repetitions (β = −0.52), high-intensity duration (β = −0.21), and total duration (β = −0.19), while higher intensity significantly improved CRF gains. This suggests that deconditioned individuals benefit most from low-volume, high-intensity protocols.

### 8.4 Cardiometabolic Risk (Overweight/Obesity, Prediabetes/Metabolic Syndrome, Hypertension)

**Overweight/obesity:** The network meta-analysis [39] found HIIT ≥75 min/week ranking first for BFP reduction (SUCRA 84.74%), WC reduction (4.82 cm), and WHR reduction. The cycling HIIT meta-analysis [23] found HIIT significantly reduced BMI in obese populations (SMD = −0.59 kg/m²) but increased BMI in disease populations.

**Prediabetes:** The Frontiers in Endocrinology (May 2026) meta-analysis [30] found HIIT significantly improved 2-hour plasma glucose (MD −0.16 mmol/L) and CRF (SMD 1.16), while MICT had a small advantage for fasting glucose. A 12-week RCT in prediabetes patients [38] found both groups improved HbA1c, but MICT was superior to HIIT in reducing visceral adipose area, body fat percentage, DBP, and triglycerides.

**Metabolic syndrome:** The 12-week RCT in middle-aged adults (45–65) with MetS [31] found HIIT produced significantly greater reductions in HbA1c (−0.72%), FBG (−0.68 mmol/L), TG (>1.0 mmol/L reduction), and HDL-C increase (>0.3 mmol/L increase) compared to MICT and control.

**Hypertension:** The 12-week RCT in 100 adults with hypertension (mean age 54) [36] found combined strength+MICT achieved the largest BP reductions (SBP −13.4 mmHg, DBP −6.8 mmHg), superior to both strength+HIIT and MICT alone.

### 8.5 Medication Status

**Beta-blockers:** Beta-blockers attenuate heart rate response to exercise, making HR-based intensity prescription unreliable. The ACSM recommends using RPE (Borg scale) for intensity prescription in patients on beta-blockers, targeting RPE 12–14 for moderate and 14–17 for vigorous intensity [1].

**Metformin:** A systematic review [73] suggests metformin may attenuate the beneficial effects of exercise on VO₂max and insulin sensitivity. However, the clinical significance of this interaction remains unclear, and patients should not discontinue metformin for exercise training.

**Statins:** Statin use is associated with a small but significant reduction in exercise capacity and may be associated with increased risk of myopathy during high-intensity exercise. However, the benefits of exercise training in statin users outweigh the risks, and exercise can help mitigate statin-related side effects [73].

**Antihypertensive medications:** Exercise training is recommended as first-line therapy for hypertension. The interaction between antihypertensive medications and exercise is generally favorable, with exercise enhancing the blood pressure-lowering effects of most medications [1].

## 9. Subgroup Analysis: ≤60 Minutes Per Week

### 9.1 Low-Volume HIIT (≤60 min/week)

The **SMD analysis (2023, 21 studies, 849 participants)** [8] specifically examined LV-HIIT (≤5 min high-intensity exercise within ≤15 min session). Compared to untrained controls, LV-HIIT significantly improved:
- CRF: SMD = 1.19 [0.87, 1.50]
- Systolic BP: SMD = −1.44 [−1.68, −1.20]
- Diastolic BP: SMD = −1.51 [−1.75, −1.27]
- Mean arterial pressure: SMD = −1.55 [−1.80, −1.30]
- MetS z-score: SMD = −0.76 [−1.02, −0.49]
- Fat mass (kg): SMD = −0.22 [−0.44, 0.00]
- Fat mass (%): SMD = −0.22 [−0.41, −0.02]
- Waist circumference: SMD = −0.53 [−0.75, −0.31]

Despite requiring only 14–47% of the time of MICT, no significant differences were found between LV-HIIT and MICT for any outcome.

### 9.2 REHIT (20–30 min/week)

**Metcalfe and Vollaard (2024) review** [9] demonstrates that REHIT (two 20-second all-out sprints within a 10-minute session, 2–3 sessions/week) improves VO₂max by 10–15% after 6 weeks. ACE-commissioned studies showed an 8-week REHIT program improved cardiorespiratory fitness by 12.3% (vs. 6.9% for MICT) and reduced systolic blood pressure three times more. A 3-week REHIT program counteracted negative effects of sedentary behavior.

**The original REHIT study** [18] found that six weeks of REHIT improved VO₂max by 15% in men and 12% in women, and increased insulin sensitivity by 28% in men, from a total of 30 minutes of weekly exercise.

### 9.3 Network Meta-Analysis: HIIT <75 vs. ≥75 min/week

The **BMC Sports Science network meta-analysis (2026, 18 RCTs, 952 overweight/obese adults)** [39] stratified by weekly exercise volume:
- HIIT ≥75 min/week: largest reductions in BFP (MD −3.05%), WHR (MD −0.05), and WC (MD −4.82 cm), and greatest VO₂max improvement (MD 5.94 mL/kg/min)
- HIIT <75 min/week: also effective but to a lesser extent
- MICT ≥150 min/week: significant benefits but smaller effects

### 9.4 Practical Implications

For individuals with ≤60 min/week of available training time, the evidence supports:
- **REHIT (20–30 min/week):** Effective for improving VO₂max and cardiometabolic health
- **Low-volume HIIT (≤60 min/week):** Comparable or superior to MICT requiring 150 min/week
- **SIT (≤60 min/week):** Similar gains to HIIT for CRF
- **MICT (≤60 min/week):** May provide benefits but likely insufficient to meet WHO guidelines independently

## 10. Key Findings Summary

### 10.1 Cardiorespiratory Fitness
- HIIT consistently produces superior VO₂max improvements compared to MICT (0.5–3.8 mL/kg/min greater)
- Low-volume HIIT (≤60 min/week) produces comparable results to MICT requiring 150 min/week
- REHIT (20–30 min/week) improves VO₂max by 10–15%
- No significant difference between HIIT and SIT for CRF gains

### 10.2 Cardiometabolic Outcomes
- **Glycemic control:** Both modalities effective; HIIT shows superiority for FBG and post-challenge glucose; MICT may be superior for HbA1c in some populations
- **Blood pressure:** Both effective; HIIT may offer modest SBP reduction advantage (−2.59 mmHg)
- **Lipids:** Neither modality clearly superior for TC/LDL; HIIT may offer small HDL-C advantage
- **Adiposity:** HIIT ≥75 min/week most effective for BFP reduction (−3.05%); both modalities comparable for visceral fat
- **Liver fat:** Both modalities equally effective; improvements occur independently of weight loss

### 10.3 Patient-Centered Outcomes
- **Adherence:** No significant difference between HIIT and MICT in supervised settings
- **Long-term adherence (≥12 months):** HIIT does not show superior adherence; may be lower in unsupervised settings
- **Enjoyment:** HIIT may be preferred acutely; long-term effects unclear
- **Adverse events:** Rare with appropriate screening; risk is low but higher than MICT (5.6× for cardiac events)

### 10.4 Protocol Recommendations
- **For maximal VO₂max improvement:** 4×4 protocol (4 min at 85–95% HRmax, 3 min recovery, 4 sets), 3 sessions/week, 12 weeks
- **For time efficiency:** REHIT (2×20s all-out sprints, 10 min session, 2 sessions/week)
- **For metabolic syndrome:** 4×4 HIIT, 3 sessions/week, ≥12 weeks
- **For cardiac rehabilitation:** Combined HIIT+MICT, 3+2 sessions/week
- **For older adults:** HIIT 3 sessions/week, 20 min/session, <80% VO₂peak, ≤60s intervals, ≤90s rest

### 10.5 Screening Recommendations
- Low-risk individuals: No medical clearance required for any intensity
- Moderate-risk (≥2 CVD risk factors): Medical clearance before vigorous activity
- High-risk (known CVD/symptoms): Medical clearance and exercise testing before any intensity
- Beta-blocker users: Use RPE rather than HR for intensity prescription
