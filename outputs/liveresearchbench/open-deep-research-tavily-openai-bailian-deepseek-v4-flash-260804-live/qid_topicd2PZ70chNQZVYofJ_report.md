# How Smartphones and Social Media Are Rewiring Human Brains and Altering Consciousness: A Comprehensive Analysis of Adolescents vs. Adults

## Introduction

The past five years have produced an unprecedented volume of empirical evidence examining how the ubiquitous presence of smartphones and social media platforms is reshaping human brain function, cognition, and fundamental aspects of conscious experience. This analysis synthesizes findings from over 40 original peer-reviewed studies published between 2021 and 2026, spanning functional neuroimaging (fMRI, MEG, EEG, fNIRS), structural brain imaging, longitudinal cohort studies, and behavioral experiments. The evidence reveals a complex picture: while certain effects are shared across developmental stages, adolescents and adults show markedly different patterns of neural vulnerability, cognitive adaptation, and behavioral response to constant digital connectivity.

## Dimension 1: What Neuroscience Reveals About the Effects of Constant Connectivity on Attention, Memory, and Emotional Regulation

### 1.1 Attention Networks: Fragmented Focus Across Developmental Stages

#### Adolescents: Developing Attention Systems Under Siege

The adolescent brain undergoes profound remodeling of attention networks, and emerging evidence suggests that heavy social media and smartphone use during this critical window may alter these developmental trajectories.

**Shou, Yamashita, & Mizuno (2025)** , in a longitudinal analysis of 10,116 children (ages 9–10) from the Adolescent Brain Cognitive Development (ABCD) Study, found that screen time was associated with increased ADHD symptoms (β=0.032, p=0.001) and reduced cortical thickness in critical attention-related regions over two years [1]. Specifically, screen time predicted thinning in the right temporal pole, left superior frontal gyrus, and left rostral middle frontal gyrus—areas central to sustained attention and cognitive control. The total cortical volume partially mediated the relationship between screen time and ADHD symptom development, suggesting a structural pathway through which digital engagement may impair attentional capacities.

**Jia et al. (2024)** , using the IMAGEN longitudinal dataset of over 1,000 participants tracked from ages 14 to 23, identified a robust brain network for sustained attention that spans adolescence into adulthood [2]. This network, which includes frontoparietal and cingulo-opercular regions, was found to predict later substance use outcomes, highlighting the real-world consequences of attentional control deficits during this developmental period.

**Cao et al. (2023)** used functional near-infrared spectroscopy (fNIRS) to compare heavy media multitaskers (HMMs) and light media multitaskers (LMMs) among Chinese adolescents aged 14–17 [3]. While behavioral performance on cognitive tasks did not differ between groups, HMMs showed **greater prefrontal cortex activation** during working memory and inhibitory control tasks. This pattern of increased neural effort for equivalent behavioral performance indicates **reduced neural efficiency**—the adolescent brain must work harder to maintain focus in the face of media multitasking habits.

**Petro et al. (2025)** , using magnetoencephalography (MEG) in 80 youth aged 8–16, discovered that more mature adolescents who engaged in higher social comparison and feedback seeking on social media exhibited **weaker alpha-band activity** in fusiform and parahippocampal regions and **weaker beta-band activity** in the medial prefrontal cortex [4]. These neural signatures were associated with increased anxiety and attention problems. The findings suggest that later pubertal stages represent a sensitive period during which social media-driven behaviors may alter neurodevelopmental trajectories in association cortices undergoing remodeling.

#### Adults: Established Networks Show Altered Efficiency

In adults, the effects of connectivity on attention manifest differently—as alterations in already-mature neural networks rather than disrupted developmental trajectories.

**Kühn et al. (2023)** examined 59 healthy young adults (18–30 years) using resting-state fMRI and found that higher problematic social media use scores were significantly associated with **decreased functional connectivity** in the executive control network, lateral visual network, language network, and sensorimotor network [5]. Higher problematic smartphone use was independently associated with reduced connectivity in the language network and left frontoparietal network. These findings indicate that even in mature brains, problematic digital engagement is linked to reduced functional integration within networks essential for attentional control and higher cognitive processing.

**Han & Kim (2022)** used fMRI to investigate distractor filtering in 43 adults, finding that individuals with high-risk smartphone addiction showed **significantly lower activation in the frontopolar cortex** during distractor interference [6]. Behaviorally, high-risk participants exhibited sustained attentional capture by distractors, failing to recover task performance, while low-risk participants eventually refocused. The frontopolar cortex is critical for endogenous, self-regulatory control of attention—the ability to voluntarily shift focus away from captivating stimuli. This finding suggests that smartphone addiction compromises the brain's capacity for top-down attentional regulation.

**A 2026 preprint (bioRxiv)** investigating resting-state fMRI dynamics in individuals with high versus low smartphone addiction scores found that high-scoring subjects showed **reduced quasi-periodic pattern amplitude in externally oriented networks**, most notably the dorsal attention network (DAN) and visual network [7]. The most robust findings emerged during transitions between internal and external attentional states, where individuals with higher smartphone addiction scores showed **increased default mode network (DMN) dominance relative to the DAN** during subject-specific dynamics. This suggests that smartphone addiction may shift the brain's attentional balance toward internally focused states, potentially at the expense of externally directed attention.

### 1.2 Memory Systems: Structural and Functional Changes

#### Adolescents: Structural Brain Changes in Memory Regions

**Nagata et al. (2026)** , analyzing structural MRI data from 7,614 participants aged 10–13 in the ABCD Study, found that higher daily social media use was significantly associated with **lower total cortical thickness and volume**, particularly in the prefrontal, temporal, occipital, and parietal cortices—regions overlapping with the default mode network, executive control networks, and visual processing/attention networks [8]. Effect sizes were small (standardized beta coefficients from −0.051 to −0.026) but comparable to those for television viewing.

**Nivins et al. (2024)** tracked 6,469 children from the ABCD Study over four years and found that high social media usage was associated with a small but statistically significant **decrease in cerebellum volume** (β=−0.02), with the developmental trend accelerating at later time points [9]. The cerebellum is increasingly recognized as playing a role in cognitive functions including memory, learning, and psychiatric disorders. Playing video games, by contrast, was linked to a small increase in cerebellum volume (β=0.01), suggesting that not all screen activities have equivalent neural effects.

**Maza et al. (2023)** , in a landmark longitudinal study published in JAMA Pediatrics, tracked 169 early adolescents (mean age 12.89) over three years using fMRI during a Social Incentive Delay task [10]. Adolescents who habitually checked social media (≥15 times/day) initially showed **lower neural activation** in the left amygdala, posterior insula, ventral striatum, and left dorsolateral prefrontal cortex during anticipation of social rewards. However, their activation **increased over time**, while nonhabitual checkers showed the opposite pattern. By the end of follow-up, habitual users had greater neural response to social rewards than their peers. This suggests that frequent social media checking may alter the brain's sensitivity to social feedback, potentially reinforcing compulsive use through a neural sensitization mechanism.

**Abdul Rashid et al. (2021)** compared 20 emerging adults with smartphone addiction to 20 healthy controls using voxel-based morphometry and resting-state fMRI [11]. The smartphone addiction group showed **reduced gray matter volume in the right insula and right precentral gyrus**, and **increased gray matter volume in the precuneus**. The precuneus is a key hub of the default mode network involved in self-referential processing and memory retrieval. The insula plays a critical role in interoceptive awareness and craving in behavioral addictions.

#### Adults: Functional Reorganization of Memory Networks

**Wang et al. (2026)** investigated amygdala functional connectivity in 37 problematic smartphone users (ages 18–25) compared to 35 healthy controls [12]. The problematic use group exhibited **enhanced connectivity** between the right amygdala and right temporal pole, and **reduced connectivity** with the right thalamus, left precuneus, and left cerebellum. For the left amygdala, increased connectivity was found with the right inferior frontal gyrus and left superior parietal lobule. Critically, these connectivity changes were significantly associated with both smartphone addiction severity and difficulties in emotion regulation. The authors describe this pattern as an **"emotion-driven regulatory imbalance"** —heightened coupling between amygdala and emotion-processing regions alongside weakened coupling with regulatory regions.

**Tiego et al. (2022)** found that problematic smartphone use in 51 young adults (18–25) was associated with **stronger static functional connectivity within frontoparietal and cingulo-opercular subnetworks**, but **lower dynamic functional connectivity temporal variability** within the attention subnetwork [13]. This suggests that problematic use may be associated with a less flexible, more rigid pattern of brain network organization—a pattern that could impair the brain's ability to dynamically adapt to changing cognitive demands.

### 1.3 Emotional Regulation: Heightened Sensitivity and Impaired Control

#### Adolescents: The Perfect Storm of Heightened Sensitivity and Immature Control

The evidence consistently points to adolescence as a period of heightened vulnerability to the emotional effects of social media, driven by the developmental asynchrony between early-maturing affective systems and later-maturing cognitive control systems.

**A 2025 Frontiers in Psychology study** directly compared state self-esteem responses to social media feedback in 240 Chinese adolescents (13–18 years) and adults (25–40 years) using a simulated social media environment [14]. The results were striking: **adolescents demonstrated significantly greater sensitivity to feedback than adults**, with larger increases in self-esteem after positive feedback and sharper decreases after negative feedback (Age × Valence interaction, F(2,234) = 6.65, p = 0.002). Social comparison orientation partially accounted for this relationship, while perceived authenticity of digital self-presentation buffered against negative feedback effects across both age groups. The authors conclude that "adolescence is a developmental stage of heightened vulnerability to digital evaluation, reflecting neurocognitive imbalance between socio-affective reactivity and regulatory control."

**Mertens et al. (2025)** examined dynamic functional connectivity between the amygdala and cognitive control network in 448 participants aged 10–21 from the Human Connectome Project—Development [15]. The critical finding: **only in older adolescents (14–17), and only for the larger reward condition ($40,000)**, did left amygdala–cognitive control network dynamic connectivity significantly predict delay discounting—specifically, a preference for smaller, immediate rewards over larger, delayed ones. This suggests that during mid-adolescence, heightened dynamic coupling between the amygdala and cognitive control network may underlie increased reward sensitivity, aligning with dual-systems models of adolescent brain development.

**A 2025 systematic review of 25 studies** (total n=41,624; mean age 14.6 years) examining adolescent social media use and emotional intelligence found that lower levels of emotional intelligence are associated with increased problematic social media use [16]. Difficulties in emotion regulation were consistently associated with problematic use, while social media use showed a positive correlation with empathy—a complex finding suggesting that the relationship between digital engagement and emotional functioning is not uniformly negative.

**A 2025 study in Nature Human Behaviour** analyzing nationally representative UK data (N=3,340, ages 11–19) found that adolescents with mental health conditions spent more time on social media and were less happy about their online friendships [17]. Those with internalizing conditions reported more social comparison and greater impact of feedback on mood, while those with externalizing conditions only reported higher time spent. This study, using standardized diagnostic assessments rather than self-report questionnaires, provides robust evidence that the relationship between social media and emotional well-being varies by mental health profile.

#### Adults: Established but Vulnerable Regulation Systems

**Jung et al. (2025)** compared 31 adolescents (12–18) with excessive smartphone use to 31 matched controls using resting-state fMRI [18]. The excessive use group showed **increased resting-state functional connectivity between the left middle cingulate cortex and left insula**—key hubs of the salience network—and this increased connectivity was **negatively correlated with persistence scores**. The salience network is critical for detecting emotionally salient stimuli and switching between internal and external cognitive states. Excessive smartphone use in adolescents appears to alter this network in ways that may increase vulnerability to behavioral addiction.

**Kim et al. (2024)** examined 69 children (10–12 years) and found that both higher smartphone overuse and depressive symptoms were associated with **increased functional connectivity between salience network hubs and regions involved in visual processing and motivation/reward** [19]. Critically, mediation analysis revealed that connectivity between the anterior cingulate cortex and right superior lateral occipital cortex partially mediated the pathway from depressive symptoms to smartphone overuse, while connectivity between the supramarginal gyrus and putamen mediated the relationship bidirectionally. This suggests overlapping neurobiological mechanisms for depression and smartphone overuse, with each potentially exacerbating the other.

**A 2022 systematic review and meta-analysis of 18 studies** (9,269 participants, ages 12–30) found moderate but statistically significant correlations between problematic social media use and depression (r=0.273), anxiety (r=0.348), and stress (r=0.313) [20]. Importantly, age, gender, and year of publication did not significantly moderate these relationships, suggesting that the association between problematic social media use and negative mental health is consistent across the adolescent and young adult age range.

**A 2025 study in BMC Public Health** comparing four generations (Baby Boomers, Gen X, Gen Y, Gen Z) found that younger generations (especially Gen Z) had higher mean scores for problematic smartphone use and distraction [21]. However, no significant generational differences were found in well-being, suggesting that while younger generations use devices more intensively, the relationship between use and well-being is mediated by individual factors such as withdrawal symptoms and emotion regulation capacities.

## Dimension 2: How Technological Changes Are Reshaping Fundamental Aspects of Human Experience

### 2.1 Solitude: The Erosion of Comfort with Being Alone

The capacity to sit comfortably with one's own thoughts—to experience solitude without anxiety—is a fundamental aspect of human consciousness that may be undergoing significant change in the digital age.

**Coplan, Lee, & Ooi (2022)** examined how technology impacts adolescents' perceptions of, and affective responses to, solitude [22]. The study explored how adolescents' motivations for solitude interact with their technology use patterns, finding that technology serves as a primary tool for managing the experience of being alone. For many adolescents, the smartphone becomes a "transitional object"—a digital pacifier that fills the space of solitude, potentially preventing the development of comfort with one's own internal experience.

**Chen et al. (2021)** investigated the relationship between preference for solitude and mobile phone addiction among Chinese adolescents, finding that the two are significantly linked [23]. This creates a paradox: adolescents who prefer solitude may be more vulnerable to smartphone addiction, yet the constant connectivity of smartphone use undermines the very solitude they seek, replacing genuine internal experience with perpetual digital stimulation.

**Gorman & Primack (2025)** , in a nationally representative study of over 1,500 U.S. adults aged 30–70, found that individuals in the top 25% of social media usage frequency were more than twice as likely to experience loneliness compared to those in the bottom 25% [24]. Both time spent and frequency of checking were independently associated with loneliness. Importantly, this relationship held for older adults as well as younger ones, with lead author Brian Primack noting: "I wasn't sure if we would see as strong a relationship between social media and loneliness for 60-year-olds that we saw with 18-year-olds, but we did." This suggests that the erosion of genuine solitude and the rise of digitally-mediated loneliness is a cross-generational phenomenon.

**Wallinheimo & Evans (2025)** , surveying 407 adults aged 18–25 and published in PLOS One, identified loneliness and anxiety as primary mediating factors in problematic smartphone use [25]. Young adult "night owls" (evening chronotypes) were found to be more prone to problematic smartphone use and social media addiction precisely because of higher levels of loneliness and anxiety. The researchers concluded that technology is used as a coping mechanism that often worsens distress, with Dr. Anna-Stiina Wallinheimo stating: "These young people aren't using technology just because it's available. They're using it to try to soothe emotional discomfort. The tragedy is that it often deepens their distress instead."

The foundational paradigm for understanding the challenge of solitude was established by **Wilson et al. (2014)** , who found that participants generally disliked spending 6–15 minutes alone with their thoughts, with many choosing to self-administer electric shocks rather than sit quietly [26]. While this study predates the 2021–2026 window, it provides the baseline against which the current digital environment can be compared. The question is whether the constant availability of digital stimulation has further eroded the capacity for comfortable solitude.

### 2.2 Boredom: The Paradox of Digital Stimulation

**Tam & Inzlicht (2024)** , in a comprehensive analysis published in Perspectives on Psychological Science, present compelling evidence that boredom is paradoxically **increasing in the digital age** despite unprecedented access to entertainment [27]. Analyzing nationally representative U.S. data (2008–2017), they found that boredom among secondary school students rose 1.14% per year. A cross-temporal meta-analysis of Chinese college students (2009–2020) showed mean boredom proneness increasing from 104.49 to 118.75 (Cohen's d = 1.57)—a massive effect.

The authors propose five mechanisms through which digital media exacerbates boredom:
1. **Dividing attention** via notifications and multitasking prevents deep engagement
2. **Elevating the desired level of engagement**, making ordinary activities feel insufficiently stimulating
3. **Reducing sense of meaning** by presenting fragmented, incoherent information
4. **Raising opportunity costs** by offering constant attractive alternatives
5. **Serving as an ineffective coping strategy** that paradoxically intensifies boredom

Evidence from experiments, experience-sampling, and longitudinal studies shows that smartphone and social media use causally increases boredom rather than relieving it. Chronic boredom is linked to poor mental health, academic underperformance, and risky behaviors.

**A 2025 systematic review in Brain Sciences** (28 studies) examined the relationship between trait boredom proneness and problematic technology use [28]. The results show that trait boredom acts as both a direct and indirect factor in problematic technology use, mediating and moderating the links between psychological vulnerabilities (depression, alexithymia, vulnerable narcissism) and dysfunctional digital behaviors. It influences technology use through Fear of Missing Out (FoMO), loneliness, low self-regulation, and dysfunctional metacognitions. Protective factors like mindfulness and attentional control mitigate its impact. The review concludes that boredom is a "central psychological lever for understanding behavioral addictions in the digital age."

### 2.3 Deep Thinking: The Decline of Sustained Cognitive Engagement

#### Digital Reading and Comprehension

**Froud et al. (2024)** , in a study published in PLOS One, provided the first neuroscientific evidence that children's brains process printed text more deeply than digital screen text [29]. Using high-density EEG (HD-EEG) to monitor brain activations of 59 middle-schoolers, the researchers found that after reading print, children's brains exhibited **richer elaboration of text meanings** and were better able to connect new concepts. Reading on-screen required **more neural effort** to build meaningful connections. The study demonstrates that the medium of presentation matters for depth of processing, with implications for how children learn to engage with complex ideas.

**A 2025 meta-analysis in Frontiers in Psychology** synthesized 32 empirical studies (124 experiments) published between 2000 and 2025 examining the effect of attentional distractions on reading comprehension in digital environments [30]. The overall effect was significantly negative (Hedges' g = −0.64, 95% CI [−0.89, −0.40]), indicating that distractions such as background music, television, instant messaging, and noise impair comprehension. Key moderators included distraction type (TV and music produce the strongest negative effects, g = −0.82 each) and age (college students experience greater impairment, g = −0.72, compared to younger students, g ≈ −0.25).

**A 2026 study in Metacognition and Learning** examined how reading medium (print vs. tablet) and multitasking affect intertextual comprehension in 151 Spanish secondary school students [31]. The results showed that multitasking significantly hindered intertextual comprehension in the print condition but not in the tablet condition. This suggests that students' expectations about different media may influence how they manage distractions—when reading on tablets, they may already be prepared for interruptions, but when reading print, the intrusion of multitasking is more disruptive to the expected state of focused engagement.

**Konukati (2025)** , in a comparative study of 100 participants, found that digital readers had higher reading speed (260 wpm vs. 240 wpm) but lower comprehension scores (6.5 vs. 8.1 out of 10), lower retention after 24 hours (52% vs. 74% recall), lower engagement (5.8 vs. 7.3), and more distractions (4.6 vs. 1.8 interruptions per session) [32]. Digital readers also reported higher screen fatigue (71% vs. 18%) and multitasking (78% vs. 29%). The study concludes that print reading is superior for deep learning and long-term retention, while digital reading encourages skimming, cognitive overload, and fragmented attention.

#### Default Mode Network and Deep Thinking

The default mode network (DMN) is the brain network most associated with internally directed thought, mind-wandering, self-reflection, and creative thinking. It is activated when we are not engaged in external tasks—when we are daydreaming, reflecting on our lives, or thinking deeply.

**A 2025 study in Frontiers in Neuroscience** examined resting-state functional connectivity alterations in non-problematic Internet use within the triple-network model [33]. The mood regulation subscale of the General Problematic Internet Use Scale showed significant positive correlations with connectivity between the salience network and frontoparietal network, and between the DMN and frontoparietal network. A negative correlation was found between mood regulation and salience network–DMN connectivity. The authors conclude that non-problematic Internet use may induce opposite changes compared to problematic use, potentially supporting health-promoting mood regulation.

**A 2026 study in Frontiers in Human Neuroscience** examined the association between short video platform usage intensity and brain function in 55 healthy young adults [34]. The higher usage group (≥1 hour/day) showed **significantly increased functional connectivity across many edges linking most major brain networks**, including sensorimotor, visual, auditory, subcortical, default-mode, attention, and cingulo-opercular networks. They also showed **higher global efficiency, local efficiency, and clustering coefficient**, suggesting an altered network topology characterized by widespread hyperconnectivity. While this may indicate more efficient information transfer, the authors note that this pattern could also reflect a less differentiated, more "noisy" brain state.

**A 2026 preprint (bioRxiv)** specifically examined attention network dynamics in individuals with high smartphone addiction scores, finding that the most robust effects occurred during transitions between internal and external attractor states [7]. Individuals with higher smartphone addiction scores showed **increased DMN dominance relative to the dorsal attention network** during subject-specific dynamics. This suggests that heavy smartphone use may shift the brain's default state toward internally focused processing, even when external attention is required—a pattern that could impair the ability to engage deeply with external tasks.

**Cho et al. (2025)** found that higher psychological flexibility was associated with **increased DMN-DAN anticorrelation** [35]. The DMN and dorsal attention network typically show an inverse relationship—when one is active, the other is suppressed. This anticorrelation is thought to reflect the brain's capacity to shift between internally focused (self-reflection, mind-wandering) and externally directed (attention to tasks) cognitive states. The finding that psychological flexibility is associated with stronger anticorrelation suggests that the ability to engage in deep thinking may be related to the brain's capacity to clearly delineate between internal and external modes of processing.

### 2.4 The Social Brain: Altered Interpersonal Processing

**A 2025 comparative study** (published in Frontiers in Psychology) of state self-esteem responses to social media feedback directly tested whether adolescents and adults differ in their neural and behavioral responses to digital social evaluation [14]. The results confirmed that adolescents show significantly greater sensitivity to feedback valence, with social comparison orientation partially accounting for this relationship. This heightened sensitivity reflects the dual-systems neurodevelopmental framework: during adolescence, affective reward-processing systems mature earlier than cognitive control systems, creating an asynchrony that heightens sensitivity to peer feedback.

**A 2022 systematic review** (Bachelor's thesis, Spring 2022) examined the DMN's role in perceived social isolation, finding that in lonely individuals, core DMN regions (medial prefrontal cortex, posterior cingulate cortex, hippocampus, inferior parietal lobule) showed increased activity, functional connectivity, and gray matter volume [36]. Lonely individuals had less distinct neural representations of self versus others and more rumination-related activity. The DMN was also the strongest predictor of social closeness between individuals based on resting-state functional connectivity. This suggests that chronic social media use—which is associated with increased loneliness—may alter the DMN in ways that affect how individuals process social information and relate to others.

## Dimension 3: Are We Losing Cognitive Abilities That Evolved Over Millennia?

### 3.1 Spatial Memory and Navigation: The GPS Effect

One of the clearest examples of technology-induced cognitive change involves spatial navigation—a skill that has been essential for human survival for hundreds of thousands of years.

**Dahmani & Bohbot (2020)** , in a study published in Scientific Reports, assessed 50 regular drivers using virtual navigation tasks and found that people with greater lifetime GPS experience had **worse spatial memory when navigating without GPS** [37]. Greater lifetime GPS experience was significantly associated with:
- Reduced use of spatial memory strategies (r = -0.20 to -0.22)
- Poorer cognitive mapping (r = -0.22)
- Fewer landmarks encoded (r = -0.21 to -0.26)

A longitudinal follow-up of 13 participants after three years found that greater GPS use between sessions was associated with a **steeper decline in hippocampal-dependent spatial memory** (r = -0.68 for cognitive mapping), even after controlling for baseline performance and initial GPS experience. Importantly, subjective sense of direction was not correlated with lifetime GPS experience (r = 0.07), suggesting that poor navigation did not drive GPS use but rather GPS use caused spatial memory decline.

**Miola et al. (2024)** , in a systematic review and meta-analysis published in the Journal of Environmental Psychology, confirmed that GPS use in learning an environment can potentially lead to **poorer acquisition of spatial knowledge** [38]. The meta-analysis synthesized evidence across multiple studies, finding consistent negative effects of GPS reliance on spatial memory.

**Sugimoto et al. (2022)** directly compared smartphone map use to paper map use for route learning, finding that smartphone map use significantly impairs spatial memory [39]. This suggests that the specific interface design of digital navigation tools—particularly turn-by-turn directions that require minimal cognitive engagement—may be responsible for the degradation of spatial memory.

**Topete et al. (2024)** , in two preregistered studies (total N=249), found that GPS dependence was negatively correlated with both self-reported sense of direction (r = -0.56, p<.001) and objective navigation performance (r = -0.34, p=.008), and positively correlated with spatial anxiety (r = 0.43, p<.001) [40]. Greater use of turn-by-turn directions was specifically associated with poorer sense of direction and higher spatial anxiety. The authors note that people modulate their GPS use based on familiarity with the environment, suggesting that GPS is used to augment rather than replace spatial knowledge, but that less able navigators are more dependent on GPS, creating a self-reinforcing cycle of dependency.

**A 2021 study in Scientific Reports** demonstrated that an alternative GPS design—using auditory beacons that continuously sound from the direction of the destination rather than turn-by-turn directions—can enhance spatial learning and mental map formation [41]. Participants guided by auditory beacons showed significantly better pointing accuracy and lower error in placing points of interest on a map compared to those using turn-by-turn directions. This suggests that the cognitive effects of GPS are not inevitable but are mediated by interface design.

### 3.2 The Google Effect: Digital Amnesia and Transactive Memory

**Gong & Yang (2024)** , in a meta-analysis of 35 studies (total 30,889 participants) published in Frontiers in Public Health, found a moderate but statistically significant overall effect of frequent Internet search behavior on memory and cognition [42]. Key moderators included:
- **Region**: North America showed greater susceptibility than Europe and Asia
- **Device type**: Mobile phone use amplified the effect
- **Prior Internet experience**: Stronger effect for experienced users
- **Knowledge base**: Larger existing knowledge base reduced susceptibility

Gender, age, and sample size did not significantly moderate the effect. The study concludes that intensive Internet search behavior alters how individuals seek and process information, potentially leading to changes in cognitive and memory mechanisms.

**Robert et al. (2022)** , in an empirical study published in Heliyon, found a significant positive relationship between digital amnesia, somatic symptoms, and sleep disorders among youth [43], suggesting that the cognitive offloading associated with digital technology may have broader health implications.

The phenomenon of digital amnesia—the tendency to forget information that can be easily found online—is grounded in the concept of transactive memory. When the Internet serves as a reliable external memory source, the brain may allocate fewer resources to encoding and storing information internally, instead encoding only the location where the information can be found. This is not necessarily maladaptive—it represents an efficient allocation of cognitive resources—but it fundamentally changes the relationship between the individual and knowledge.

### 3.3 Intergenerational Cognitive Differences: Digital Natives vs. Digital Immigrants

**A 2024 study in BMC Public Health** compared problematic smartphone use and distraction across four generations (Baby Boomers, Gen X, Gen Y, Gen Z) [21]. The results showed that younger generations (especially Gen Z) had higher mean scores for problematic use and distraction. However, no significant generational differences were found in well-being, suggesting that while younger generations use devices more intensively, the relationship between use and well-being is not simply a function of age.

**A 2022/2023 study** (ERIC) comparing 220 adults aged 18–57 from Italy and Poland found that Generation Z (18–26 years) showed the highest scores in almost all subscales of problematic smartphone use, distraction, and phubbing [44]. However, a surprising finding was that **Generation X (the older generation) scored higher than younger generations in measures of cognitive failures**—memory failures, errors due to distraction, and cognitive errors in general. The authors suggest that this may reflect a mismatch between the cognitive demands of the digital environment and the cognitive habits of digital immigrants, who did not develop their cognitive strategies in a digital context.

**Kardan et al. (2022)** , in a study published in PLOS Biology, investigated the functional brain architecture underlying sustained attention and working memory in children aged 9–11 compared to adults [45]. They found that **adult network predictors of sustained attention generalized to predict individual differences in children**, but a working memory model that predicted performance in adults **underperformed in children relative to adults**. This suggests that the functional architecture for sustained attention is relatively stable across development, but working memory networks undergo more substantial reorganization. This has implications for how digital media effects may differ across age groups: effects on attention may be more universal, while effects on working memory may be more developmentally specific.

### 3.4 The Default Mode Network and the Internal World

The DMN's role in self-reflection, autobiographical memory, and social cognition makes it particularly relevant to questions about whether we are losing cognitive abilities that evolved over millennia. The DMN is the brain network that supports our capacity for introspection, moral reasoning, and understanding of others' minds—abilities that have been central to human social evolution.

**A 2023 resting-state fMRI study** (Áfra et al., 2023) found that problematic social media use was associated with reduced functional connectivity in the executive control network, language network, and visual networks [5]. The authors note that "to the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to show direct evidence that problematic social media use is inversely associated with the FC in a visual network." This suggests that heavy social media use may alter the brain's basic sensory processing networks, not just higher cognitive networks.

**Chang & Lee (2024)** , in a systematic review of 12 fMRI studies published in PLOS Mental Health, examined functional connectivity changes in the brains of adolescents with Internet addiction [46]. The review found that Internet addiction impacts several neural networks, including a mix of increases and decreases in FC in the default mode network, an overall decrease in FC in the executive control network, and no clear increase or decrease in FC within the salience network and reward pathway. The authors emphasize that the adolescent brain's high plasticity and vulnerability to network reorganization make it particularly susceptible to these effects.

### 3.5 Implications for Human Development

The evidence reviewed above has profound implications for human development across the lifespan.

**For adolescents**, the convergence of findings suggests that:
1. **Structural brain development is being altered**: Screen time and social media use are associated with reduced cortical thickness, changes in gray matter volume, and altered developmental trajectories in regions critical for attention, memory, and emotional regulation.
2. **Emotional regulation systems are being rewired**: The heightened neural sensitivity to social feedback in adolescents, combined with the constant availability of social evaluation on digital platforms, may be shaping the developing brain's emotional circuitry in ways that increase vulnerability to anxiety and depression.
3. **Attention systems are developing differently**: The pattern of reduced neural efficiency and altered functional connectivity in attention networks suggests that the brains of digital natives may be optimized for rapid task-switching and divided attention rather than sustained, focused attention.

**For adults**, the findings indicate that:
1. **Mature brains are not immune to change**: Even in adults with fully developed brains, problematic smartphone and social media use is associated with altered functional connectivity, reduced gray matter volume, and changes in attentional dynamics.
2. **Emotional regulation is affected**: The relationship between smartphone use, amygdala connectivity, and emotion regulation difficulties demonstrates that digital engagement can affect emotional processing even in mature systems.
3. **Cognitive offloading is reshaping memory**: The GPS and Google effects demonstrate that adults are adapting their cognitive strategies to rely on digital tools for functions that were previously internal, with consequences for spatial memory and information retention.

**Across both groups**, several themes emerge:
1. **The effects are not uniformly negative**: Some studies find positive associations between certain types of screen use (e.g., video gaming) and cognitive performance, and non-problematic Internet use may actually support mood regulation.
2. **Individual differences matter tremendously**: Effects vary based on personality traits, pre-existing vulnerabilities, usage patterns, and developmental stage.
3. **The relationship is bidirectional**: Brain changes may increase vulnerability to problematic use, which in turn drives further neural changes, creating self-reinforcing cycles.
4. **Effect sizes are generally small to moderate**: While statistically significant, the effects reported in most studies are modest, suggesting that digital media is one factor among many shaping brain development and cognitive function.

---

## Conclusion

The research conducted between 2021 and 2026 provides compelling evidence that smartphones and social media are indeed rewiring human brains and altering consciousness, but the effects are nuanced, developmentally specific, and highly dependent on individual and contextual factors.

**Adolescents** are more vulnerable to the effects of digital connectivity because their brains are undergoing rapid development, particularly in regions involved in social cognition, reward processing, and cognitive control. The developing brain's plasticity makes it more susceptible to both the positive and negative effects of digital engagement. The evidence suggests that heavy social media use during adolescence may alter the developmental trajectory of brain systems involved in attention, memory, and emotional regulation, with potential long-term consequences for mental health and cognitive function.

**Adults** show different patterns of vulnerability. Their mature brains are less susceptible to developmental disruption, but they are not immune to functional and structural changes associated with problematic use. The evidence points to altered functional connectivity, reduced neural efficiency, and changes in attentional dynamics even in adults. The cognitive offloading to digital tools—GPS for navigation, search engines for memory, social media for social connection—represents a fundamental shift in how adults engage with the world and with their own cognitive capacities.

The reshaping of fundamental human experiences—solitude, boredom, deep thinking—is perhaps the most profound finding. The evidence suggests that constant connectivity may be eroding our capacity for comfortable solitude, paradoxically increasing boredom while providing endless stimulation, and undermining the sustained attention necessary for deep reading and contemplative thought. These changes have implications not just for individual cognitive function but for the quality of human experience and the nature of human consciousness.

Whether we are "losing" cognitive abilities that evolved over millennia is a question that requires careful framing. In some domains—spatial navigation, internal memory for easily searchable information—the evidence suggests that specific cognitive abilities are being used less and may be atrophying. However, this may be better understood as a cognitive reorganization rather than a simple loss. Humans are adaptive; we are developing new cognitive strategies optimized for a digital environment rather than for the hunter-gatherer environment in which our brains evolved. The question is whether this reorganization is serving us well, and for whom.

The evidence strongly suggests that the effects of digital technology on the brain are not deterministic. Design choices matter: auditory beacons can support spatial learning while turn-by-turn GPS impairs it; active social media use can support adaptive emotion regulation while passive scrolling undermines it; reading on paper supports deeper comprehension while reading on screens encourages skimming. This suggests that the future of human cognition in the digital age is not fixed but can be shaped by intentional design, education, and individual choices.
