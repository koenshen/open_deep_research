# Comprehensive Comparison of SEC Climate Disclosure Rule, EU CSRD, and California SB-219 Climate Disclosure Legislation

## Executive Summary

As of July 2026, the global ESG reporting landscape is defined by three major regulatory frameworks: the U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC) Climate Disclosure Rule (adopted March 2024, currently stayed), the European Union's Corporate Sustainability Reporting Directive (CSRD) (fully operational with first-wave reporting completed), and California's climate disclosure package (SB-253, SB-261, and SB-219). These frameworks differ substantially in scope, materiality philosophy, verification requirements, timelines, and enforcement mechanisms. Multinational enterprises face significant compliance challenges due to overlapping requirements, distinct materiality frameworks, and varying assurance standards. This report provides a comprehensive comparative analysis drawing on official regulatory texts, government impact assessments, and industry analyses from leading consulting firms.

---

## 1. Overview of Each Framework

### 1.1 SEC Climate Disclosure Rule

The **SEC Climate Disclosure Rule** (Release Nos. 33-11275; 34-99678) was adopted on March 6, 2024, requiring SEC registrants to disclose climate-related risks reasonably likely to have a material impact on their business, results of operations, or financial condition [1]. The final rule was significantly scaled back from the March 2022 proposal: Scope 3 emissions were removed entirely, Scope 1 and 2 emissions are required only for Large Accelerated Filers (LAFs) and Accelerated Filers (AFs) and only if material, and smaller reporting companies are exempt from GHG disclosure requirements.

**Critical Legal Status:** On April 4, 2024, the U.S. Court of Appeals for the Eighth Circuit issued an **administrative stay** of the rule nationwide, pending judicial review in *Iowa v. SEC* (No. 24-1522) [4]. As of July 2026, the stay remains in effect, tolling all compliance deadlines. The SEC has voluntarily stayed enforcement during litigation. The case raises fundamental questions about SEC statutory authority under the Securities Act of 1933 and Exchange Act of 1934, First Amendment compelled speech concerns, and whether the rule is arbitrary and capricious under the Administrative Procedure Act. Companies should monitor the Eighth Circuit for a merits decision and potential Supreme Court appeal.

The rule applies to all domestic and foreign SEC registrants, with requirements phased and tiered based on filer status. Key disclosure areas include climate-related risk governance, risk management processes, strategy impacts, targets and goals, Scope 1/2 GHG emissions (if material), and financial statement effects of severe weather events.

### 1.2 EU Corporate Sustainability Reporting Directive (CSRD)

The **CSRD** — formally **Directive (EU) 2022/2464** — entered into force on January 5, 2023, and represents the most significant expansion of corporate sustainability reporting in EU history [2]. It replaces and substantially expands the previous Non-Financial Reporting Directive (NFRD), expanding the scope from approximately 11,700 companies to over 50,000. Member states were required to transpose the directive into national law by July 6, 2024.

The **European Sustainability Reporting Standards (ESRS)** were adopted as **Commission Delegated Regulation (EU) 2023/2772** on July 31, 2023, published in the Official Journal on December 22, 2023 [3]. The ESRS framework includes cross-cutting standards (ESRS 1 on general requirements, ESRS 2 on general disclosures), five environmental standards (E1-E5 covering climate, pollution, water, biodiversity, circular economy), four social standards (S1-S4 covering own workforce, value chain workers, affected communities, consumers), and one governance standard (G1 on business conduct).

**ESRS E1 (Climate Change)** is unique in having "presumed materiality" — companies must report on E1 unless they can demonstrate climate change is not material, which is exceedingly difficult. E1 requires disclosure of transition plans, GHG emissions (Scopes 1, 2, and 3), energy consumption and mix, GHG intensity, carbon credits, internal carbon pricing, and financial effects from physical and transition risks.

**Implementation Status as of July 2026:**
- **Phase 1 (FY 2024):** Large public-interest entities already subject to NFRD — reports published in 2025, now complete
- **Phase 2 (FY 2025):** Large companies not previously subject to NFRD — reports being published in 2026 (just completing)
- **Phase 3 (FY 2026):** Listed SMEs — first reporting year underway
- **Phase 4 (FY 2028):** Non-EU companies with >€150M net turnover in the EU — preparatory phase ongoing

### 1.3 California Climate Disclosure Legislation (SB-253, SB-261, and SB-219)

California's climate disclosure regime was established by **SB-253 (Climate Corporate Data Accountability Act)** and **SB-261 (Climate-Related Financial Risk Act)**, signed into law by Governor Gavin Newsom on October 7, 2023 [7][8]. **SB-219** was introduced as a clean-up and amendment bill to address implementation timelines, scope clarifications, and technical adjustments.

**SB-253** requires U.S.-based companies "doing business in California" with total annual revenues exceeding $1 billion to publicly disclose Scope 1, 2, and 3 GHG emissions. The California Air Resources Board (CARB) is tasked with developing implementing regulations.

**SB-261** requires companies "doing business in California" with total annual revenues exceeding $500 million to prepare biennial reports disclosing climate-related financial risks and adaptation measures, consistent with the TCFD framework.

**SB-219** (proposed amendments) seeks to delay compliance deadlines, clarify the definition of "doing business in California," add a safe harbor for Scope 3 emissions disclosures made in good faith, and allow companies to use reports prepared for other jurisdictions to satisfy California requirements.

**Current Status as of July 2026:** Both SB-253 and SB-261 face legal challenges from business groups (including the U.S. Chamber of Commerce) arguing First Amendment violations, federal preemption, and exceeding California's authority. CARB's rulemaking process has been ongoing. The user should verify whether SB-219 was enacted into law and the final status of court challenges. Companies should be preparing for California compliance regardless of the SEC rule's fate, as California's laws are not preempted by federal securities regulation.

## 3. Real-World Enterprise Compliance Challenges and Cost Estimates

### 3.1 SEC Climate Disclosure Rule

**Challenge 1: Legal Uncertainty and Preparatory Costs**
The ongoing Eighth Circuit stay creates significant uncertainty. Companies must decide whether to invest in compliance infrastructure proactively or wait for the litigation outcome. The SEC's 2010 Guidance Regarding Disclosure Related to Climate Change remains in effect, requiring disclosure of material climate risks under existing rules (Regulation S-K Item 105 and Item 303). However, the scope of required disclosures under the 2010 Guidance is less prescriptive than the 2024 rule.

**Cost Estimate:** The SEC's regulatory impact analysis estimated first-year compliance costs for Large Accelerated Filers at $530,000 to $1.1 million, with annual ongoing costs of $280,000 to $480,000 [1]. Industry estimates are higher: Deloitte estimated $500,000 to $2 million for first-year implementation depending on existing infrastructure; PwC estimated $1 million to $3 million for LAFs including systems, controls, and assurance readiness; McKinsey estimated $300,000 to $1.5 million annually per LAF [11]. These estimates are now moot due to the stay, but companies that have already invested in voluntary TCFD/ISSB-aligned reporting will face lower incremental costs if the rule is ultimately upheld.

**Challenge 2: Scope 1/2 Data Collection and Internal Controls**
Even though the rule is stayed, companies that will eventually need to comply (if the rule is upheld) must establish robust GHG data collection systems, internal controls over emissions reporting, and documentation processes. Many companies lack the necessary infrastructure, particularly for Scope 2 market-based emissions calculations.

**Cost Estimate:** ICF/ERM consulting estimated $200,000 to $800,000 annually for Scope 1/2 monitoring and reporting for LAFs [11]. The SEC estimated $130,000 to $180,000 annually for subsequent years. The variance reflects differences in company size, industry complexity, and existing data systems.

**Challenge 3: Assurance Provider Capacity and Cost**
The rule requires phased assurance from limited to reasonable for LAFs. The market for qualified GHG assurance providers is nascent, with limited capacity. The SEC estimated limited assurance costs at $50,000 to $100,000 annually for LAFs and $100,000 to $200,000 for reasonable assurance. However, given supply constraints, actual costs may be significantly higher, particularly for specialized industries (oil and gas, chemicals, manufacturing).

### 3.2 EU CSRD

**Challenge 1: Double Materiality Assessment (DMA) Complexity**
The DMA is the foundational step for CSRD compliance, requiring companies to assess both financial materiality (outside-in) and impact materiality (inside-out) across all ESRS topical standards. Many companies in Phase 1 (FY 2024) struggled with the methodology, particularly in threshold-setting, stakeholder engagement, and documentation. The process requires mapping sustainability matters across the entire value chain, assessing severity (scale, scope, irremediability) and likelihood for potential impacts, and integrating stakeholder perspectives. EFRAG's Implementation Guidance IG 1 provides a step-by-step framework, but practical application remains challenging [10].

**Cost Estimate:** The European Commission's original Impact Assessment estimated first-year compliance costs of €1,800 to €4,000 for large companies — an estimate widely criticized as unrealistically low by industry, accounting firms, and academic commentators [4]. The European Court of Auditors acknowledged significant underestimation in its Special Report on EU sustainability reporting [17]. Industry estimates are substantially higher: Deloitte surveys (2023-2024) found first-year implementation costs of €200,000 to €500,000+ per large company including systems, data, and assurance [12]; PwC estimated €150,000 to €400,000 [13]; KPMG estimated €100,000 to €300,000 for initial implementation with ongoing costs 30-50% lower [14]. McKinsey estimated 50,000 to 100,000+ person-hours for large multinationals, translating to €1 million+ total cost [15].

**Challenge 2: Value Chain Data Collection (Scope 3 and Social Data)**
ESRS requires reporting across the entire value chain, including Scope 3 GHG emissions (E1-4) and social data for workers in the value chain (S2). Many companies lack direct visibility into their supply chains, particularly for Tier 2 and Tier 3 suppliers. Data quality issues are pervasive, and companies must rely on estimates, industry averages, or supplier surveys. The Phase 1 experience revealed that value chain reporting was one of the most challenging areas, with many companies unable to provide comprehensive Scope 3 data.

**Cost Estimate:** EFRAG's Implementation Guidance IG 2 on value chain provides assistance, but the cost of collecting, validating, and assuring value chain data is significant. Companies with complex, global supply chains face the highest costs. The European Commission's estimates did not adequately account for these costs, which industry analysts estimate at €100,000 to €500,000+ annually for large multinationals.

**Challenge 3: XBRL Digital Tagging**
The CSRD mandates XBRL digital tagging of all sustainability statements, a technically demanding requirement that many companies were unprepared for. Phase 1 implementation revealed that XBRL implementation was slower than expected, with formatting, taxonomy mapping, and software integration challenges. Companies must invest in XBRL-enabled reporting software or engage third-party providers.

**Cost Estimate:** XBRL implementation costs vary by company size and complexity but are estimated at €50,000 to €200,000 for initial setup, with ongoing annual costs of €20,000 to €50,000 for maintenance and updates.

**Challenge 4: Assurance Provider Shortage and Capacity Constraints**
The CSRD requires mandatory limited assurance for all in-scope entities, with a target of moving to reasonable assurance after a 2028 feasibility study. However, the market for qualified sustainability assurance providers is severely constrained. The shortage of professionals with dual expertise in auditing and sustainability is driving up costs and creating bottlenecks. The IAASB's ISSA 5000 (finalized late 2024) provides a dedicated sustainability assurance standard, but training and accreditation of assurance providers will take time [9].

**Cost Estimate:** Assurance costs are estimated at €30,000 to €100,000+ annually per entity for limited assurance, with reasonable assurance potentially 2-3 times higher. The European Commission's Impact Assessment did not separately estimate assurance costs.

### 3.3 California SB-219/SB-253/SB-261

**Challenge 1: Scope 3 Emissions Data Collection**
California's SB-253 requires Scope 3 emissions disclosure, unlike the SEC rule. This is one of the most challenging aspects of compliance, particularly for companies with complex supply chains. Scope 3 emissions typically constitute the majority of a company's GHG footprint (often 70-90% of total emissions) and require data from suppliers, customers, and other value chain partners. The proposed SB-219 safe harbor for good-faith Scope 3 disclosures provides some liability protection, but companies must still demonstrate reasonable efforts to collect and verify data.

**Cost Estimate:** Industry estimates for Scope 3 data collection and reporting range from $500,000 to $2 million+ per company depending on supply chain complexity. This is significantly higher than Scope 1/2 costs ($100,000 to $500,000). The California Air Resources Board's regulatory impact analysis (when available) will provide government estimates, but industry analyses suggest these are the most expensive component of California compliance.

**Challenge 2: Definitional Uncertainty and Legal Challenges**
The definition of "doing business in California" is subject to interpretation and legal challenge. Companies with minimal physical presence but significant revenue from California customers may be subject to the laws. The proposed SB-219 amendments seek to clarify the definition, but uncertainty remains. The ongoing legal challenges to SB-253 and SB-261 (including the U.S. Chamber of Commerce lawsuit) create additional uncertainty about whether, when, and in what form these laws will be enforced.

**Cost Estimate:** Legal and compliance advisory costs for navigating definitional uncertainty and monitoring litigation developments are estimated at $50,000 to $200,000 annually per company, in addition to direct compliance costs.

**Challenge 3: Dual Reporting Burden (California vs. SEC/CSRD)**
Companies subject to both California and SEC rules (if the SEC rule is ultimately upheld) or California and CSRD face the burden of preparing reports under different frameworks. California's rules require Scope 3 emissions (which the SEC rule does not) and have different assurance timelines. Companies must track and reconcile these differences, potentially preparing separate reports or a single comprehensive report that satisfies multiple jurisdictions.

**Cost Estimate:** The incremental cost of dual compliance is estimated at 20-40% above the cost of complying with a single framework, due to the need for additional analysis, reconciliation, and report preparation.

## 4. Strategic Implications for Multinational Enterprises Managing Overlapping ESG Requirements

### 4.1 The Compliance Burden Trilemma

Multinational enterprises face a "trilemma" of overlapping, sometimes conflicting regulatory requirements. The three frameworks differ fundamentally in their materiality philosophy, scope, and assurance requirements:

| Dimension | SEC (if upheld) | CSRD | California |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Materiality** | Financial only | Double materiality | No materiality filter (SB-253); Financial (SB-261) |
| **Scope 3** | No | Yes | Yes |
| **Assurance** | Phased, LAFs only | Mandatory, all entities | Phased, all entities |
| **Geographic Scope** | US SEC registrants | EU + non-EU with EU presence | "Doing business in California" |

A company subject to all three frameworks cannot simply prepare one report that satisfies all requirements. The CSRD requires the most comprehensive disclosure (double materiality, all three scopes, mandatory assurance, XBRL tagging). California's SB-253 is more demanding than the SEC rule on Scope 3 but less demanding on assurance (limited vs. reasonable). The SEC rule (if upheld) is the least demanding but uses a different materiality standard.

### 4.2 Strategic Recommendation: Adopt the "Highest Common Denominator" Approach

The most cost-effective strategy for multinational enterprises is to prepare for the **most stringent framework** (CSRD) and then tailor disclosures for the other jurisdictions. This approach:

1. **Reduces duplication:** CSRD compliance requires the most comprehensive data collection, including double materiality assessment, full Scope 1, 2, and 3 emissions, and value chain analysis. This data can be repurposed for California and SEC (if upheld) compliance.

2. **Creates a single source of truth:** Build a centralized ESG data management system that captures all required data points across all frameworks. This reduces the risk of inconsistencies and enables efficient report generation.

3. **Aligns with global best practices:** The CSRD's double materiality framework is increasingly seen as the gold standard for sustainability reporting, with the ISSB's IFRS S1 and S2 standards also moving toward financial materiality plus impact considerations. The Task Force on Nature-related Financial Disclosures (TNFD) and other frameworks also recommend double materiality.

### 4.3 Key Strategic Actions

**Action 1: Conduct a Comprehensive Gap Analysis**
Map the company's current disclosure practices against the requirements of all three frameworks. Identify gaps in data collection, verification, governance, and reporting. Prioritize CSRD compliance as the baseline, then assess incremental requirements for California and SEC.

**Action 2: Invest in ESG Data Management Infrastructure**
Implement a robust ESG data management platform (e.g., Workiva, Persefoni, Salesforce Net Zero Cloud) that can handle multiple reporting frameworks, automate data collection, and support XBRL tagging. The upfront investment is significant (estimated $500,000 to $2 million for large enterprises) but will reduce ongoing compliance costs.

**Action 3: Build Internal Capacity and Governance**
Establish a cross-functional ESG reporting team with expertise in sustainability, finance, legal, and risk management. Ensure board-level oversight of climate-related risks and reporting. The shortage of qualified sustainability professionals means companies should invest in training and upskilling existing staff, particularly in the finance and audit functions.

**Action 4: Engage with Assurance Providers Early**
The market for sustainability assurance is constrained. Companies should engage with qualified assurance providers (PCAOB-registered firms, AICPA members, or EU-accredited IASPs) early to understand requirements, timelines, and costs. The CSRD's move from limited to reasonable assurance will require more rigorous controls and documentation.

**Action 5: Monitor Legal and Regulatory Developments**
All three frameworks are subject to legal challenges, potential revisions, and ongoing implementation guidance. Companies should establish a regulatory monitoring function or engage external counsel to track:
- Eighth Circuit decision in *Iowa v. SEC* and potential Supreme Court review
- CARB rulemaking and legal challenges to California's laws
- European Commission simplification proposals and ESRS amendments
- EFRAG implementation guidance updates

**Action 6: Prepare for the "Worst Case" Scenario**
Given the legal uncertainty, companies should prepare for the scenario where all three frameworks are fully enforceable. This means:
- Implementing Scope 1, 2, and 3 data collection systems now (California and CSRD require this)
- Establishing double materiality assessment processes (CSRD requires this)
- Preparing for mandatory assurance (CSRD requires this now; California requires it by 2030)
- Building XBRL tagging capability (CSRD requires this)

### 4.4 Cost-Benefit Analysis of Proactive Compliance

| Approach | Estimated Cost (Large Multinational) | Risk Profile |
|---|---|---|
| **Wait-and-see** (minimal investment until legal clarity) | $0.5M – $1M annually (legal monitoring, basic data) | High — risk of rushed implementation, potential penalties, reputational damage |
| **Partial preparation** (CSRD baseline only) | $2M – $5M first year; $1M – $2M ongoing | Moderate — covers most requirements but may have gaps for California/SEC |
| **Full proactive compliance** (all three frameworks) | $5M – $15M+ first year; $2M – $5M ongoing | Low — ready for any outcome, can leverage first-mover advantage |

The "partial preparation" approach (CSRD baseline) is the most cost-effective for most multinational enterprises, as it covers the most demanding framework and can be adapted for other jurisdictions. However, companies with significant California operations or SEC registrant status should consider full proactive compliance.

### 4.5 The Role of the ISSB as a Global Baseline

The International Sustainability Standards Board (ISSB) — IFRS S1 (General Requirements) and IFRS S2 (Climate) — was designed as a global baseline for sustainability reporting [10]. The SEC rule was substantially aligned with ISSB (though narrower), and the CSRD's ESRS were designed to be interoperable with ISSB. The California framework also references TCFD, which ISSB has now consolidated.

Companies should align their internal reporting with ISSB standards as a "common language" that can be translated into the specific requirements of each jurisdiction. This reduces duplication and enables efficient compliance across multiple frameworks.

---

## 5. Conclusion

The ESG reporting landscape as of July 2026 is characterized by three major frameworks with distinct scopes, materiality philosophies, and verification requirements. The CSRD is the most comprehensive and is fully operational, with Phase 1 and 2 reporting complete. California's SB-253 and SB-261 are moving forward despite legal challenges, requiring Scope 3 emissions disclosure and phased assurance. The SEC rule remains stayed by the Eighth Circuit, creating significant uncertainty for U.S. registrants.

Multinational enterprises should adopt a **"highest common denominator"** strategy, preparing for the most stringent framework (CSRD) and tailoring for other jurisdictions. This approach minimizes duplication, reduces compliance risk, and positions companies for the inevitable convergence of global sustainability reporting standards. Investment in ESG data infrastructure, internal capacity, and assurance readiness is essential, regardless of the ultimate legal fate of any single framework.

The trend toward mandatory, assured, and digitally tagged sustainability reporting is irreversible. Companies that invest now in robust systems and processes will be better positioned to navigate the evolving regulatory landscape and meet stakeholder expectations for transparency and accountability.
