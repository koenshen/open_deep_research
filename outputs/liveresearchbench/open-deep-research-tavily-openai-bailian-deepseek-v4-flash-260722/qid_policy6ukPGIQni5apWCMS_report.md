# Multi-State Biometric Privacy Compliance Briefing: Illinois BIPA, Texas CUBI, and Washington RCW 19.375

**Prepared for:** Privacy Compliance Manager  
**Date:** July 22, 2026  
**Status:** Current as of effective date, with caveats for 2025–2026 amendments where noted

## Executive Summary

This briefing provides a comprehensive comparison of the three most significant state biometric privacy laws in the United States: Illinois' Biometric Information Privacy Act (BIPA, 740 ILCS 14), Texas' Capture or Use of Biometric Identifier Act (CUBI, Tex. Bus. & Comm. Code Chapter 503), and Washington's biometric privacy law (RCW 19.375). All three laws are enforceable as of July 22, 2026, with Texas having undergone the most significant recent transformation through the addition of a private right of action in 2023.

Enterprises operating across multiple states face a complex compliance landscape characterized by divergent consent requirements, varying penalty structures, and inconsistent technology restrictions. Illinois BIPA remains the most aggressive and litigated statute, Texas CUBI has rapidly emerged as a high-risk jurisdiction following the 2023 private right of action addition, and Washington's law occupies a middle ground with its explicit written consent requirement and $1,000 per violation statutory damages.

## Section 1: Scope and Applicability of Each Law

### 1.1 Illinois Biometric Information Privacy Act (740 ILCS 14)

**Citation and Enactment:** 740 ILCS 14/1 et seq., effective 2008.

**Entities Covered:** Applies to any "private entity" that collects, captures, purchases, receives through trade, or otherwise obtains biometric identifiers or biometric information. Government entities, state agencies, and financial institutions subject to the Gramm-Leach-Bliley Act are exempt under Section 40.

**Biometric Data Covered:** The statute defines "biometric identifier" as a retina or iris scan, fingerprint, voiceprint, or scan of hand or face geometry. "Biometric information" is defined broadly as any information, regardless of how it is captured, converted, stored, or shared, based on an individual's biometric identifier used to identify an individual. Expressly excluded are writing samples, written signatures, photographs, demographic data, tattoo descriptions, and physical descriptions such as height, weight, hair color, or eye color.

**Who Is Protected:** Both consumers and employees are protected. The statute does not distinguish between these categories; any individual whose biometric identifier or biometric information is collected is protected. The Illinois Supreme Court in *Rosenbach v. Six Flags Entertainment Corporation* (2019) confirmed that a person need not allege actual injury beyond the statutory violation to have standing.

**Key Definitions (Section 5):** The statute underwent clarification via SB 2979 (2023) regarding the definition of "biometric data." The definition of "written release" under Section 15 includes a release executed by the subject or their legally authorized representative.

### 1.2 Texas Capture or Use of Biometric Identifier Act (Tex. Bus. & Comm. Code Chapter 503)

**Citation and Enactment:** Tex. Bus. & Comm. Code § 503.001, effective 2009. Significantly amended by HB 3160, effective September 1, 2023.

**Entities Covered:** Applies to any "private entity" that possesses a biometric identifier of an individual or captures a biometric identifier for a commercial purpose. Government entities are not covered by Chapter 503. The statute covers a broad range of entities including retailers, employers, healthcare providers, technology companies, and any business collecting biometric data.

**Biometric Data Covered:** The statute defines "biometric identifier" as a retina or iris scan, fingerprint, voiceprint, or record of hand or face geometry. Critical distinction: the statute does not cover a "photograph or a video recording" unless the photograph or video is used to generate a biometric identifier. DNA, gait analysis, keystroke dynamics, and behavioral biometrics are not covered.

**Who Is Protected:** Any individual whose biometric identifier is captured or possessed by a covered private entity. There is no residency requirement—unlike Illinois BIPA, Texas CUBI protects any person whose biometric data is collected in Texas or by a Texas entity. This means a California resident visiting Texas and scanned at a Houston retailer is protected.

**2023 Amendment Impact:** HB 3160 added a private right of action, which fundamentally transformed the law from an Attorney General-only enforcement regime to a high-litigation-risk statute.

### 1.3 Washington Biometric Privacy Law (RCW 19.375)

**Citation and Enactment:** RCW 19.375 (Washington Biometric Identifiers Act), effective 2017.

**Entities Covered:** Applies to any "private entity" that collects, captures, enrolls, stores, or uses biometric identifiers. Government entities are not covered. Financial institutions subject to GLBA are exempt if the biometric data is collected as part of a financial transaction or account.

**Biometric Data Covered:** The statute defines "biometric identifier" as data generated by automatic measurements of an individual's biological characteristics, including fingerprints, voiceprints, iris scans, retina scans, hand geometry, and facial geometry. Expressly excluded are writing samples (signatures), photographs (when used in isolation), demographic data, physical descriptions, and biometric data used for healthcare treatment or payment (covered by HIPAA).

**Who Is Protected:** The law protects "individuals" with no explicit carve-out for employees versus consumers. The law applies to any biometric data collected from a natural person. The applicability to the employment context has been a subject of litigation but no definitive Washington Supreme Court ruling has been issued.

## Section 2: Multi-Column Comparison Table

### 2.1 Consent Mechanisms

| Aspect | Illinois BIPA | Texas CUBI | Washington RCW 19.375 |
|--------|--------------|------------|----------------------|
| **Notice Requirement** | Must inform subject in writing that biometric data is being collected, stored, or used | Must provide notice before capturing biometric identifier; must inform of specific purpose and duration | Must provide notice that biometric data is being collected, stored, and used |
| **Consent Type** | Written release executed by the subject or legally authorized representative | "Consent" required but statute does not explicitly require written consent (best practice: written) | Informed written consent or electronic signature per ESIGN Act |
| **Opt-In vs. Opt-Out** | Opt-in (written release required) | Opt-in (affirmative consent before capture) | Opt-in (written/electronic consent before collection) |
| **Employer-Specific Rules** | Same consent requirements apply to employers; no employment carve-out | No separate employer provision; consent validity in employment context is litigated | No specific employment exception; plain language suggests workplace collection is covered |
| **Purpose Specification** | Must inform of specific purpose and length of term | Must inform of specific purpose and duration | Must inform of specific purpose |
| **Public Policy Requirement** | Must develop written policy, made available to public, establishing retention schedule and destruction guidelines | No explicit public policy requirement | Must develop written policy, made available to public, establishing retention schedule and destruction guidelines |

**Key Consent Differences:**

- **Illinois** requires a "written release" executed by the subject, which is the most formal consent requirement among the three states.
- **Texas** does not explicitly require written consent in the statutory text, though best practice demands written consent given the litigation environment.
- **Washington** requires "informed written consent" or an electronic signature, which is a middle ground—formal but allows electronic alternatives.

### 2.2 Penalty Structures

| Aspect | Illinois BIPA | Texas CUBI | Washington RCW 19.375 |
|--------|--------------|------------|----------------------|
| **Negligent Violation** | $1,000 per violation | $2,000 per violation | $1,000 per violation |
| **Intentional/Reckless Violation** | $5,000 per violation | $10,000 per violation | $1,000 per violation (no tiered structure) |
| **Actual Damages** | Available as alternative to statutory damages | Available in addition to statutory damages | Available if greater than statutory damages |
| **Aggregate Cap** | No cap on aggregate damages | No cap on aggregate damages | No cap on aggregate damages |
| **Attorney's Fees** | Available to prevailing party | Available to prevailing party | Available to prevailing party |
| **AG Enforcement Penalties** | AG can seek civil penalties | AG can seek up to $25,000 per violation plus injunctive relief | AG can seek civil penalties and injunctive relief |

**Critical Penalty Exposure Analysis:**

**Illinois BIPA:** After *Cothron v. White Castle* (2023), each scan of a fingerprint can constitute a separate violation. An employee who clocks in/out twice daily for 250 days a year over 5 years generates 2,500 separate violations. At $1,000 per negligent violation, that is $2.5 million per employee. At $5,000 per intentional/reckless violation, that is $12.5 million per employee. In a class action with hundreds of employees, exposure can reach billions.

**Texas CUBI:** With $2,000 per negligent violation and $10,000 per intentional/reckless violation, and no cap, the exposure is similarly massive. The Texas AG's $1.4 billion settlement with Meta (2024) demonstrates the scale of potential liability.

**Washington RCW 19.375:** At $1,000 per violation with no tiered structure, and no cap, the exposure is still significant but the lack of a "per scan" ruling comparable to *Cothron* creates some uncertainty. However, each collection or use without consent could be a separate violation.

### 2.3 Private Right of Action

| Aspect | Illinois BIPA | Texas CUBI | Washington RCW 19.375 |
|--------|--------------|------------|----------------------|
| **Private Right of Action** | Yes, always available | Yes, added by HB 3160 (effective Sept 1, 2023) | Yes, always available |
| **Who Can Sue** | Any person aggrieved by a violation | Any person whose biometric identifier was captured in violation of the statute | Any person aggrieved by a violation |
| **Standing Requirement** | No actual injury required (*Rosenbach* 2019) | No requirement to prove actual injury | No requirement to prove actual harm beyond statutory violation |
| **Class Actions** | Yes, extensively used | Yes, class actions available | Yes, class actions available (subject to court interpretation) |
| **Statute of Limitations** | 5 years (735 ILCS 5/13-205, *McDonald v. Symphony Bronzeville Park* 2023) | 2–4 years (debated; general residual period applies) | Not explicitly defined in statute; likely 2–3 years |
| **Pre-Suit Requirements** | 30-day cure period (added 2024) | No pre-suit notice or cure period | No pre-suit notice or cure period |

**Key Private Right of Action Differences:**

- **Illinois** has the most developed class action landscape, with the Illinois Supreme Court providing clear guidance on standing (*Rosenbach*), per-violation accrual (*Cothron*), and statute of limitations (*McDonald*).
- **Texas** is the newest private right of action (2023), creating a rapidly evolving litigation landscape with significant uncertainty around class certification and statute of limitations.
- **Washington** has a private right of action but less developed case law, creating uncertainty about how courts will interpret key provisions.

### 2.4 Technology Restrictions

| Aspect | Illinois BIPA | Texas CUBI | Washington RCW 19.375 |
|--------|--------------|------------|----------------------|
| **Data Types Covered** | Biometric identifiers (retina/iris, fingerprint, voiceprint, hand/face geometry) + biometric information derived from identifiers | Biometric identifiers (retina/iris, fingerprint, voiceprint, hand/face geometry) | Biometric identifiers (fingerprints, voiceprints, iris/retina scans, hand/face geometry) |
| **Prohibited Sales** | Cannot sell, lease, trade, or otherwise profit from biometric data | Cannot sell, lease, trade, or otherwise profit from biometric identifiers | Cannot sell, lease, trade, or otherwise profit from biometric identifiers |
| **Disclosure Restrictions** | Cannot disclose without consent (exceptions: financial transaction, legal requirement, identity verification) | Cannot disclose to third party without consent (exceptions: service provider for financial transaction, legal requirement, individual consent) | Cannot disclose without consent (exceptions: legal requirement, financial transaction, contractor/agent for service) |
| **Retention Limit** | Must destroy when purpose satisfied or within 3 years of last interaction, whichever occurs first | Must destroy within 1 year after purpose for collection expires | Must destroy within reasonable time, or within 3 years of last interaction, whichever is earlier |
| **Destruction Policy** | Written policy required, made available to public | No explicit policy requirement | Written policy required, made available to public |
| **Security Standards** | No specific statutory standard | "Reasonable care" standard; must protect as well as other confidential information | No specific statutory standard |
| **Service Provider Exception** | Limited; disclosure requires consent or specific statutory exception | Yes, for completing financial transactions at individual's request | Yes, for contractors/agents providing service, with contractual compliance requirement |

**Key Technology Restriction Differences:**

- **Texas** has the shortest retention period (1 year after purpose expires) compared to Illinois and Washington (3 years after last interaction).
- **Illinois** has the broadest data coverage, including "biometric information" derived from identifiers, which captures more data than the other states.
- **Texas** has the most explicit "reasonable care" security standard, while Illinois and Washington have no specific statutory security requirements.
- **Washington** explicitly requires contractual compliance obligations for contractors and agents handling biometric data.

## Section 3: Real-World Enforcement Cases and Class Action Examples

### 3.1 Illinois BIPA Cases

**Landmark Supreme Court Rulings:**

*Rosenbach v. Six Flags Entertainment Corporation* (2019 IL 123186): The Illinois Supreme Court held that a person whose biometric data is collected in violation of BIPA's notice/consent provisions is "aggrieved" and has standing to sue regardless of whether they suffered any actual injury or adverse effect. This ruling opened the floodgates for BIPA class actions by eliminating the need to prove harm.

*Cothron v. White Castle Systems, Inc.* (2023 IL 128004): The Illinois Supreme Court held that a separate claim accrues under Sections 15(b) and 15(d) each time a private entity scans a person's biometric identifier or transmits such data to a third party. This dramatically increased potential damages exposure for defendants using fingerprint time clocks, potentially reaching billions of dollars for large employers.

*McDonald v. Symphony Bronzeville Park LLC* (2023 IL 128495): The Illinois Supreme Court held that the 5-year statute of limitations under 735 ILCS 5/13-205 applies to BIPA claims, giving plaintiffs a longer window to bring claims than the 1-year or 2-year limitations periods applicable to other privacy torts.

**Class Action Contexts:**

- **Retail:** *Rosenbach* itself involved a Six Flags amusement park collecting fingerprints from season pass holders. Retailers using facial recognition for loss prevention or customer analytics have been frequent targets.

- **Healthcare:** Hospitals and clinics using biometric data collection practices have faced BIPA lawsuits. The healthcare context is particularly complex due to HIPAA interactions.

- **Workplace/Employment:** This is the most active area of litigation. *Cothron v. White Castle* is the leading case. Employers using fingerprint or hand-geometry scanners for timekeeping have been the primary targets. Major settlements include:
  - *Facebook/Meta*: $650 million settlement (2021) for facial recognition tag suggestions
  - *BNSF Railway*: $75 million settlement (2022) for fingerprint time clock violations
  - Various fast-food chains, manufacturers, and healthcare systems have faced class actions

### 3.2 Texas CUBI Cases

**Pre-2023 Attorney General Enforcement (Before Private Right of Action):**

*Texas v. Facebook/Meta (2022):* The Texas AG sued Meta over its use of facial recognition technology on user photos without consent, alleging violations of both CUBI and the Texas Deceptive Trade Practices Act. This resulted in a landmark $1.4 billion settlement (announced 2024)—one of the largest state privacy settlements ever.

*Texas v. Google (2022):* The AG sued Google over its collection of biometric data (voiceprints and face geometry) through Google Photos, Google Assistant, and Nest devices. This case was ongoing as of 2024–2025.

**Post-HB 3160 Private Actions (2023–2026):**

- **Retail/Facial Recognition:** Multiple class actions filed against Kroger and its subsidiaries (2024–2025) for using facial recognition cameras at store entrances to identify customers without consent. Similar actions filed against Macy's and other major retailers.

- **Workplace/Employment:** Several class actions filed against employers using fingerprint or facial recognition time clocks. Key issues include whether consent obtained as a condition of employment is valid, whether the "financial transaction" exception protects employers, and whether the statute of limitations is 2 or 4 years.

- **Healthcare:** Class actions against hospitals using facial recognition or fingerprint scanning for visitor management, arguing that the "security" purpose was not a valid exception to the notice/consent requirement.

**Key Legal Interpretations:**

- The definition of "consent" remains untested in Texas courts regarding whether it must be written or can be implied.
- The scope of "commercial purpose" is broad but not definitively defined.
- Courts have held that CUBI is not preempted by federal law, including HIPAA.

### 3.3 Washington RCW 19.375 Cases

*Fulp v. Microsoft Corp. (W.D. Wash.):* A class action alleging that Microsoft's Windows Hello biometric authentication system violated the WBIA. The court dismissed the case, holding that Microsoft's collection of biometric data for security purposes, with user consent through the setup process, satisfied the WBIA's requirements.

*Lopez v. Apple Inc. (N.D. Cal., transferred to W.D. Wash.):* Alleged violations related to Face ID technology. The case involved questions of whether consent obtained through the device setup process constituted adequate written consent.

**Employment Context:** Several wage-and-hour class actions have been filed against employers using fingerprint time clocks, arguing that the collection of employee fingerprints for time tracking without written consent violates the WBIA. However, no definitive Washington Supreme Court ruling has addressed whether workplace collection of biometrics triggers the WBIA's requirements.

**Washington Attorney General Enforcement:** The Washington AG has been active in privacy enforcement generally under the Consumer Protection Act (RCW 19.86), but no high-profile AG enforcement actions specifically under RCW 19.375 have been identified as of the latest available information.

**Retail and Healthcare Context:** Retailers using facial recognition for security, customer analytics, or loss prevention are subject to the WBIA's notice and consent requirements. Healthcare providers using biometric data must comply with the WBIA if the data is not covered by HIPAA's treatment/payment exemption.

## Section 4: Strategic Implications for Multi-State Enterprises

### 4.1 Compliance Friction Points

**Divergent Consent Requirements:**

The most significant compliance friction point is the difference in consent formality across the three states:
- Illinois requires a formal "written release" executed by the subject
- Washington requires "informed written consent" or electronic signature
- Texas requires "consent" but does not specify written form

For multi-state operations, the safest approach is to implement the most stringent requirement (written release) across all states. However, this creates operational complexity, particularly in high-volume consumer-facing environments where obtaining written releases from every customer is impractical.

**Conflicting Retention Periods:**

- Texas: 1 year after purpose expires
- Illinois: 3 years after last interaction or when purpose satisfied
- Washington: 3 years after last interaction

Enterprises must implement state-specific retention schedules or adopt the shortest retention period (Texas at 1 year) across all operations. However, adopting the Texas standard may create operational challenges for purposes that require longer retention, such as employee time tracking for wage and hour compliance.

**Definitional Variances:**

- Illinois covers "biometric information" derived from identifiers, which is broader than the other states
- Texas explicitly excludes photographs and video unless used to generate biometric identifiers
- Washington excludes photographs when used in isolation

Enterprises must conduct data mapping exercises to determine which data types trigger which state's requirements, particularly for facial recognition and video analytics systems.

### 4.2 Litigation Risk Assessment

| Risk Factor | Illinois BIPA | Texas CUBI | Washington RCW 19.375 |
|-------------|---------------|------------|----------------------|
| **Overall Litigation Risk** | Extremely High | High (rapidly increasing) | Moderate-High |
| **Class Action Activity** | Very High | High (post-2023) | Moderate |
| **Per-Violation Exposure** | Very High ($1,000-$5,000 per scan) | Very High ($2,000-$10,000 per violation) | High ($1,000 per violation) |
| **Standing Hurdle** | None (Rosenbach) | None | None |
| **Statute of Limitations** | 5 years (longer window) | 2-4 years (uncertain) | 2-3 years (uncertain) |
| **Pre-Suit Cure Period** | 30 days (added 2024) | None | None |
| **AG Enforcement** | Active | Very Active (Meta $1.4B) | Moderate |

**Illinois remains the highest litigation risk state** due to the combination of: (1) the *Cothron* per-scan ruling, (2) the 5-year statute of limitations, (3) no actual harm requirement, and (4) the most developed class action infrastructure.

**Texas has rapidly emerged as a high-risk jurisdiction** following the 2023 private right of action addition. The $1.4 billion Meta settlement signals that Texas takes biometric privacy seriously, and plaintiffs' attorneys are actively pursuing class actions.

**Washington presents moderate risk** but with significant uncertainty. The lack of definitive case law on key issues (employment context, per-scan vs. per-person violations, statute of limitations) creates unpredictability.

### 4.3 Regulatory Divergence

**Data Sharing and Third-Party Transfers:**

- Illinois prohibits sale or profit from biometric data with limited exceptions
- Texas prohibits sale, lease, trade, or profit, with a service provider exception for financial transactions
- Washington prohibits sale, lease, trade, or profit, with a contractor/agent exception requiring contractual compliance

The Texas service provider exception is narrower than Washington's contractor/agent exception, creating compliance challenges for enterprises using third-party vendors for biometric processing across states.

**Security Standards:**

- Texas has the most explicit security requirement ("reasonable care" standard)
- Illinois and Washington have no specific statutory security standards

Enterprises should implement the Texas standard (reasonable care, consistent with protection of other confidential information) across all operations as a baseline.

**Enforcement Mechanisms:**

- Illinois: Private right of action + AG enforcement
- Texas: Private right of action (added 2023) + AG enforcement (historically very active)
- Washington: Private right of action + AG enforcement

The availability of multiple enforcement mechanisms in all three states means enterprises face exposure from both private class actions and government enforcement actions.

### 4.4 Practical Risk Mitigation Strategies

**Immediate Actions (0–30 Days):**

1. **Conduct a comprehensive biometric data audit** across all operations in all three states. Identify every instance where biometric data is collected, stored, or processed—including through third-party vendors (time clocks, access control systems, HR SaaS platforms, security cameras with facial recognition, point-of-sale systems).

2. **Map data flows** documenting where biometric data goes, who has access, how it's stored, and how it's destroyed. This is essential for determining which state's laws apply and which compliance requirements are triggered.

3. **Review vendor contracts** to ensure service providers handling biometric data are contractually bound to comply with applicable state laws and to destroy data within the required timeframes.

**Consent Program (30–90 Days):**

4. **Implement a universal written consent program** using the most stringent standard (Illinois' written release requirement) across all three states. For employees, this should be a standalone consent form that is not buried in employee handbooks. For consumers, implement point-of-collection consent mechanisms.

5. **For Texas operations**, note that while the statute does not explicitly require written consent, best practice demands written consent given the litigation environment. Do not rely on the textual difference.

6. **For Washington operations**, ensure consent is obtained through written or electronic signature meeting the ESIGN Act requirements.

7. **Provide clear notice** that includes: what biometric data is being collected, the specific purpose and duration of collection, storage, and use, and that the data will not be sold or shared (except as required by law or for authorized transactions).

**Data Governance (90+ Days):**

8. **Implement automated destruction protocols** based on the shortest applicable retention period. The Texas requirement (1 year after purpose expires) should be the default standard across all states unless a longer retention is legally required.

9. **Develop and publish written retention/destruction policies** as required by Illinois and Washington law. Make these policies available on websites and at physical locations.

10. **Implement security measures** meeting the Texas "reasonable care" standard, including encryption at rest and in transit, access controls, and audit logging.

11. **Establish contractual compliance obligations** for all third-party vendors handling biometric data. Ensure contracts include: compliance with applicable state laws, data destruction requirements, indemnification for violations, and audit rights.

**Litigation Preparedness:**

12. **Document everything**—consent forms, notice procedures, retention schedules, destruction logs, and security measures. Meticulous documentation is the best defense against class action claims.

13. **Consider arbitration agreements with class action waivers** for employment contexts. However, note that the enforceability of such agreements for biometric claims varies by state and is subject to litigation.

14. **Review insurance coverage** for biometric privacy claims. Many cyber insurance policies exclude biometric claims or have sublimits. Consider purchasing specific biometric privacy coverage.

15. **Establish a response protocol** for demand letters. In Illinois, take advantage of the 30-day cure period (added 2024). In Texas and Washington, there is no cure period, so prompt response is critical.

**Technology Assessment:**

16. **Evaluate whether biometric data collection is truly necessary.** Consider alternatives that do not trigger biometric privacy laws, such as: key cards, PINs, mobile apps, or passwords for time tracking and access control; traditional security cameras without facial recognition for loss prevention; and non-biometric authentication methods for customer identification.

17. **If biometric collection is necessary**, choose technologies that minimize data collection and retention. For example, use fingerprint templates (which cannot be reverse-engineered) rather than full fingerprint images, and avoid storing raw biometric data.

**State-Specific Considerations:**

- **Illinois:** The most aggressive litigation environment. Consider whether operating in Illinois justifies the biometric privacy risk. If so, implement the most rigorous compliance program.
- **Texas:** The rapidly evolving legal landscape requires frequent monitoring of new case law and legislative developments. The 2025 legislative session may have produced significant amendments.
- **Washington:** While less litigated than Illinois or Texas, the lack of clear precedent creates uncertainty. Expect increased litigation activity as plaintiffs' attorneys explore the boundaries of the law.

### 4.5 Strategic Recommendations

**For Enterprises with Multi-State Operations:**

1. **Adopt a "comply with the strictest standard" approach** across all jurisdictions. Implement Illinois' written release requirement, Texas' retention period (1 year), and Washington's security and contractual compliance standards. This minimizes compliance friction and reduces litigation risk.

2. **Establish a centralized biometric data governance program** that oversees compliance across all states. This program should include: a data inventory, consent management system, destruction automation, vendor management, and incident response.

3. **Monitor for legislative changes** in all three states. The 2025 Texas legislative session may have produced amendments to CUBI. Illinois may have further amendments following the *Cothron* decision. Washington may have amendments to address gaps in the current law.

4. **Engage with industry associations** to advocate for federal biometric privacy legislation that would preempt the patchwork of state laws. However, do not rely on federal preemption in the near term.

5. **Budget for litigation defense** even with robust compliance programs. The absence of an actual harm requirement in all three states means that technical violations can still result in class action exposure.
