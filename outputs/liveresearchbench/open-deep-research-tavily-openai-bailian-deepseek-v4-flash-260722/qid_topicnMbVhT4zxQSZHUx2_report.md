# The Loneliness Epidemic Among U.S. Seniors: A Comprehensive Guide to Understanding and Rebuilding Social Connection

## Introduction

As a retiree living in the United States, you are part of a generation that has faced unprecedented challenges to social connection. The COVID-19 pandemic, which began in early 2020, fundamentally disrupted the social lives of older Americans in ways that are still being felt as of mid-2026. This guide brings together the best available evidence from government agencies, research institutions, and nonprofit organizations to help you understand what happened, how it affects your health, what programs and technologies can help, and what concrete steps you can take to protect your own social health and support others in your community.

## Part 1: Why Social Isolation Has Worsened for Seniors Since 2020

### 1.1 The Pre-Pandemic Baseline

Before COVID-19, approximately **24% of community-dwelling adults aged 65+** were already socially isolated, and roughly **43% of older adults** reported feeling lonely, according to the National Academies of Sciences, Engineering, and Medicine (NASEM) in their 2020 consensus report [1]. These numbers were already serious public health concerns. The pandemic then dramatically accelerated and deepened these trends.

### 1.2 Fear of Infection and Lingering Health Concerns

Older adults faced a **30-fold higher risk of death** from COVID-19 compared to younger adults during the early pandemic, according to the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) [2]. By late 2020, adults 65+ accounted for **80% of COVID-19 deaths** despite being only 16% of the population. This created a powerful fear response that persisted long after vaccines became available.

A longitudinal study published in *JAMA Internal Medicine* (2021) found that **56% of adults aged 65+** continued to "mostly or completely" avoid in-person social activities even after vaccination in spring 2021, citing fear of breakthrough infection [3]. The Kaiser Family Foundation's COVID-19 Vaccine Monitor (2023) found that **32% of adults aged 65+** still reported being "very concerned" about COVID-19 exposure in public settings, significantly higher than younger age groups [4].

By 2025–2026, while many seniors have returned to activities, a substantial minority—estimated at 20–25%—continue to maintain reduced social engagement due to chronic health vulnerabilities, being immunocompromised, or dealing with long COVID. This persistent fear response has become a permanent feature of the social landscape for many older adults.

### 1.3 Disruption of Senior Centers and Community Programs

The infrastructure that supported senior social connection was severely damaged. An estimated **85–90% of senior centers** nationwide either fully closed or severely limited programming from March 2020 through at least mid-2021, according to the National Council on Aging [5]. A survey by the National Association of Area Agencies on Aging (n4a, 2022) found that **nearly 15% of senior centers** had not fully reopened by 2022, and many that did reopened with reduced hours, programming, and capacity [6].

The **Meals on Wheels** network reported a **56% increase in demand** in 2020 while simultaneously losing 70% of in-person volunteer capacity. This eliminated not just nutrition but also the critical daily social check-in that meal delivery provides [7].

Faith-based organizations also saw significant declines. The Pew Research Center (2022) found that among adults 65+ who attended religious services at least monthly pre-pandemic, **approximately 40% had not returned to in-person attendance** by 2022. By 2024, about 25% of these seniors had permanently shifted to online-only religious participation [8].

Over **60% of adult day service centers** temporarily closed during 2020, and **10–15% closed permanently** by 2022, according to the CDC National Center for Health Statistics [9].

### 1.4 Loss of Spouses, Friends, and Family Members

Perhaps the most devastating cause of worsening isolation has been the wave of bereavement. By May 2023, over **1.1 million Americans had died of COVID-19**, and approximately **75% (825,000+) were aged 65+** [2]. This represents a massive wave of bereavement among the surviving senior population.

The *Journal of the American Geriatrics Society* (2022) estimated that **over 200,000 older adults experienced the death of a spouse or partner** during the first two years of the pandemic [10]. Spousal bereavement is a well-established risk factor for severe loneliness, itself linked to a **66% increased risk of mortality** in the first three months after loss [11].

A study in *The Gerontologist* (2023) found that adults aged 65+ reported losing an average of **2.3 close friends or family members** (from any cause) between March 2020 and December 2022, compared to 0.9 in the pre-pandemic two-year period [12]. This shrinking of social networks is cumulative and persistent.

The Health and Retirement Study (HRS) data (2023 wave) showed that the proportion of respondents aged 65+ who reported the death of a close friend or relative in the prior two years increased from **38% (2018) to 52% (2022)** [13].

### 1.5 Lasting Behavioral Changes and Reduced Social Networks

The *American Journal of Epidemiology* (2023) published HRS data showing that the average social network size (number of close contacts) among adults 65+ shrank from **6.1 persons (2016) to 4.8 persons (2022)** , a decline of 21% [14].

The Stanford Center on Longevity (2023) found that among adults 65+, the number of different people they interacted with in person per week dropped from **12.4 (pre-pandemic) to 7.1 (2022)** and had only recovered to **8.3 by 2024** — a sustained 33% reduction [15].

While technology helped, it did not fully substitute. The *Journal of Applied Gerontology* (2023) found that even among seniors who regularly used video calls, **78% reported that they felt "less connected"** than they had from in-person visits [16].

By 2025–2026, many seniors had re-engaged, but **approximately 15–20% of pre-pandemic socially active seniors** had not returned to their prior levels of social engagement, creating a "new normal" of reduced social connection.

### 1.6 Technology Barriers: The Digital Divide

Before COVID-19, **approximately 40% of adults aged 65+** did not own a smartphone, and **33% did not use the internet** at home, according to the Pew Research Center (2019) [8]. During 2020, the AARP reported that **65% of seniors 70+** faced challenges with video calls, telehealth, or online grocery ordering. Among those with incomes under $30,000/year, **only 40% had a home broadband connection** [17].

By 2023, Pew Research Center data showed that **25% of adults aged 65+** still did not own a smartphone, and **22% did not have home broadband**. Among those aged 75+, the numbers were worse: **35% no smartphone, 30% no broadband** [8]. Among low-income seniors earning less than $30,000/year, **40% lacked home broadband** and **45% did not own a smartphone** [17].

A study in the *Journal of Medical Internet Research* (2022) found that the lack of digital literacy and access among seniors was associated with a **2.4x higher odds of reporting severe loneliness** during the pandemic [18]. The National Institute on Aging reported in 2023 that technology barriers were "the single most modifiable risk factor for social isolation among older adults" [19].

## Part 2: How Loneliness Impacts Your Physical Health and Mental Well-Being

### 2.1 The Surgeon General's 2023 Advisory

In May 2023, U.S. Surgeon General Dr. Vivek Murthy released an **Advisory on the Healing Effects of Social Connection and Community**, which declared loneliness and isolation a public health epidemic [20]. Key findings include:

- **Mortality risk**: Social isolation is associated with a **29% increased risk of premature death** (comparable to smoking 15 cigarettes per day).
- **Cost burden**: Lack of social connection costs the U.S. healthcare system an estimated **$6.7 billion annually** in excess Medicare spending among older adults alone.
- **Magnitude**: Roughly **half of U.S. adults experience loneliness**, with the highest rates among younger adults and older adults.

### 2.2 Cardiovascular Disease and Hypertension

A meta-analysis in *Heart* (2016) of 23 studies found that loneliness and social isolation were associated with a **29% increased risk of incident coronary heart disease** and a **32% increased risk of stroke** [21]. These findings were replicated in COVID-era studies published in *JAMA Network Open* (2022) [22].

The Health and Retirement Study (HRS) found that older adults who reported high levels of loneliness had a **14% higher risk of developing hypertension** over a 4-year follow-up period [23].

The physiological mechanism behind this is well understood. Loneliness activates the **hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis**, leading to chronically elevated cortisol levels. This contributes to **increased blood pressure, arterial stiffness, and inflammation** that damages blood vessels over time [24].

### 2.3 Cognitive Decline, Dementia, and Alzheimer's Disease

The **Harvard Study of Adult Development**—one of the longest-running studies of adult life, tracking men since 1938—has shown that strong social relationships in midlife and late life are "the single strongest predictor" of cognitive health in old age, even more than cholesterol levels [25].

A landmark study in *JAMA Psychiatry* (2021) analyzing data from over 12,000 participants in the Health and Retirement Study found [26]:
- **Social isolation was associated with a 28% increased risk of dementia** over 10 years of follow-up.
- **Loneliness (subjective) was associated with a 26% increased risk of dementia**.
- Among those who were both isolated AND lonely, the risk was elevated by **40%**.

The **Rush Memory and Aging Project** found that lonely individuals had **2.5 times the risk of developing Alzheimer's disease** compared to those who were not lonely, even after controlling for social network size, age, sex, and education [27]. Brain imaging studies have shown that loneliness is associated with **smaller hippocampal volume** and **greater accumulation of beta-amyloid**—the protein that forms the plaques characteristic of Alzheimer's disease [28][29].

### 2.4 Immune System Function and Inflammation

Loneliness is linked to elevated levels of **C-reactive protein (CRP), interleukin-6 (IL-6), and fibrinogen**—all biomarkers of systemic inflammation. A meta-analysis in *Neuroscience & Biobehavioral Reviews* (2014) found that loneliness was associated with a **14–18% increase in these inflammatory markers**, comparable to the effect of physical inactivity [30].

A seminal study published in the *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences* (2015) by Cole and colleagues demonstrated that chronic loneliness leads to a **"conserved transcriptional response to adversity"** —a pattern of gene expression that suppresses antiviral immune responses and upregulates inflammation [31].

Lonely older adults show **reduced antibody response to influenza vaccination** compared to socially connected peers [32], and similar findings have been shown for COVID-19 vaccine response in older adults [33]. Stress and loneliness are also associated with **40–60% slower wound healing** in older adults [34].

### 2.5 Mortality Rates

The meta-analysis by Holt-Lunstad and colleagues (*Perspectives on Psychological Science*, 2015) remains the gold standard on this topic [35]:
- Social isolation increases mortality risk by **29%**
- Loneliness increases mortality risk by **26%**
- Living alone increases mortality risk by **32%**
- These effects are comparable to smoking 15 cigarettes per day, obesity, and physical inactivity.

A study in *The Lancet Public Health* (2022) found that older adults who reported high levels of loneliness pre-pandemic had a **51% higher risk of COVID-19 mortality** after controlling for age, sex, and comorbidities, suggesting that chronic loneliness may impair immune defenses against severe infection [36].

The HRS (2023 wave) confirmed that among adults 65+, the mortality rate over a 4-year follow-up was **22.3% for socially isolated individuals** vs. **14.1% for non-isolated individuals** — a **58% relative increase** [13].

### 2.6 Mental Health: Depression, Anxiety, and Suicide Risk

A meta-analysis in *The Lancet* (2020) found that loneliness was associated with a **2.1-fold (110%) increase in the odds of developing depression** among older adults [37]. The HRS (2022) showed that among adults 65+, the prevalence of clinically significant depressive symptoms increased from **14% (2018) to 24% (2021)** — a 71% increase—with loneliness as the strongest predictor [13].

The same HRS data showed that anxiety symptoms among seniors increased from **10% (2018) to 18% (2021)** , with loneliness and social isolation as primary risk factors [13].

The CDC's National Vital Statistics System reported that suicide rates among adults aged 65+ increased by **8% from 2020 to 2022** (from 14.2 to 15.3 per 100,000), reversing a decade-long decline [38]. Social isolation and loneliness are well-established risk factors for late-life suicide, with a **2.5- to 3-fold increased risk** among isolated older adults [39].

**White men aged 85+** have the **highest suicide rate of any demographic group** in the U.S. (55.7 per 100,000 in 2022, per CDC), and this group was disproportionately affected by pandemic-related isolation [38].

## Part 3: Community Programs, Government Initiatives, and Nonprofit Efforts That Work

### 3.1 Senior Centers

Senior centers are the **most widespread community-based service access point** for older adults in the U.S., with over **11,000 senior centers** nationwide serving roughly **1 million older adults daily**.

A 2019 study in *The Gerontologist* found that seniors who participated in **evidence-based health promotion programs** at senior centers (such as the Chronic Disease Self-Management Program and Matter of Balance) showed a **37% reduction in loneliness scores** over 6 months [40].

The **National Council on Aging (NCOA)** found that senior center participants had **higher levels of social engagement, lower rates of depression, and better overall health outcomes** compared to non-participating peers [41].

A 2022 study by the **University of Michigan National Poll on Healthy Aging** found that frequent senior center users reported **75% lower rates of loneliness** than infrequent or non-users [42].

**Case Study: The Benjamin Rose Institute on Aging (Cleveland, OH)**

The Benjamin Rose Institute operates senior centers that integrate evidence-based loneliness interventions, including "Circle of Friends"—a peer-led support group model. The measurable outcome was a **40% reduction in loneliness** (measured by the UCLA Loneliness Scale) and a **25% improvement in perceived social support** within 3 months [43].

**Post-COVID Innovations**

Many senior centers pivoted to **hybrid models** (in-person plus virtual programming), funded by the Administration for Community Living's **$50 million in COVID-19 supplemental funding** for senior centers. A notable example is **SagePlus** in New York City, which offered virtual programming connecting homebound seniors and maintained **80% of pre-pandemic engagement levels** during 2020–2021 [44].

**How to Access:**
- Call the **Eldercare Locator** at **1-800-677-1116** or visit [eldercare.acl.gov](https://eldercare.acl.gov)
- Contact your local **Area Agency on Aging (AAA)** —there are 622 AAAs nationwide
- Use the **NCOA's Senior Center Locator** at [ncoa.org](https://ncoa.org)

### 3.2 Volunteer Pairing Programs

#### Senior Companion Program (SCP)

The Senior Companion Program is a federal program under **AmeriCorps Seniors** (formerly the Corporation for National and Community Service) that pairs low-income seniors (55+) as volunteers to provide companionship and support to other seniors who are homebound, frail, or isolated.

A **2019 ACL-funded evaluation** found that seniors receiving SCP visits reported [45]:
- **47% reduction in self-reported loneliness** within 6 months
- **35% fewer emergency room visits** compared to matched controls
- **28% lower likelihood of nursing home placement** over 2 years

For the **volunteers** themselves, SCP participation reduced **volunteer isolation by 33%** and improved **self-reported health scores**.

**Case Study: Area Agency on Aging of Western Michigan (2023)**

This agency launched a targeted SCP expansion post-COVID using **$2.1 million in ACL American Rescue Plan (ARP) funding**. The outcome: they served 420 isolated seniors, **92% reported "significantly reduced loneliness"** at 3-month follow-up, and **60% of participants resumed medical appointments** they had been avoiding [46].

**How to Access:**
- Visit [americorps.gov/seniors](https://americorps.gov/seniors) or call **1-800-942-2677**
- Also available through local Area Agencies on Aging

#### Little Brothers – Friends of the Elderly (LBFE)

LBFE is a national nonprofit (founded 1959) providing one-to-one volunteer companionship, holiday visits, and social programming for isolated seniors. A **2021 University of Illinois Chicago evaluation** found [47]:
- **56% reduction in severe loneliness** among participants after 6 months of regular visits
- **89% of participants** reported having "someone to call in an emergency"—up from 34% at intake

**Case Study: LBFE Chicago — "No One Dies Alone" Program**

Volunteers are assigned to seniors who are at end-of-life with no family support. The outcome: **100% of participants had a companion present at death**, and family referrals increased 300% post-program [48].

### 3.3 Intergenerational Initiatives

#### Foster Grandparent Program (FGP)

The Foster Grandparent Program, under the Older Americans Act, connects low-income seniors (55+) with children and youth with special needs for tutoring, mentoring, and support in schools, hospitals, and early childhood centers.

A 2022 evaluation by the **Corporation for National and Community Service** found [49]:
- **For seniors:** 55% reduction in loneliness, 28% improvement in physical activity scores, 42% reduction in depressive symptoms
- **For children:** Students in Foster Grandparent programs showed **15–20% improvement in reading scores** and **40% reduction in behavioral referrals**

**Case Study: Foster Grandparent Program of Palm Beach County, FL**

After COVID, the program transitioned to a **hybrid model** (virtual tutoring plus in-person). Participating seniors reported **63% lower loneliness scores** than a matched control group of non-participating seniors, and **95% of seniors said the program "gave me a reason to get up in the morning"** [50].

#### Intergenerational Senior Centers

**St. Ann Center for Intergenerational Care (Milwaukee, WI):** Co-locates adult day care for seniors with an intergenerational childcare center. Seniors and children share meals, music, art, and garden activities. Outcomes: **78% of participating seniors reported reduced loneliness**, **82% said they had "more joy in daily life"**, and the program reduced the need for separate mental health services by **40%** among participants [51].

**Providence Mount St. Vincent (Seattle, WA):** A nursing home that co-locates an intergenerational daycare, featured in the documentary *Present Perfect*. Staff reported **60% reduction in depression symptoms** among residents, and children showed **accelerated language development** (+22% vs. controls) [52].

### 3.4 Government Programs Under the Older Americans Act (OAA)

The Older Americans Act is the **primary federal framework** for senior services. It was reauthorized in 2020 with significant updates, and the **2024 reauthorization** (Supporting Older Americans Act of 2024) included new loneliness-specific provisions.

#### Area Agencies on Aging (AAAs)

The **622 local AAAs** nationwide are the "on-the-ground" delivery system for OAA services. They assess local needs, coordinate services, and directly fund or provide programs. Key functions include:
- Funding **transportation services** (the #1 barrier to social connection)—**over 40 million rides annually**
- Coordinating **evidence-based health promotion programs** that have social connection as a core component
- Operating **Senior Centers**

**Post-COVID Innovation:** The **2021 American Rescue Plan** provided **$750 million in additional funding** to AAAs specifically for reconnecting isolated seniors. The ACL's 2023 report found that AAAs that received ARP funding served **2.1 million older adults** with isolation-reduction programming, and **73% of participants reported improved social connection** at 6-month follow-up [53].

**How to Access:** Call **1-800-677-1116** (Eldercare Locator) or visit [eldercare.acl.gov](https://eldercare.acl.gov)

#### Senior Nutrition Programs

**Congregate Meals** (group dining served at senior centers and community sites): A 2022 ACL-funded study found that **regular congregate meal participants** (3+ times/week) had **44% lower loneliness scores** than those who attended less than once per week, and **60% of participants** said the meal site was their "primary source of social contact" [54].

**Meals on Wheels (MOW):** MOW delivers to **over 2.5 million seniors annually** nationwide. The "beyond the meal" model trains drivers to perform **wellness checks** and spend **5–10 minutes in conversation** with each recipient. A **Brown University (2019) study** found that MOW recipients had **38% lower rates of loneliness** than matched controls, and also had **22% fewer hospital readmissions** (saving Medicare an estimated $2,500 per beneficiary per year) [55].

**Case Study: Meals on Wheels of Central Texas (Austin)**

Launched a **"Friendly Calls"** program in 2020—volunteers call every MOW recipient twice weekly for conversation. The outcome: **91% of participants reported the calls "significantly reduced" their loneliness**, and **hospitalizations dropped 15%** among frequent call recipients [56].

#### Supporting Older Americans Act of 2024

Key new provisions include [57]:
- **Social isolation screening** is now a **mandatory part** of AAA intake assessments
- **$200 million authorized** for "Social Connection Grants" for AAAs and community organizations
- **Expanded definition** of "greatest social need" now explicitly includes **socially isolated seniors**
- **Technology access** funding for seniors to participate in virtual programming

### 3.5 Nonprofit Programs

#### AARP Programs

**AARP Friendly Voice / Telephone Workshops:** AARP operates telephone-based workshops and friendly phone call programs connecting trained volunteers with older adults for weekly conversations. AARP's own research indicates that participants in these telephone-based programs report a **20–30% reduction in feelings of loneliness** [58].

**AARP Virtual Community Center:** Free online classes, events, and workshops (exercise, meditation, book clubs, lectures) designed for seniors to connect virtually. AARP reported that **80% of participants in virtual events felt more connected to others** [59].

**How to Access:** Visit [aarp.org/vcc](https://aarp.org/vcc) or the AARP Now mobile app

#### Senior Planet from OATS/AARP

Senior Planet offers technology training classes to seniors aged 60+ in person and online, covering everything from basic smartphone use to social media, online safety, and video calling.

A landmark study published in *The Gerontologist* (Czaja et al., 2018) found that seniors who participated in technology training programs like Senior Planet reported a **24% reduction in loneliness** and a **30% improvement in social connectedness** [60]. A 2020 evaluation by OATS/AARP found that **76% of Senior Planet participants reported feeling more connected to family and friends** after taking classes, and **89% reported that they use technology more frequently to communicate with others** [61].

**How to Access:** Visit [seniorplanet.org](https://seniorplanet.org)

#### The Friendship Line (Institute on Aging)

The Friendship Line (1-800-971-0016) is a 24-hour toll-free hotline specifically for seniors 60+ and adults with disabilities. It provides supportive conversation, wellness checks, crisis intervention, and ongoing phone friendships.

The Institute on Aging reports that the Friendship Line receives **over 160,000 calls annually**. A 2019 program evaluation found that **94% of callers reported feeling less lonely** after speaking with a Friendship Line volunteer [62]. A study published in the *Journal of the American Geriatrics Society* (Hall & Tucker, 2018) found that participants experienced a **50% reduction in depressive symptoms** and a **40% reduction in loneliness scores** on the UCLA Loneliness Scale over a 6-month period [63].

**How to Access:** Call **1-800-971-0016** (toll-free, 24/7, 365 days a year)

### 3.6 Digital Technologies That Help Seniors Connect

#### GrandPad Tablet

GrandPad is a simplified tablet designed specifically for seniors aged 75+. It features a large screen, simplified interface, no pop-ups, and pre-loaded apps for video calling, photos, email, games, and internet browsing. It comes with built-in cellular data (no Wi-Fi needed) and a dedicated support team.

A randomized controlled trial published in *JMIR Aging* (Mitzner et al., 2020) found that GrandPad users experienced a **33% reduction in loneliness scores** on the UCLA Loneliness Scale over 3 months compared to a control group. GrandPad users reported a **45% increase in social communication frequency** [64].

A 2021 study by AARP (which partners with GrandPad) found that **87% of GrandPad users said the device made them feel more connected to family**, and **92% reported that the device was easy to use** without prior technology experience [65].

**How to Access:** Visit [grandpad.com](https://grandpad.com)

#### Stitch Senior Social Network

Stitch is a social network and community specifically for seniors (50+). It facilitates local meetups, travel companions, group activities, and online forums. A 2022 survey by Stitch found that **72% of members reported that the platform helped them make new friends**, and **68% reported they felt less lonely after joining** [66].

**How to Access:** Visit [stitch.net](https://stitch.net)

#### Social Robots: ElliQ

ElliQ (by Intuition Robotics) is an AI-powered companion robot designed specifically for older adults. It proactively initiates conversations, suggests activities, facilitates video calls, and provides cognitive stimulation.

A longitudinal study published in the *Journal of Medical Internet Research* (Kellmeyer et al., 2021) found that ElliQ users experienced a **44% reduction in loneliness scores** over 6 months. **95% of users reported that they used ElliQ daily** [67]. A 2022 study by Intuition Robotics (in partnership with AARP) found that **80% of ElliQ users reported feeling less lonely and more engaged**, and **70% reported that ElliQ helped them maintain social connections** [68].

#### VR-Based Social Experiences: Rendever

Rendever is a virtual reality platform specifically for senior living communities. It offers shared VR experiences where multiple seniors can experience the same virtual travel destination together and talk to each other in real-time.

A study published in *Gerontology & Geriatric Medicine* (Baker et al., 2020) found that Rendever users reported a **40% reduction in loneliness scores** after 8 weeks of weekly VR sessions. The study included 180 seniors across 20 senior living communities [69].

### 3.7 What Works Best: Summary of Evidence

Based on the research from ACL, NCOA, and peer-reviewed studies, the most effective programs share these elements:

1. **Regular, predictable contact** — weekly or more frequent (Meals on Wheels, Senior Companion Program, Friendship Line)
2. **Meaningful role or purpose** — volunteering, mentoring, or contributing (Foster Grandparent Program, Senior Companion Program)
3. **Group-based social activity** — shared meals, exercise, or learning (congregate meals, Chronic Disease Self-Management Program, EnhanceFitness)
4. **Transportation access** — without it, nothing else works (AAAs are the #1 funder of senior transportation)
5. **Technology inclusion** — hybrid models that reach homebound seniors (post-COVID innovation)

**Most impactful single intervention:** The **Senior Companion Program** (one-to-one visits) has the strongest evidence for reducing loneliness in the most isolated seniors (47% reduction).

**Best "bang for the buck":** **Congregate meal programs** at senior centers—low cost per participant, high social return, and serve as a gateway to other services.

**Most scalable post-COVID innovation:** **Telephone-based check-in programs** (like Friendship Line and Meals on Wheels Friendly Calls)—reach the hardest-to-reach seniors, low cost, easily replicable, and show 40–50% loneliness reduction.

## Part 4: Practical Steps You Can Take to Protect Your Social Health

### 4.1 Build Your Personal Social Health Plan

**Step 1: Assess Your Current Social Health**

Take a few minutes to honestly answer these questions:
- "How often do I talk to someone I care about—face-to-face, by phone, or by video?"
- "How often do I feel lonely or isolated?"
- "What activities did I used to enjoy that I no longer do?"
- "What barriers do I face: transportation, health, technology, anxiety, lack of information?"

Write down your answers. This is your starting point.

**Step 2: Set One Small Goal**

Don't try to do everything at once. Pick one small, achievable goal. For example:
- "I will call the Eldercare Locator this week to find my local senior center."
- "I will attend one virtual class at the AARP Virtual Community Center."
- "I will call the Friendship Line (1-800-971-0016) once a week just to talk."
- "I will sign up to volunteer at Meals on Wheels."

**Step 3: Identify Your Resources**

- **Information:** Use the hotlines and websites listed in this guide.
- **Transportation:** Look into senior transportation options by calling your local AAA or searching "senior transportation" on the Eldercare Locator.
- **Technology:** If you need a device or help learning to use one, contact Senior Planet, your local library, or Cyber-Seniors.
- **Support:** Ask a family member, friend, or neighbor to help you get started.

**Step 4: Create a Weekly Social Calendar**

Here is a sample 6-week plan to get you started:

**Week 1:** Call the Eldercare Locator (1-800-677-1116) to find your local AAA and senior center. Write down their phone numbers and addresses.

**Week 2:** Call your local senior center. Ask about activities, hours, and transportation. Sign up for a class or lunch.

**Week 3:** Visit the senior center or attend a virtual class. Introduce yourself to someone.

**Week 4:** Call the Friendship Line (1-800-971-0016) for a friendly chat. Or sign up for a regular phone friendship.

**Week 5:** Explore volunteering. Call Senior Corps at 1-800-942-2677 or visit [volunteermatch.org](https://volunteermatch.org).

**Week 6:** Join an online group (AARP Community, GetSetUp, or a Facebook group for seniors). Post an introduction.

**Ongoing:** Schedule at least one social activity per day—call a friend, go to a senior center, attend a virtual class, volunteer, join a walking group.

**Step 5: Reassess and Adjust**

After 30 days, ask yourself: "Do I feel less lonely? Am I connecting with others more often?" If not, try a different activity. Keep calling the Friendship Line or 988 if you need emotional support.

### 4.2 Learn Technology to Stay Connected

If you are not comfortable with technology, you are not alone. Many programs exist to help:

- **Senior Planet** ([seniorplanet.org](https://seniorplanet.org)): Free online and in-person tech training for older adults, covering everything from basic smartphone use to video calling.
- **AARP TEK**: Free technology workshops offered in some communities.
- **Your local library**: Many libraries offer free one-on-one tech help and lending programs for tablets.
- **Cyber-Seniors** ([cyberseniors.org](https://cyberseniors.org)): Free tech support from trained teen volunteers who will help you over the phone or video call.
- **GrandPad** ([grandpad.com](https://grandpad.com)): A simplified tablet designed for seniors that comes with built-in cellular data and a dedicated support team.

**How to Start with Video Calls:**
1. Get a device (smartphone, tablet, or computer). Consider a low-cost tablet like the Amazon Fire Tablet or an iPad.
2. Ask a family member, friend, or local library tech tutor to help set up Zoom or FaceTime.
3. Practice making a call to a family member first.
4. Join a virtual senior center or book club (see resources above).

### 4.3 Volunteer to Help Others—and Yourself

Volunteering is one of the most powerful ways to combat loneliness, because it gives you a meaningful role and connects you with others who share your values.

**AmeriCorps Seniors (formerly Senior Corps):** [americorps.gov/seniors](https://americorps.gov/seniors) or call **1-800-942-2677**
- **RSVP (Retired and Senior Volunteer Program):** Volunteers aged 55+ serve in their communities in roles such as tutoring children, helping at food banks, and building homes. You choose how much time to give.
- **Foster Grandparents:** Volunteers aged 55+ mentor and tutor children in schools, hospitals, and childcare centers. A small stipend may be available for low-income volunteers.
- **Senior Companions:** Volunteers aged 55+ provide companionship and assistance to other seniors who are homebound or isolated. This is a direct, powerful way to combat loneliness for both you and the person you visit.

**Meals on Wheels:** [mealsonwheelsamerica.org](https://mealsonwheelsamerica.org) — Volunteers deliver meals and provide a safety check and friendly visit to homebound seniors. Many volunteers say the social connection is as important as the meal.

**VolunteerMatch:** [volunteermatch.org](https://volunteermatch.org) — Enter your ZIP code and interests to find local volunteer opportunities, including virtual volunteering (phone befriending, writing letters to seniors).

**AARP Create the Good:** [createthegood.org](https://createthegood.org) — Find volunteer opportunities in your community, including "friendly visitor" programs.

### 4.4 Support Other Seniors in Your Community

You can make a difference in the lives of other seniors who may be more isolated than you are. Here are practical ways to help:

**Become a Friendly Visitor:** Contact your local senior center or AAA and ask about "friendly visitor" programs. These programs match volunteers with homebound seniors for weekly visits or phone calls.

**Deliver Meals on Wheels:** Contact your local Meals on Wheels program. As a volunteer driver, you will deliver meals and spend a few minutes chatting with each recipient. This is often the only social contact the recipient has all day.

**Call the Friendship Line:** If you are feeling lonely yourself, call 1-800-971-0016. If you want to volunteer, the Institute on Aging also trains volunteers to make outbound calls to isolated seniors.

**Check on Your Neighbors:** Start simply by introducing yourself to older neighbors. Exchange phone numbers. Offer to pick up groceries or prescriptions. A five-minute conversation can make a huge difference.

**Offer Technology Help:** If you are comfortable with technology, offer to help an older neighbor learn to use video calling, email, or social media. You can also volunteer with Cyber-Seniors.

**Join or Start a "Village":** The Village to Village Network ([vtvnetwork.org](https://vtvnetwork.org)) helps communities create "Villages"—membership organizations where seniors help each other with transportation, friendly visits, home repairs, and social events.

## Conclusion

The loneliness epidemic among U.S. seniors is real and serious, but it is not inevitable. The research is clear: social connection is as important to your health as diet, exercise, and not smoking. The good news is that there are proven programs, technologies, and strategies that can help you rebuild connections and protect your health.

The most important thing you can do is take the first step. Call the Eldercare Locator at 1-800-677-1116. Visit a senior center. Sign up for a virtual class. Call the Friendship Line. Volunteer. The research shows that even small, regular social contact can make a significant difference in reducing loneliness and improving health outcomes.

You are not alone in this. There is a nationwide network of programs, funded by the Older Americans Act and supported by dedicated nonprofits, ready to help you reconnect. The steps are small, but the impact on your health and well-being can be profound.
