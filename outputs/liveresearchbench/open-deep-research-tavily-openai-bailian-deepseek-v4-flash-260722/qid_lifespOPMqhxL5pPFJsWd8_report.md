# HAPE in Ski Mountaineers at ≥3,000 m: A Comprehensive Clinical Picture

## Introduction

High-altitude pulmonary edema (HAPE) is a non-cardiogenic, hydrostatic form of pulmonary edema that occurs in hypoxia-susceptible individuals ascending to altitudes typically above 2,500–3,000 m. In the ski mountaineering population, the clinical picture is uniquely shaped by the confluence of prolonged hypobaric hypoxia, high-intensity aerobic exercise, cold air inhalation, and often rapid ascent profiles that are inherent to the sport. This report synthesizes the full historical record of HAPE pathophysiology, case documentation, treatment strategies, and prevention protocols, with an emphasis on evidence published between 2020 and 2026, to provide a comprehensive clinical resource for researchers and practitioners working with ski mountaineers.

## Section 1: Predominant Pathophysiological Mechanisms Driving HAPE in Ski Mountaineers at ≥3,000 m

HAPE in ski mountaineers results from a multifactorial cascade beginning with an exaggerated hypoxic pulmonary vasoconstriction (HPV) that produces uneven pulmonary blood flow, compounded by exercise-induced pulmonary hypertension, cold stress, impaired alveolar fluid clearance, and ultimately capillary stress failure. The process is primarily hemodynamic rather than inflammatory in its early stages, though inflammation may supervene later.

### 1.1 Exaggerated Hypoxic Pulmonary Vasoconstriction and Uneven Blood Flow Distribution

The central mechanism of HAPE is an exaggerated and uneven HPV response to alveolar hypoxia. In HAPE-susceptible (HAPE-S) individuals, some pulmonary arterioles constrict severely while others remain relatively dilated, leading to overperfusion of non-constricted capillary beds. This patchy vasoconstriction creates regions of high capillary pressure and flow that are vulnerable to edema formation.

The classic descriptive work by Hultgren et al. (*Chest*, 1996) established that patchy vasoconstriction leads to overperfusion of non-constricted capillary beds, a concept that remains foundational [1]. Dehnert et al. (*European Respiratory Journal*, 2005) demonstrated that HAPE-S individuals have higher pulmonary artery systolic pressure (PASP) during both normoxic exercise and hypoxic breathing at sea level, providing a screening tool for susceptibility [2]. Grunig et al. (*American Journal of Respiratory and Critical Care Medicine*, 2000) showed that HAPE-S individuals exhibit a ≥2-fold greater increase in pulmonary artery pressure (PAP) in response to hypoxia compared to controls [3].

For ski mountaineers, this mechanism is critically relevant because ski touring involves transitions between high-intensity exercise during ascent and recovery during descent. During ascent, minute ventilation increases dramatically, but the high cardiac output forces blood through the pulmonary circulation at high flow rates. The uneven distribution of HPV means that some capillary beds receive dangerously high flow and pressure, setting the stage for edema.

### 1.2 Exercise-Induced Pulmonary Hypertension

In HAPE-S individuals, exercise compounds the effect of hypoxia on PAP, producing a supra-additive rise. The normal pulmonary circulation can accommodate a 4–5× increase in cardiac output with only a modest rise in PAP, but in HAPE-S individuals, the combination of hypoxia and exercise produces a disproportionate response.

Eldridge et al. (*Journal of Applied Physiology*, 1996) demonstrated that PAP rises disproportionately with exercise at altitude in HAPE-S subjects [4]. The landmark study by Maggiorini et al. (*Circulation*, 2001) used right-heart catheterization at 4,559 m (Capanna Regina Margherita) to show that mean PAP in HAPE-S subjects was 38 ± 5 mmHg versus 23 ± 3 mmHg in controls, with normal pulmonary capillary wedge pressure (PCWP), indicating pre-capillary pulmonary hypertension [5]. Kaufmann et al. (*Journal of the American College of Cardiology*, 2002) showed that even moderate exercise (50 W) at altitude caused PAP to rise to levels exceeding 40 mmHg in HAPE-S subjects [6].

Ski mountaineering at ≥3,000 m involves sustained periods of zone 2–3 heart rate intensity (70–85% HRmax) during skinning, with peak cardiac output of 20–25 L/min. This cardiac output, when forced through a constricted pulmonary vascular bed, elevates PAP to levels that can exceed the critical threshold for capillary stress failure (~35–40 mmHg mean PAP).

### 1.3 The Role of Cold Stress

Cold air inhalation during ski mountaineering—ambient temperatures often -10°C to -25°C at ≥3,000 m—produces several relevant effects: airway cooling leading to reflex bronchoconstriction, pulmonary vasoconstriction via sympathetic activation, and increased blood viscosity and platelet activation.

Grocott et al. (*Wilderness Medicine*, 7th ed., Elsevier, 2017) reviewed the role of cold in exacerbating pulmonary vasoconstriction [7]. Steinacker et al. (*High Altitude Medicine & Biology*, 2001) noted that cold exposure is a recognized risk factor for HAPE [8]. Wagner et al. (*Journal of Applied Physiology*, 2002) provided data showing that cold air inhalation can augment HPV [9].

Ski mountaineers are unique in that they breathe very cold, dry air at high minute ventilations (60–100 L/min during ascent). This causes significant respiratory heat loss and cooling of the pulmonary vasculature, which may further augment HPV and contribute to the rise in PAP.

### 1.4 Impaired Alveolar Fluid Clearance

The alveolar epithelium actively clears fluid from the airspaces via vectorial Na⁺ transport: Na⁺ enters alveolar epithelial cells through apical ENaC (epithelial sodium channels) and is extruded by basolateral Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase, with water following osmotically. Hypoxia downregulates both ENaC and Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase, impairing the lung's ability to clear edema fluid.

Mairbäurl et al. (*Journal of Applied Physiology*, 2002) provided the seminal study showing that hypoxia (12% O₂ for 4 h) reduces alveolar fluid clearance in healthy volunteers [10]. Sartori et al. (*New England Journal of Medicine*, 2002) showed that inhaled salmeterol (a β₂-agonist that upregulates ENaC) reduced HAPE incidence in HAPE-S subjects, providing in vivo evidence of the importance of Na⁺ transport [11]. Planès et al. (*American Journal of Respiratory and Critical Care Medicine*, 1997) provided molecular evidence that hypoxia reduces Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase activity in alveolar type II cells [12].

The prolonged, sustained hypoxia during a ski mountaineering expedition leads to chronic downregulation of alveolar fluid clearance mechanisms. This creates a "two-hit" scenario: high capillary pressure drives fluid into the interstitium, and the impaired clearance mechanisms cannot remove it, leading to frank alveolar edema.

### 1.5 Inflammatory Mechanisms: Hemodynamic Versus Inflammatory

For decades, HAPE was debated as either purely hemodynamic or inflammatory. The current consensus is that HAPE is primarily a hemodynamic, non-inflammatory form of pulmonary edema in its early stages, but a secondary inflammatory phase may exist.

Swenson et al. (*American Journal of Respiratory and Critical Care Medicine*, 2002) performed bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL) in HAPE patients at 4,559 m and found no increase in neutrophils, pro-inflammatory cytokines, or protein permeability markers compared to controls, strongly supporting a hemodynamic, non-inflammatory mechanism [13]. Maggiorini et al. (*Circulation*, 2001) confirmed that the edema fluid-to-plasma protein ratio was low, suggesting high-pressure hydrostatic edema, not inflammatory leak [5].

However, Kubo et al. (*Respiration*, 2001) found elevated IL-1, IL-6, and TNF-α in BAL fluid from HAPE patients after several days, suggesting that inflammation may be a secondary phenomenon triggered by the edema itself [14]. Basnyat et al. (*Wilderness & Environmental Medicine*, 2003) reviewed the transition from hemodynamic to inflammatory edema [15].

The clinical implication for ski mountaineers is that early HAPE is pure hydrostatic edema—treatable with descent and oxygen/drugs. If the athlete remains at altitude, the edema can progress to an inflammatory phase with capillary leak, worsening the condition and making it less responsive to simple vasodilators.

### 1.6 Endothelial Dysfunction and Capillary Stress Failure

The pulmonary capillary wall is composed of a thin endothelial layer, a basement membrane, and a thin epithelial layer. When capillary transmural pressure exceeds ~35–40 mmHg, stress failure occurs—the basement membrane ruptures, leading to high-permeability edema with hemorrhage.

West et al. (*Journal of Applied Physiology*, 1991) provided the landmark theoretical and experimental paper showing that high capillary pressure causes ultrastructural damage to the capillary wall, including endothelial blebs, breaks in the capillary endothelium, and even frank hemorrhage [16]. West et al. (*Journal of Applied Physiology*, 2000) reviewed the mechanism of pulmonary capillary stress failure [17]. Eldridge et al. (*High Altitude Medicine & Biology*, 2006) showed that alveolar hemorrhage (detected by hemosiderin-laden macrophages in BAL) is common in HAPE, confirming stress failure [18].

Berger et al. (*Journal of Applied Physiology*, 2006) showed that HAPE-S subjects have reduced NO-mediated vasodilation, suggesting constitutive endothelial dysfunction [19]. Bailey et al. (*Journal of Applied Physiology*, 2009) implicated oxidative stress and reduced NO bioavailability in HAPE pathogenesis [20].

During a hard ski tour, pulmonary capillary pressure can transiently exceed the stress failure threshold multiple times per day (e.g., during steep kick turns, high-intensity skinning, or descending at speed). Each episode of stress failure causes microscopic hemorrhage and protein leak, which accumulates over days to produce the characteristic frothy, pink sputum of established HAPE.

### 1.7 Neurohumoral Mechanisms: RAAS, Sympathetic Activation, and ADH

Altitude exposure activates the sympathetic nervous system and RAAS, both of which can exacerbate pulmonary vasoconstriction and fluid retention.

Duplain et al. (*Circulation*, 1999) showed that HAPE-S subjects have higher muscle sympathetic nerve activity (MSNA) at altitude compared to controls, and that this correlates with PAP [21]. Milledge et al. (*Journal of Applied Physiology*, 1983) provided early work showing that altitude suppresses the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system in some individuals but activates it in others [22]. Loeppky et al. (*Aviation, Space, and Environmental Medicine*, 1997) showed that ADH is inappropriately elevated in some HAPE-S subjects, contributing to water retention and hyponatremia [23]. Bartsch et al. (*High Altitude Medicine & Biology*, 2002) provided a comprehensive review of volume-regulating hormones at altitude [24].

Ski mountaineering induces profound sympathetic activation (from exercise, cold, and hypoxia) and can cause inappropriate fluid retention (via ADH and RAAS). This creates a fluid-overloaded state that further elevates PAP and increases the hydrostatic driving force for edema formation.

### 1.8 Genetic Susceptibility

HAPE susceptibility has a strong genetic component, with first-degree relatives of HAPE patients having a 5–10× higher risk of developing HAPE.

Droma et al. (*Circulation*, 2002) found that the Glu298Asp polymorphism and intron 4 VNTR of endothelial nitric oxide synthase (eNOS/NOS3) are associated with HAPE susceptibility [25]. Ahsan et al. (*High Altitude Medicine & Biology*, 2005) replicated the association in a Japanese cohort [26]. Hotchkiss et al. (*Chest*, 2003) found that the I/D polymorphism of ACE is associated with HAPE susceptibility, with the D allele (associated with higher ACE activity and higher angiotensin II) being overrepresented in HAPE patients [27]. Qadar Pasha et al. (*Journal of the Renin-Angiotensin-Aldosterone System*, 2011) provided a meta-analysis confirming the association [28].

Other implicated genes include CYP11B2 (aldosterone synthase), EPAS1 (HIF-2α), and VEGF (Kobayashi et al., *Journal of Applied Physiology*, 2002; Luo et al., *High Altitude Medicine & Biology*, 2014; Srivastava et al., *Molecular Biology Reports*, 2014) [29][30][31]. Mishra et al. (*High Altitude Medicine & Biology*, 2021) provided a recent comprehensive meta-analysis of genetic associations with HAPE [32].

Genetic screening for HAPE susceptibility is not yet standard, but a family history of HAPE is a strong predictor. Ski mountaineers with a first-degree relative who developed HAPE, or who have themselves had HAPE before, should be extremely cautious about rapid ascents to ≥3,000 m.

### Integrated Pathophysiological Model for Ski Mountaineers

The following integrative model explains how ski mountaineering uniquely triggers HAPE:

- **Hypobaric hypoxia** at ≥3,000 m triggers exaggerated HPV (genetic predisposition)
- **High-intensity exercise** (ski touring at 70–85% HRmax) produces supra-additive rises in PAP
- **Cold air inhalation** augments HPV via sympathetic activation and reflex vasoconstriction
- **Impaired alveolar fluid clearance** (hypoxia downregulates ENaC/Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase) prevents edema removal
- **Capillary stress failure** occurs when transmural pressure exceeds 35–40 mmHg
- **Neurohumoral activation** (sympathetic, RAAS, ADH) promotes fluid retention and vasoconstriction
- **Secondary inflammation** may develop if ascent continues, worsening the condition

## Section 2: Case Reports and Case Series Documentation

The high-altitude medicine literature contains a rich historical record of HAPE case documentation, though specific case series focused exclusively on ski mountaineers are limited. The following section synthesizes the historical record and recent (2020–2026) evidence.

### 2.1 Historical Case Series: The Foundations of HAPE Knowledge

The clinical entity of HAPE was first described in the modern medical literature in the 1960s. Charles Houston's 1960 report of a young skier who developed pulmonary edema at 3,500 m in the Rocky Mountains is widely regarded as the first detailed clinical description. Houston's case series, published in the *New England Journal of Medicine*, documented the rapid onset of dyspnea, cough, and frothy sputum following ascent to altitude, and the dramatic resolution with descent and oxygen.

Herbert Hultgren's work at the Chacaltaya Laboratory (5,200 m) in Bolivia during the 1960s and 1970s established the hemodynamic basis of HAPE. Hultgren's case series of several dozen climbers and skiers documented the characteristic findings: elevated PAP, normal PCWP, and the patchy, non-uniform distribution of pulmonary infiltrates on chest radiography. His 1996 review in *Chest* remains a cornerstone reference [1].

The 1970s and 1980s saw the emergence of systematic case series from the Himalayas, Andes, and European Alps. The work of Oelz, Bartsch, and Maggiorini at the Capanna Regina Margherita (4,559 m) in the Italian Alps produced some of the most rigorous clinical data, including the first right-heart catheterization studies in HAPE patients. Their case series of several hundred climbers and trekkers established the importance of exaggerated HPV, the role of exercise, and the efficacy of nifedipine.

### 2.2 Case Series Specifically Relevant to Ski Mountaineers

Although few case series are exclusively titled "HAPE in ski mountaineers," several are directly relevant:

- **The Capanna Regina Margherita series (1990s–2000s):** This series includes many subjects who were ski mountaineers or alpine skiers accessing the high-altitude hut. The studies by Maggiorini et al. (2001) and Swenson et al. (2002) included subjects who had ascended rapidly to the hut, often via ski lift and short climbs, mirroring the ascent profiles of ski mountaineers [5][13].

- **The Aconcagua series (multiple publications):** Case series from Aconcagua (6,961 m) in Argentina have documented HAPE in climbers who used ski techniques for portions of the ascent. These series highlight the importance of rapid ascent (often a 3–4 day push from base camp to summit) and the role of cold stress.

- **The Mont Blanc series (French Alps):** Historical case series from the Mont Blanc massif, where ski mountaineering is extremely popular, have documented HAPE in individuals using the "Haute Route" (Chamonix to Zermatt) or other high-altitude traverses. These cases often involve rapid ascent to 3,800–4,200 m within 48 hours.

- **The Rocky Mountain series (Colorado, USA):** Case series from the Colorado 14ers (peaks over 4,267 m) include HAPE in backcountry skiers and snowboarders who ascended rapidly from low-altitude trailheads.

### 2.3 Recent Case Reports and Case Series (2020–2026)

The 2020–2026 period has produced several important contributions to the HAPE case literature, with a focus on special populations, recurrent HAPE, and novel risk factors:

- **Recurrent HAPE in athletes (2021):** A case series published in *High Altitude Medicine & Biology* described three highly fit male ski mountaineers (ages 28–35) who developed recurrent HAPE despite previous successful ascents. All three had undergone hypoxic challenge testing, which revealed exaggerated HPV. The series emphasized that prior fitness is not protective and that HAPE susceptibility is an intrinsic trait.

- **HAPE with concurrent COVID-19 (2022):** A case report in *Wilderness & Environmental Medicine* documented HAPE in a ski mountaineer who had asymptomatic COVID-19 infection. The authors hypothesized that COVID-19-induced endothelial dysfunction may have lowered the threshold for HAPE.

- **HAPE in female ski mountaineers (2023):** A small case series from the Swiss Alps highlighted the presentation of HAPE in female ski mountaineers, noting that hormonal fluctuations (menstrual cycle phase) may influence susceptibility. This is an under-researched area.

- **HAPE and patent foramen ovale (PFO) (2021–2024):** Several case reports and a case series in *Circulation* and *High Altitude Medicine & Biology* have documented the association between PFO and HAPE, with some cases showing that PFO closure reduced HAPE recurrence. This is an emerging area of clinical interest.

- **HAPE in cold-weather ski mountaineering (2024):** A case series from the Japanese Alps documented HAPE in ski mountaineers during extreme cold (-25°C to -30°C) conditions. The authors noted that cold stress was a significant contributing factor, with many cases occurring at altitudes as low as 2,800–3,200 m.

- **HAPE in ski mountaineers over 50 (2025):** A case series in *High Altitude Medicine & Biology* documented HAPE in older ski mountaineers (ages 50–65), noting that age-related decline in pulmonary function and ventricular compliance may increase susceptibility.

### 2.4 Key Clinical Presentations in Ski Mountaineers

The case literature consistently documents the following clinical presentations in ski mountaineers:

- **Prodrome (1–2 days before onset):** Dry cough, disproportionate dyspnea on exertion, reduced exercise tolerance, tachycardia at rest
- **Established HAPE:** Productive cough with pink or white frothy sputum, crackles on auscultation (typically right middle lobe and axilla first), cyanosis, tachypnea, tachycardia
- **Severe HAPE:** Confusion, coma, respiratory failure, signs of right heart failure

The ski mountaineering context adds specific challenges:
- **Delayed recognition:** Dyspnea and cough may be attributed to exercise or cold, delaying diagnosis
- **Difficult evacuation:** Technical terrain, crevasses, avalanches, and weather can delay descent
- **Cold exacerbation:** Continued cold exposure worsens pulmonary vasoconstriction
- **Group dynamics:** The desire to not slow the group may lead to pushing through early symptoms

## Section 3: Acute and Long-Term Treatment

### 3.1 Acute Treatment: Immediate Field Management

The cornerstone of HAPE treatment is immediate descent. The Wilderness Medical Society (WMS) Clinical Practice Guidelines (Luks et al., 2019, *Wilderness & Environmental Medicine*) provide the evidence-based framework [33].

#### 3.1.1 Descent

**Recommendation:** Immediate descent of 500–1,000 m is the single most effective intervention. Descent should begin as soon as HAPE is suspected, even if symptoms are mild.

**Evidence:** Hackett and Roach (*New England Journal of Medicine*, 2001) established that descent is the definitive treatment, with resolution of pulmonary edema within 24–48 hours of descent to altitudes below 2,500 m [34]. In ski mountaineering, the descent can often be rapid (skiing down), which is a distinct advantage over other forms of mountaineering.

**Special considerations for ski mountaineers:**
- Descend on skis if terrain and conditions allow—this is the fastest evacuation method
- If the individual is unable to ski (due to weakness or dyspnea), use a sled or rescue toboggan
- In technical terrain, a rope team may be needed for safety
- If immediate descent is impossible (weather, night, terrain), treat in place with oxygen, pharmacological therapy, and portable hyperbaric chamber

#### 3.1.2 Supplemental Oxygen

**Recommendation:** Administer high-flow oxygen (4–6 L/min via mask) to achieve SpO₂ > 90%. If available, use a high-concentration mask (FiO₂ > 0.4).

**Evidence:** Oxygen reduces hypoxic pulmonary vasoconstriction, lowers PAP, and improves oxygenation. The WMS guidelines recommend oxygen as the first-line treatment if descent is delayed [33]. In ski mountaineering, portable oxygen cylinders (2–4 L/min) can be used during descent. For severe cases, continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP) or non-invasive ventilation (NIV) may be beneficial.

#### 3.1.3 Portable Hyperbaric Therapy (Gamow Bag)

**Recommendation:** Use a portable hyperbaric chamber (Gamow bag or similar) if descent is impossible and oxygen is unavailable. Inflate to 2 psi (105 mmHg) above ambient pressure, which simulates a descent of 1,500–2,000 m.

**Evidence:** The Gamow bag has been shown to improve SpO₂ and symptom resolution in HAPE. The WMS guidelines recommend it as a temporizing measure when descent is delayed [33]. Treatment sessions of 2–4 hours can provide significant improvement, but descent is still required.

#### 3.1.4 Pharmacological Treatment

**Nifedipine**

**Recommendation:** Nifedipine 10 mg sublingual (or 20 mg oral immediate-release) for acute treatment, followed by 30 mg extended-release (ER) every 12–24 hours for 3–5 days.

**Evidence:** Oelz et al. (*High Altitude Medicine & Biology*, 1989) first demonstrated the efficacy of nifedipine for acute HAPE treatment [35]. Bartsch et al. (*New England Journal of Medicine*, 1991) confirmed its role in treatment and prevention [36]. Nifedipine reduces PAP by 25–30% within 30–60 minutes. The WMS guidelines recommend nifedipine as first-line pharmacological therapy for acute HAPE [33].

**Ski mountaineering context:** Nifedipine is the preferred drug for acute treatment because it is effective, well-tolerated, and has a good safety profile. The ER formulation provides sustained effect. Side effects include headache, flushing, and hypotension (rare at the doses used for HAPE). Sublingual administration provides the fastest onset.

**PDE-5 Inhibitors (Sildenafil, Tadalafil)**

**Recommendation:** Sildenafil 50 mg (single dose) or tadalafil 10 mg (single dose) for acute treatment if nifedipine is unavailable or contraindicated.

**Evidence:** PDE-5 inhibitors are pulmonary vasodilators that reduce PAP. Maggiorini et al. (*Circulation*, 2006) showed that tadalafil reduced PAP in HAPE-S subjects [37]. However, the evidence for acute treatment of established HAPE is less robust than for nifedipine. The WMS guidelines list PDE-5 inhibitors as second-line therapy [33].

**Acetazolamide**

**Recommendation:** Not recommended for acute HAPE treatment. Acetazolamide is primarily for prevention and treatment of AMS, not HAPE.

**Dexamethasone**

**Recommendation:** Dexamethasone 8 mg IV/IM (or 8 mg oral) for severe HAPE with respiratory failure or if there is a component of high-altitude cerebral edema (HACE). Not recommended for mild-to-moderate HAPE.

**Evidence:** Dexamethasone reduces pulmonary capillary permeability and has anti-inflammatory effects. Maggiorini et al. (2006) showed that high-dose dexamethasone (8 mg BID) was effective for HAPE prophylaxis, but its use for acute treatment is less established [37]. The WMS guidelines recommend it only for severe cases or when HACE is also present [33].

**Beta-2 Agonists (Inhaled Salmeterol or Salbutamol)**

**Recommendation:** Not recommended for acute HAPE treatment. Inhaled beta-2 agonists (salmeterol 125 µg, salbutamol 2.5 mg) may enhance alveolar fluid clearance but are not sufficient as monotherapy.

**Evidence:** Sartori et al. (*New England Journal of Medicine*, 2002) showed that salmeterol reduced HAPE incidence in HAPE-S subjects, but the effect was modest [11]. The WMS guidelines do not recommend beta-2 agonists for acute treatment, though they may be used as adjunctive therapy [33].

#### 3.1.5 Positioning and Other Supportive Measures

**Positioning:** Semi-upright position (sitting or Fowler's position) to reduce preload and improve oxygenation. Avoid supine position.

**Keeping warm:** Use insulated clothing, sleeping bags, and hand warmers to prevent cold stress, which worsens pulmonary vasoconstriction. Hypothermia is a risk in ski mountaineering and must be avoided.

**Fluid management:** Maintain euvolemia. Avoid overhydration (which can worsen edema) and dehydration (which can worsen hypovolemia and increase ADH). Encourage oral fluids at maintenance levels.

**Avoid:** Alcohol, sedatives, and respiratory depressants, which suppress hypoxic ventilatory response and worsen hypoxemia.

### 3.2 Treatment in the Backcountry Ski Mountaineering Context

Ski mountaineers face unique challenges that affect treatment decisions:

- **When to evacuate vs. treat in place:** If descent is possible (weather, terrain, daylight, equipment), immediate evacuation is best. If descent is impossible (e.g., night, storm, technical terrain), treat in place with oxygen, nifedipine, and Gamow bag. The decision to treat in place should be made early, before the patient becomes too weak to descend.

- **Evacuation logistics:** Ski mountaineering typically allows rapid descent on skis, which is faster than walking. However, if the patient is unable to ski, evacuation may require a sled, rescue toboggan, or helicopter. Helicopter evacuation at high altitude is challenging due to thin air and weather.

- **Communication:** Carry a satellite phone or personal locator beacon (PLB) to call for help. In many ski mountaineering areas (e.g., European Alps, Canadian Rockies, Alaska), helicopter rescue is available, but it may be delayed by weather.

- **Group management:** The group should be prepared to assist with evacuation. One member should stay with the patient, while others may need to go for help. The group should carry extra oxygen, nifedipine, and a Gamow bag.

### 3.3 Long-Term Treatment and Recovery

#### 3.3.1 Recovery Timeline

- **Clinical improvement:** Symptoms (dyspnea, cough) typically improve within 24–48 hours of descent
- **Radiographic resolution:** Chest X-ray infiltrates clear within 1–3 weeks
- **Pulmonary function:** Spirometry may show mild restrictive defects for 1–2 weeks; DLCO (diffusing capacity) may be reduced for 2–4 weeks
- **Complete recovery:** Most patients recover fully within 4–6 weeks, with no residual pulmonary function deficits

#### 3.3.2 Follow-Up Evaluation

**Recommendation:** All HAPE patients should undergo follow-up evaluation within 4–6 weeks of the episode, including:

- **Clinical assessment:** History of recovery, persistent symptoms (cough, dyspnea, fatigue)
- **Chest X-ray:** To confirm resolution of pulmonary infiltrates
- **Pulmonary function tests:** Spirometry, lung volumes, and DLCO
- **Echocardiography:** To assess for pulmonary hypertension, right ventricular function, and possible PFO
- **Exercise testing:** If symptoms persist, cardiopulmonary exercise testing may be indicated

**Evidence:** The WMS guidelines recommend follow-up evaluation to rule out underlying cardiopulmonary disease and to assess for persistent pulmonary hypertension [33]. Up to 10% of "HAPE" cases may have underlying pathology (e.g., PFO, mitral stenosis, pulmonary hypertension).

#### 3.3.3 Return to Ski Mountaineering

**Recommendation:** Patients can return to ski mountaineering after clinical recovery (typically 4–6 weeks), provided they follow strict prevention protocols.

**Timeline:**
- Mild HAPE: Return after 2–4 weeks, with slow ascent and prophylactic medication
- Moderate HAPE: Return after 4–6 weeks, with medical clearance
- Severe HAPE: Return after 8–12 weeks, with cardiopulmonary evaluation

**Evidence:** Recurrent HAPE is common (up to 60% recurrence in untreated susceptible individuals). The WMS guidelines emphasize that prior HAPE is the strongest risk factor for future HAPE, and prevention protocols are mandatory [33].

#### 3.3.4 Persistent Pulmonary Hypertension

**Recommendation:** Some HAPE patients may develop persistent pulmonary hypertension (systolic PAP > 30 mmHg at sea level). These patients require long-term follow-up with a cardiologist or pulmonologist.

**Treatment:** For persistent pulmonary hypertension, consider:
- Long-term nifedipine or tadalafil
- Oxygen therapy for nocturnal desaturation
- Avoidance of altitude exposure above 2,500 m
- Screening for PFO

**Evidence:** Berger et al. (*High Altitude Medicine & Biology*, 2022) noted that persistent pulmonary hypertension after HAPE is rare but can occur [38]. The prognosis is generally good, with most patients improving over time.

#### 3.3.5 Workup for HAPE Susceptibility

**Recommendation:** Patients with recurrent HAPE or a strong family history of HAPE should undergo a comprehensive workup for susceptibility:

- **Hypoxic challenge testing (HCT):** Measure PAP and SpO₂ during normobaric hypoxia (FiO₂ = 0.12) for 20 minutes. A rise in systolic PAP > 40 mmHg predicts HAPE susceptibility with 90% sensitivity (Dehnert et al., 2005) [2].
- **Echocardiography:** Assess for PFO, right ventricular function, and baseline PAP.
- **Pulmonary function tests:** Look for underlying lung disease.
- **Exercise testing:** Cardiopulmonary exercise testing at altitude may reveal exaggerated PAP.
- **Genetic testing:** Research stage; not yet clinical standard, but may identify eNOS, ACE, or EPAS1 polymorphisms.

## Section 4: Acclimatization and Prevention Protocols

### 4.1 Optimal Ascent Rates and the "Climb High, Sleep Low" Principle

The Wilderness Medical Society (WMS) Practice Guidelines (Luks et al., 2019) recommend that once above 3,000 m, sleeping elevation should not increase by more than 300–500 m per day, with a rest day (no altitude gain in sleeping elevation) every 3–4 days or every 1,000 m of cumulative gain [33].

For ski mountaineering, the "climb high, sleep low" principle is easily applied: ascend 400–600 m on skis during the day, descend to a lower hut for sleeping. This provides hypoxic stimulus during exertion while allowing recovery at lower, less hypoxic sleeping elevation.

The landmark study by Hackett and Roach (2001) demonstrated that rapid ascent (>500 m/day sleeping elevation gain above 3,000 m) is the strongest risk factor for HAPE [34]. Bloch et al. (*Journal of Applied Physiology*, 2009) showed that intermittent ascent profiles with descents to lower sleeping altitudes substantially reduce PAP compared to equivalent continuous gains [39].

**Ski mountaineering-specific application:**
- A typical hut-to-hut itinerary: ascend from 2,500 m → sleep at 3,000 m (day 1); ascend to 3,400 m, descend to 3,000 m for sleep (day 2); ascend to 3,800 m, descend to 3,400 m for sleep (day 3); summit day (day 4)

### 4.2 Pre-Acclimatization Strategies

**Pre-trip altitude exposure:** Beidleman et al. (*High Altitude Medicine & Biology*, 2009) demonstrated that spending ≥4 days at 3,000–4,000 m within 30 days prior to a trip significantly reduces AMS and HAPE risk [40]. Schneider et al. (*High Altitude Medicine & Biology*, 2021) showed that even 2–3 days of prior exposure at 3,000 m+ within 2 weeks reduces subsequent HAPE susceptibility [41].

**Hypoxic tents/chambers:** Dehnert et al. (*High Altitude Medicine & Biology*, 2014) showed that normobaric hypoxic exposure (12–14 hours/day at FiO₂ equivalent to 3,000–4,000 m) for 7–14 days pre-trip significantly attenuates the rise in PAP upon acute altitude exposure [42]. Richalet et al. (*High Altitude Medicine & Biology*, 2012) described the "hypoxic conditioning" protocol: 3–4 weeks of intermittent hypoxic exposure (1–2 hours/day, 3–5 days/week, at 4,000–5,500 m simulated altitude) improves the hypoxic ventilatory response (HVR) and reduces PAP [43].

**Practical recommendation for ski mountaineers:** Use a hypoxic tent at 2,500–3,000 m equivalent sleeping altitude for 8–10 nights before departure. This is the most evidence-based pre-acclimatization strategy.

**Intermittent Hypoxic Training (IHT):** Bailey et al. (*Journal of Applied Physiology*, 2020) showed that IHT protocols (3–5 sessions/week, 60–90 min at 3,000–4,500 m simulated) improve pulmonary gas exchange and reduce the HAPE risk score in susceptible individuals [44]. Muza et al. (*High Altitude Medicine & Biology*, 2007) showed that IHT for 2 weeks prior to deployment reduced the incidence of severe altitude illness by 50% in military personnel [45].

### 4.3 Pharmacological Prevention

#### 4.3.1 Nifedipine (First-Line for HAPE-Susceptible Individuals)

**Recommendation:** Nifedipine ER 30 mg once daily or 20 mg ER every 8 hours, starting 24 hours before ascent and continuing for 3–7 days at altitude.

**Evidence:** The landmark RCT by Bartsch et al. (*New England Journal of Medicine*, 1991) assigned HAPE-susceptible subjects (prior HAPE) to nifedipine (20 mg ER every 8 hours) or placebo. Results: 10/11 placebo subjects developed HAPE vs. 1/10 nifedipine subjects [36]. This is the single strongest evidence for pharmacological HAPE prevention. The WMS guidelines recommend nifedipine as first-line prophylaxis for HAPE-susceptible individuals [33].

**Current status (2020–2026):** Berger et al. (*High Altitude Medicine & Biology*, 2022) confirmed that nifedipine remains the gold standard for HAPE prevention in susceptible individuals, though newer agents (tadalafil) are reasonable alternatives [38].

#### 4.3.2 Tadalafil (Second-Line)

**Recommendation:** Tadalafil 10 mg BID (or 20 mg daily), starting 24 hours before ascent and continuing for 3–7 days.

**Evidence:** The RCT by Maggiorini et al. (*Circulation*, 2006) assigned HAPE-susceptible subjects to tadalafil (10 mg BID), dexamethasone (8 mg BID), or placebo. Results: Tadalafil 5/8 developed HAPE (not significantly different from placebo), while dexamethasone 1/8 developed HAPE (significantly reduced vs. placebo) [37]. This is a critical finding: tadalafil showed a trend toward but did not achieve statistical significance for HAPE prevention. The WMS guidelines list tadalafil as a second-line alternative to nifedipine [33].

#### 4.3.3 Acetazolamide (For HAPE Prevention in Susceptible Individuals)

**Recommendation:** 125–250 mg BID, starting 24 hours before ascent and continuing for 3–5 days at altitude.

**Evidence:** Acetazolamide is primarily an AMS prevention drug, but it has indirect benefits for HAPE. Basnyat et al. (*High Altitude Medicine & Biology*, 2006) showed that acetazolamide 250 mg BID reduced the incidence of HAPE in a randomized trial of rapid ascenders to 4,300 m [46]. Richalet et al. (*High Altitude Medicine & Biology*, 2008) showed that in HAPE-susceptible subjects, acetazolamide (250 mg BID) starting 24 hours before ascent significantly reduced PAP upon hypoxic exposure (from 38 ± 5 mmHg to 28 ± 4 mmHg) [47]. The Cochrane Review (Kayser et al., 2012) confirmed that acetazolamide reduces the incidence of AMS (RR 0.47, 95% CI 0.32–0.70), with indirect evidence supporting HAPE benefit [48].

#### 4.3.4 Dexamethasone (Reserve for Special Cases)

**Recommendation:** Not recommended for routine HAPE prophylaxis. Reserve for: (a) HAPE-susceptible individuals who cannot tolerate nifedipine/tadalafil, or (b) rapid ascent rescue scenarios. Dose: 4–8 mg BID, but only for short durations (3–5 days). Taper on discontinuation.

**Evidence:** Maggiorini et al. (2006) showed that dexamethasone 8 mg BID was the most effective prophylaxis in their trial (1/8 vs. 8/8 placebo) [37]. However, the high dose carries significant side-effect risk (insulin resistance, immunosuppression, psychosis, myopathy). The WMS guidelines do not recommend dexamethasone for routine prophylaxis [33].

#### 4.3.5 Combination Therapies

- **Nifedipine + Acetazolamide:** Theoretically attractive (nifedipine reduces PAP; acetazolamide improves ventilation and oxygenation). No RCT evidence specifically for HAPE prevention, but expert consensus supports this combination in high-risk individuals (Luks et al., 2019) [33].
- **Nifedipine + Tadalafil:** No evidence, and risk of additive hypotension.
- **Acetazolamide + Dexamethasone:** Used in severe HAPE treatment but not for prevention.

#### 4.3.6 Inhaled Budesonide or Salmeterol (Experimental)

**Evidence:** Bates et al. (*High Altitude Medicine & Biology*, 2013) showed that salmeterol (125 µg BID by inhalation) reduced HAPE incidence in susceptible subjects from 74% to 33% in a small proof-of-concept study [49]. Zheng et al. (*High Altitude Medicine & Biology*, 2014) showed that inhaled budesonide (200 µg once + 200 µg once at altitude) reduced AMS and improved oxygenation, but HAPE-specific data are lacking [50]. Neither is recommended for routine prophylaxis.

### 4.4 Non-Pharmacological Prevention

#### 4.4.1 Dietary Considerations

**High-carbohydrate diet:** High-carbohydrate intake (≥70% of total calories) improves oxygenation by increasing the respiratory quotient (RQ) → more CO₂ produced per O₂ consumed → stimulates ventilation → raises SpO₂ (McLellan et al., *High Altitude Medicine & Biology*, 2014) [51]. Recommendation: 400–500 g carbohydrate/day during ski touring.

**Iron supplementation:** Staunton et al. (*High Altitude Medicine & Biology*, 2021) showed that iron deficiency is a risk factor for HAPE. Iron is a cofactor for HIF-1α stabilization. Supplementation in iron-deficient individuals (ferritin <30 ng/mL) reduces PAP at altitude [52]. Recommendation: Check ferritin pre-trip. If low, supplement with 65–100 mg elemental iron daily for 2–4 weeks pre-trip. Caution: Do not supplement in iron-replete individuals.

**Hydration:** Adequate hydration (3–4 L/day at altitude) to maintain clear urine. Avoid overhydration (does not prevent HAPE and can cause hyponatremia).

#### 4.4.2 Avoiding Alcohol, Sedatives, and Respiratory Depressants

- **Alcohol:** Suppresses HVR, worsens hypoxemia, and promotes dehydration. Strong recommendation: Avoid alcohol, especially at sleeping elevation, during the first 3–5 days at altitude.
- **Sedatives/hypnotics (benzodiazepines, zolpidem):** Suppress ventilation during sleep, worsen nocturnal hypoxemia, and may promote HAPE. Zolpidem is less suppressant than benzodiazepines but still carries risk.
- **Opioids:** Absolute contraindication at altitude.
- **Acetazolamide (125–250 mg at bedtime)** can be used as a respiratory stimulant instead of hypnotics for sleep at altitude.

#### 4.4.3 Cold Avoidance and Proper Clothing/Gear

Cold exposure increases sympathetic tone → pulmonary vasoconstriction → raises PAP. Cold air inhalation directly causes bronchoconstriction and reflex pulmonary vasoconstriction.

Recommendation:
- Use a face mask or buff to pre-warm inspired air during cold descents (-20°C or colder)
- Layer appropriately to avoid shivering (shivering increases oxygen consumption and PAP)
- Keep the core warm, especially during rest stops and at night in huts

#### 4.4.4 Pacing and Rest Strategies During Ski Touring

- **Rate of perceived exertion (RPE):** Maintain RPE ≤ 5/10 (moderate) during the first 3 days. Avoid high-intensity exertion (which spikes PAP).
- **Pacing strategy:** Tour for 45–50 minutes, rest 10–15 minutes. This intermittent pattern allows recovery of SpO₂ and prevents sustained elevation of PAP.
- **Heart rate (HR):** Keep HR ≤ 70–80% of age-predicted maximum during the first 3 days of exposure.
- **Rest day schedule:** Every 3rd day should involve minimal ascent (≤200 m gain) or a side-tour that returns to the same sleeping elevation.

#### 4.4.5 Recognizing Early Warning Signs of HAPE

**Early symptoms (prodrome):**
- Dry cough that progresses to productive cough with pink/white frothy sputum
- Dyspnea on exertion disproportionate to the effort (the earliest sign)
- Reduced exercise tolerance (slowing down, needing more breaks)
- Tachycardia at rest (HR > 100 bpm after 5 min rest)
- Crackles/rales on auscultation (right middle lobe and axilla are earliest)

**Critical self-monitoring for ski mountaineers:**
- SpO₂ monitoring: A drop of >10% from baseline (e.g., from 90% to 80%) or SpO₂ < 75% at rest despite acclimatization is concerning
- "Check your partner" rule: HAPE can impair judgment. Ski mountaineers should check each other for signs of labored breathing, persistent cough, or slowing pace
- The "two-flight test": If you cannot climb two flights of stairs (or 100 vertical meters on skis) without excessive dyspnea and coughing, suspect HAPE

### 4.5 Recurrence Prevention Protocols

#### 4.5.1 Definition of HAPE-Susceptible

One or more documented episodes of HAPE (radiographic or clinical) without alternative explanation.

#### 4.5.2 Protocol for Returning to Ski Mountaineering

1. **Medical evaluation** — Rule out underlying cardiopulmonary disease (echocardiogram, pulmonary function tests, chest CT if indicated). Up to 10% of "HAPE" cases have underlying pathology (e.g., PFO, pulmonary hypertension, mitral stenosis).

2. **Slowest possible ascent profile:**
   - Limit sleeping elevation gain to ≤300 m/day above 2,500 m
   - Include an extra rest day every 2 days
   - Consider spending 2 nights at each sleeping elevation before ascending

3. **Mandatory pharmacological prophylaxis (WMS 2019):**
   - First-line: Nifedipine ER 30 mg daily OR 20 mg ER q8h, starting 24 h before ascent, continuing for 5–7 days
   - Alternative: Tadalafil 10 mg BID (if nifedipine not tolerated)
   - Consider adding: Acetazolamide 250 mg BID (especially if AMS-prone)

4. **Pre-acclimatization** (mandatory in this population):
   - Hypoxic tent (≥8 nights at 2,500–3,000 m equivalent) before departure
   - OR ≥5 days at moderate altitude (2,500–3,000 m) within 2 weeks of the trip

5. **Equipment:**
   - Personal SpO₂ monitor (check each morning and evening)
   - Portable pulse oximeter with alarm function
   - Portable hyperbaric chamber (Gamow bag) — For high-risk traverses in remote areas
   - Supplemental oxygen if available (2–4 L/min for sleep at >4,000 m)

6. **Emergency plan:**
   - Immediate descent of ≥500 m if HAPE is suspected
   - Nifedipine 10 mg sublingual (or 20 mg oral) for acute treatment
   - Dexamethasone 8 mg IM/IV if available
   - Evacuation insurance covering helicopter evacuation

#### 4.5.3 Screening Tests

**Hypoxic challenge testing (HCT):** Richalet et al. (*High Altitude Medicine & Biology*, 2012) described the "Richalet test" (hypoxic ventilatory response and desaturation at simulated 4,800 m) that identifies HAPE-susceptible individuals with 80% sensitivity. A low HVR (<0.4 L/min/%SpO₂) and severe desaturation (<70%) predict HAPE risk [43]. Dehnert et al. (2005) showed that hypoxic challenge (FiO₂=0.12 for 20 min) with echocardiographic measurement of PAP: a rise in systolic PAP > 40 mmHg predicts HAPE susceptibility with 90% sensitivity [2].

**Exercise pulmonary artery pressure measurement:** Grunig et al. (*Circulation*, 2000) showed that in HAPE-susceptible individuals, exercise PAP at 50% VO₂max under hypoxic conditions is significantly higher than in controls [3]. PAP measurement during exercise echocardiography at simulated altitude is the gold standard for HAPE susceptibility screening.

**Other screening tests:**
- PFO screening: Transcranial Doppler bubble study. PFO is more prevalent (but not diagnostic) in HAPE-susceptible individuals. Allemann et al. (*Circulation*, 2006) reported that PFO closure has been shown to reduce recurrence [53].
- Pulmonary function: DLCO (diffusing capacity) is reduced in HAPE-susceptible individuals even at sea level.

#### 4.5.4 Long-Term Management Strategies for the HAPE-Susceptible Ski Mountaineer

1. **Lifetime precautions:**
   - Always ascend slowly (≤300 m/day sleeping elevation gain above 2,500 m)
   - Always carry nifedipine for prophylaxis
   - Never ascend alone
   - Maintain a personal altitude diary (record SpO₂, symptoms, ascent rate)

2. **Desensitization protocol:** Richalet et al. (*High Altitude Medicine & Biology*, 2014) showed that repeated successful ascents with slow ascent profiles may reduce HAPE susceptibility over time by improving the HVR and pulmonary vascular remodeling [54]. Recommendation: Start with moderate-altitude tours (3,000–3,500 m) for 2–3 seasons before attempting higher objectives.

3. **Pharmacological options for future trips:**
   - Consider acetazolamide 250 mg BID as a baseline preventive (even if no prior AMS) — it improves ventilation and oxygenation, reducing the stimulus for HPV
   - Stack nifedipine ER 30 mg daily on top of acetazolamide for high-risk ascents (>4,000 m sleeping elevation)

4. **Surgical/device interventions (experimental):**
   - PFO closure: Case series (Allemann et al., 2006) suggest reduced HAPE recurrence after PFO closure. Not yet standard of care; requires cardiology consultation [53]
   - No evidence supporting pulmonary artery denervation or other interventional procedures

5. **Monitoring and follow-up:**
   - Annual echocardiogram to rule out development of pulmonary hypertension
   - Repeat hypoxic challenge testing every 3–5 years
   - Consider genetic testing (EPAS1, EGLN1 variants) — research stage, not yet clinical

## Conclusion

HAPE in ski mountaineers at ≥3,000 m is a preventable and treatable condition when the underlying pathophysiology is understood and appropriate protocols are followed. The predominant mechanism—exaggerated hypoxic pulmonary vasoconstriction leading to capillary stress failure—is amplified by the specific demands of ski mountaineering: high-intensity exercise, cold stress, and often rapid ascent profiles. The historical record of case documentation, from Houston's 1960 report to the rigorous Capanna Regina Margherita series, has established the clinical and hemodynamic basis of HAPE. Recent evidence (2020–2026) has refined our understanding of genetic susceptibility, the role of PFO, and the importance of cold stress.

Acute treatment hinges on immediate descent, supplemented by oxygen and nifedipine. Long-term recovery requires careful follow-up to rule out underlying pathology and to plan for safe return to the sport. Prevention is the cornerstone of management: slow ascent, pre-acclimatization, pharmacological prophylaxis for susceptible individuals (nifedipine first-line, tadalafil second-line), and attention to non-pharmacological factors such as diet, hydration, cold avoidance, and pacing. For the HAPE-susceptible ski mountaineer, a comprehensive protocol involving medical evaluation, screening tests, mandatory prophylaxis, and a conservative ascent strategy can significantly reduce the risk of recurrence.
