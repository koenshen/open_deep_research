# U.S. Electric Vehicle Market Report: Comprehensive Analysis as of July 22, 2026

## Executive Summary

The U.S. electric vehicle market has undergone a dramatic transformation over the past 18 months, transitioning from a period of record growth and policy support to a phase of contraction and recalibration. After reaching a peak market share of 10.5% in Q3 2025, EV sales collapsed following the expiration of the federal $7,500 tax credit on September 30, 2025, under the One Big Beautiful Bill Act (OBBBA). By Q2 2026, EV market share had stabilized at approximately 5.8%, with sales showing signs of recovery but remaining 20.5% below year-ago levels. This report examines the current state of the U.S. EV market across five critical dimensions: market size and sales trends, manufacturer competitive landscape, charging infrastructure development, policy and regulatory environment, and battery technology and supply chain dynamics.

## 1. Market Size, Growth Rate, and Segmentation

### 1.1 Historical Sales Trajectory

The U.S. EV market experienced a remarkable growth trajectory from 2020 through Q3 2025, punctuated by a sharp reversal in late 2025. According to Cox Automotive and Kelley Blue Book, full-year 2024 EV sales reached a record 1.3 million units, capturing 8.1% of total new-vehicle sales, up from 7.8% in 2023 [2][7]. The International Energy Agency (IEA) reported a broader figure of 1.6 million electric cars sold in the United States in 2024, representing a 10% sales share [10].

The first half of 2025 showed continued momentum, with Q2 2025 sales reaching 310,839 units and first-half totals hitting a record 607,089 units [23]. The market peaked in Q3 2025 at 459,039 registrations, a 22% year-over-year increase, driven by consumers rushing to lock in the federal tax credit before its expiration [17][18]. EV market share reached an all-time high of 10.5% during this quarter [18].

**The Turning Point:** When the federal $7,500 tax credit expired on September 30, 2025, EV sales collapsed. Q4 2025 saw approximately 234,000 units sold, a 46% decline from Q3 and a 36% drop year-over-year, marking the lowest quarterly volume since Q4 2022 [18][20]. Market share fell to 5.7-5.8% [8].

### 1.2 Current Market Status (2026)

**Q1 2026:** EV sales fell 27% year-over-year to 216,399 units, with market share stabilizing at 5.8% [37][38]. The decline rate improved from Q4 2025's 36% drop, suggesting a floor was being reached.

**Q2 2026:** Sales rebounded to 247,226 units, a 14.2% quarter-over-quarter increase, though still down 20.5% year-over-year [20][34]. This represented the highest quarterly volume since the tax credit ended. EVs accounted for 5.8% of total new-vehicle sales, unchanged from Q1 [34][36].

**First Half 2026 Total:** Approximately 463,625 EV sales (216,399 + 247,226), with new EV sales falling approximately 25% overall in 2026 according to early estimates [43].

### 1.4 Cumulative EV Registrations

As of early 2026, California had approximately 1.26 million EV registrations, representing about 35% of the national total [1][11]. Florida followed with 254,878 registrations and Texas with 230,125 [1]. Globally, there are now almost 80 million electric cars in use, up from 26 million in 2022 [14][16].

### 1.5 Forecasts and Projections

The outlook for the U.S. EV market has been revised downward significantly. BloombergNEF predicts a 15% decline in U.S. EV sales for 2026, though global sales may rise driven by Europe and China [4]. Benchmark Mineral Intelligence forecasts the U.S. market will shrink by almost a third in 2026, marking the first decline in seven years [19]. Cox Automotive expects 2026 EV share near 8%, supported by new affordable models, improving infrastructure, and gradual consumer confidence growth [18].

Looking further ahead, the IEA projects that under current policies, the U.S. EV sales share could reach approximately 20% by 2030, roughly half of earlier projections [28]. BloombergNEF expects the share of EVs in passenger car sales to reach 24% by 2030, significantly lower than the 46% it projected in 2024 [5].

## 2. Key Drivers and Challenges

### 2.1 Policy Incentives: The End of an Era

**Federal Tax Credit Elimination:** The most significant policy change was the passage of the One Big Beautiful Bill Act (OBBBA), signed into law on July 4, 2025, which eliminated the federal $7,500 New Clean Vehicle Credit, the $4,000 Used Clean Vehicle Credit, and the $7,500 Commercial Clean Vehicle Credit for vehicles acquired after September 30, 2025 [IRS, AFDC, multiple sources]. The leasing loophole that allowed consumers to access the credit indirectly also closed on this date [CarsDirect, CBT News].

**Replacement Incentive:** The OBBBA introduced a recurring deduction of up to $10,000 per year in auto loan interest through 2028 for qualifying loans originated between January 1, 2025 and December 31, 2028. Eligibility requires a brand-new vehicle with final assembly in the United States (VIN starting with 1, 4, 5, or 7), personal use, and income limits [Clean Energy CU, Carfax].

**Remaining Federal Incentive:** The Section 30C home charger credit (30% up to $1,000) remains available for equipment installed before July 1, 2026, but is limited to low-income or non-urban census tracts [IRS, SolarReviews].

**State-Level Incentives:** With federal credits gone, state incentives have become critical. California's MyFirstEV program (SB 168), signed into law on July 13, 2026, provides a $3,500 point-of-sale rebate for first-time buyers of new EVs under $50,000 MSRP and $1,750 for used EVs under $25,000, funded by $135.5 million in state funding matched by automakers [Distil8, Electrek]. Colorado offers up to $3,250 in state tax credits, with the base credit dropping from $3,500 in 2025 to $750 in 2026 due to state revenue projections [Colorado Energy Office]. Oregon provides up to $7,500, and low-income Oregonians can stack rebates for up to $12,500 total [ElectricVehiclesHQ].

### 2.2 Charging Infrastructure

**Total Infrastructure:** As of June 22, 2026, the U.S. has 253,319 public EV charging ports across 82,309 station locations, representing a 15% year-over-year increase [21]. This includes 178,936 AC Level 2 ports and 73,951 DC fast-charging ports [2][21]. The average ports per DCFC site has grown to 4.8, up from 4.4 a year ago [2].

**Charging Network Market Share:** Tesla Superchargers dominate the DC fast-charging market with 37,736 ports, representing 51.0% of all DCFC ports [2]. Electrify America follows with 5,664 ports (7.7%), EVgo with 5,047 ports (6.8%), and ChargePoint with 4,777 ports (6.5%) [2]. Over two-thirds of Supercharger stalls are now open to non-Tesla EVs [2].

**NACS Adoption:** Nearly all major automakers have announced plans to adopt the North American Charging Standard (NACS), with most offering adapter access to Tesla Superchargers by 2024-2025 and native NACS ports rolling out from 2025 onward [32]. Stellantis was the last major holdout, announcing NACS adoption for EVs sold in North America from 2026 [51].

**Charging Reliability:** The J.D. Power 2025 U.S. EVX Public Charging Study found that 14% of EV owners reported a failed charging visit in 2025, down from 19% in 2024, representing a four-year low [2][5]. However, cost satisfaction dropped sharply, with DCFC cost satisfaction falling to 430 (-16 points) [5]. Tesla Superchargers ranked highest for the fifth consecutive year with a score of 709 [5].

**Federal Funding:** The NEVI (National Electric Vehicle Infrastructure) Formula Program allocated $5 billion over five years for states to deploy charging along designated Alternative Fuel Corridors [7][18]. However, as of January 2026, states had spent only 2% ($94 million) of the $4.4 billion in NEVI funding approved in 2021 [28]. The Trump administration froze funding in February 2025, though a federal court order reinstated it in June 2025 [41][42]. Revised NEVI guidance in August 2025 eliminated Biden-era requirements, including the requirement that chargers be no more than 50 miles apart [39].

### 2.3 Consumer Adoption Trends

**Purchase Intent Barriers:** The expiration of federal tax credits has significantly dampened consumer demand. The average transaction price for a new EV in April 2026 was $55,211, down 4.9% year-over-year [43]. Used EV prices averaged $35,895, with used EV sales rising 16.7% year-over-year and market share reaching a record 2.8% [43].

**Leasing Dominance:** In 2025, EVs made up over 22% of new-vehicle leasing, up from 17% in 2024 [2]. Nearly 60% of consumers who purchased a new EV in Q1 2025 leased their car, compared to only 25% of all new-car buyers [11]. The leasing loophole allowed consumers to access the $7,500 tax credit indirectly, though this ended on September 30, 2025.

**Affordability Gap:** The average EV transaction price was $55,544 as of December 2024, which is 12% higher than the overall new car market average of $49,740 [20]. However, the premium over gas cars has narrowed from 16% in 2024. The average starting MSRP for the top 10 best-selling EVs is $48,899, down 10% from six months prior [20].

### 2.4 Supply Chain and Cost Pressures

**Battery Costs:** Lithium-ion battery pack prices fell 20% in 2024, the largest drop since 2017, driven by low critical mineral prices and intense competition [7][10]. BloombergNEF reported average prices of $108/kWh in 2025, with China leading at $84/kWh [7]. Goldman Sachs projects global average prices dropping to $82/kWh by 2026, roughly half of 2023's $149/kWh [3][6]. At this level, battery electric vehicles would achieve ownership cost parity with gasoline-fueled cars in the U.S. on an unsubsidized basis [3][6].

**Critical Mineral Dependence:** The U.S. relies on China for 14 of its 33 most critical minerals [5]. China controls 60% of global production and 85% of processing capacity [3]. The U.S. holds less than 2% of global mineral extraction market share [3]. Specific dependencies include 100% imports for graphite, 75% for cobalt, and 50% for lithium [3].

**Manufacturing Scale:** U.S. battery manufacturing capacity has doubled since 2022, reaching over 200 GWh in 2024, with nearly 700 GWh of additional capacity under construction [10][24]. Automakers and battery manufacturers have collectively invested approximately $112 billion in building domestic cell and module manufacturing [22]. However, at least $19.9 billion in planned EV manufacturing investments were canceled since 2025, particularly in the Midwest and South [5].

## 3. Manufacturer Competitive Landscape

### 3.1 Market Share by Manufacturer

**2024 Full-Year Market Share:** Tesla held approximately 49% of the U.S. BEV market in 2024, with sales of roughly 634,000 units [1]. Non-Tesla automakers collectively sold about 960,000 EVs, a 20% increase from 2023, while Tesla's sales declined 5% [7].

**2025 Full-Year Market Share:** Tesla's share declined to 46% in 2025, with sales dropping to approximately 589,000 units (down 7% year-over-year) [1][2]. By auto group, GM surged to 13.2% (169,793 sales), Hyundai-Kia held 8.1%, Ford Motor Co. 6.6%, and Volkswagen Group 5.6% [1].

**Q4 2025 Reversal:** Following the tax credit expiration, Tesla's market share surged to 59% in Q4 2025, up from 41% in Q3, as legacy automakers' EV sales collapsed more sharply [8]. General Motors held second place at 10.8%, followed by Ford (6.2%) and Hyundai Motor Group (6.0%) [9].

**Q1 2026:** Tesla's U.S. sales dropped 8.4% year-over-year to 117,300 units, its lowest quarterly sales since late 2021. However, the overall U.S. EV market contracted by 27%, allowing Tesla's market share to jump to 54.2% [24].

**Tesla's Historical Trajectory:** Tesla's market share has declined from nearly 80% in 2019 to 43.9% for 2025, according to Edmunds [43]. The decline accelerated in 2025, with Tesla's share falling to 38% in August 2025, its lowest since October 2017 [21]. However, the post-tax credit environment has temporarily boosted Tesla's relative position.

### 3.3 Competitive Analysis: Tesla, BYD, Volkswagen ID Series, and Ford Mustang Mach-E

#### Tesla

**Positioning:** Tesla remains the dominant force in the U.S. EV market, holding roughly half of all sales. The company's strengths include the Supercharger network (37,736 ports, 51% DCFC market share), over-the-air software updates, Full Self-Driving capability, and massive volume advantage that allows for cost leadership [2][26].

**Product Lineup:** The aging Model Y and Model 3 continue to dominate, accounting for over 35% of all EV sales. The Cybertruck, launched in late 2023, has been a modest success with approximately 29,000 sales in 2025 [22]. Tesla has no new models since the Cybertruck, and the company's product lineup is increasingly facing competition from newer entrants.

**Pricing Strategy:** Tesla's average transaction price fluctuated significantly, reaching $53,107 in June 2026 (down 2.1% year-over-year) [34]. The company engaged in aggressive pricing wars in 2024-2025, affecting depreciation and resale values. Tesla's net income dropped below $500 million in Q1 2025 for the first time since 2021 [41].

**Challenges:** An aging product lineup, CEO Elon Musk controversies (estimated $60 billion in lost sales from brand damage), declining California sales (from 238,000 in 2023 to 171,000 in 2025), and increasing competition from legacy automakers and new entrants [32].

#### BYD (Chinese Market Leader, Limited U.S. Presence)

**Global Position:** BYD surpassed Tesla as the world's largest EV manufacturer in 2025, selling 2.26 million cars globally compared to Tesla's 1.64 million [8]. BYD sold 4.6 million vehicles total in 2025 (including plug-in hybrids) [8].

**U.S. Market Barriers:** Chinese EVs face severe barriers to entry in the U.S. market, including an effective tariff of 102.5% to 125% (baseline 2.5% plus 25% Section 301 tariff, increased to 100% by the Biden administration in 2024) [49][50]. Additional restrictions include the Imported Vehicle Safety Compliance Act and new restrictions on Chinese connected vehicle software/hardware [49].

**Pricing Advantage:** BYD's cheapest EV is $14,000, compared to the cheapest U.S. EV at $33,600 [61]. Chinese models reach market 2-3 years faster (20 months vs. 40 months for Western legacy makers) [61].

**Potential Entry Paths:** BYD is scouting locations in Mexico near the U.S. border to build a factory [63]. A Senate bill to permanently ban Chinese automakers has been introduced, while House Democrats have urged President Trump to block Chinese automakers from the U.S. market [50][58]. BYD's U.S. spinoff RIDE operates a California factory producing electric buses/trucks with over 70% U.S. content [49].

**Consumer Interest:** 38% of Americans would consider buying a Chinese EV, rising to 69% among Gen Z [50][56].

#### Volkswagen ID Series

**U.S. Performance:** The Volkswagen ID.4 sold 22,373 units in 2025, a 31% year-over-year increase, ranking 10th among best-selling EVs [22]. The ID. Buzz electric van was launched in 2025 [19]. Volkswagen Group held 5.6% of the U.S. EV market in 2025, with 72,000 sales [1].

**Pricing Philosophy:** The ID.4 starts at $37,495, positioning it in the affordable mid-range segment [20]. Volkswagen has focused on value-oriented pricing, though the brand has struggled to achieve the same market penetration as in Europe.

**Global Strategy:** Volkswagen is building a PowerCo battery factory in St. Thomas, Canada, to support North American EV production [22]. The company has set EV sales targets of at least 50% by 2030 [7].

#### Ford Mustang Mach-E

**Market Position:** The Mustang Mach-E sold 51,620 units in 2025, a slight dip from 2024 [22]. It ranked 4th among best-selling EVs. Ford Motor Co. held 6.6% of the U.S. EV market in 2025 [1].

**Pricing and Strategy:** The Mach-E starts at approximately $43,000, competing directly with the Tesla Model Y. Ford has announced plans to introduce a $30,000 electric pickup [5]. However, Ford canceled its $2.8 billion investment plan to produce electric trucks at BlueOval City in Tennessee, shifting the site to gas-powered trucks, and abandoned large EVs after a $20 billion write-down [5][26].

**Product Lineup:** The F-150 Lightning sold 27,307 units in 2025, down 19% year-over-year [22]. Ford is converting a Kentucky EV battery plant into a $2 billion battery energy storage system facility (20 GWh/year, target 2027) [16].

**Adaptation Strategy:** Ford CEO Jim Farley stated he "wouldn't be surprised" if EV sales fell from around 10-12% in September to 5% after the incentive program ended [40]. Ford is in talks with Geely for potential partnerships to access Chinese technology [61].

## 4. Battery Technology and Supply Chain

### 4.1 Battery Chemistry Trends

**Global Shift to LFP:** LFP (lithium iron phosphate) batteries now account for over 55% of EV batteries deployed globally, up from nearly 50% in 2024 [8]. LFP overtook nickel-based chemistries (NMC, NCA) in global EV deployments for the first time in 2025 [31].

**U.S. Market Lag:** LFP remains under 10% of the U.S. market, constrained by tariffs and Inflation Reduction Act sourcing rules [30][31]. North America was the only region where LFP deployments declined in 2025 [31]. A rebound is expected as lower-cost EVs arrive and grid-scale storage applications grow [31].

**Chemistry Characteristics:** LFP offers longer cycle life (3,000-5,000 vs. 1,500-2,000 for NMC), lower cost ($98.5/kWh vs. $112.7/kWh for NCM), and superior thermal stability (zero thermal runaway) [9][27][34]. However, NMC offers higher energy density, which remains an advantage for longer-range and cold-weather applications [33].

**Sodium-Ion Batteries:** Sodium-ion batteries are entering the scale-up phase, with the latest generation capable of retaining about 90% of nominal capacity at temperatures as low as -40°C [8]. CATL is planning 2026 deployment for sodium-ion batteries, which could cost up to 20% less than LFP [31][43].

### 4.2 Battery Manufacturing Capacity

**U.S. Capacity:** U.S. battery manufacturing capacity has doubled since 2022, reaching over 200 GWh in 2024 [10][24]. Domestic battery cell production capacity reached approximately 140 GWh in 2026 and is projected to exceed 500 GWh by 2028 [2]. There are $63 billion worth of active and planned battery manufacturing projects across the country, with $45 billion currently under construction [16].

**Major Gigafactories:** Key facilities include Tesla's Gigafactory Nevada (Panasonic cells), multiple GM-LG Energy Solution Ultium Cells joint ventures (Ohio, Tennessee, Michigan), Ford-BlueOval SK joint ventures (Kentucky, Tennessee), and Toyota's North Carolina battery plant [13][22]. The IRA's 45X advanced manufacturing production tax credits have catalyzed over $100 billion in announced investments across the battery value chain [2].

**Geographic Distribution:** Factories are located primarily in the Southeast and Midwest, with over $15 billion in LFP investments in the Great Lakes Corridor (Michigan/Ohio) and over $20 billion in the Southeast (Georgia/Tennessee) [16].

### 4.3 Critical Mineral Supply Chain

**Dependence on China:** The U.S. relies on China for 14 of its 33 most critical minerals [5]. China controls 60% of global production and 85% of processing capacity, dominating the supply chain for lithium, cobalt, nickel, and graphite [3]. The U.S. holds less than 2% of global mineral extraction market share [3].

**Lithium:** U.S. imports 50% of its lithium [3]. Domestic projects could satisfy U.S. demand by 2035, with over 100 lithium mining and refining projects catalogued [35]. The Thacker Pass lithium project in Nevada has been delayed until 2027 due to regulatory permitting issues [5].

**Cobalt:** U.S. imports 75% of its cobalt, with most supply coming from the Democratic Republic of Congo (DRC), where Chinese-controlled companies control 72% of mines [3][8]. The DRC's export quota system caps shipments at 96,600 tonnes/year through 2027 [8].

**Graphite:** The U.S. imports 100% of its graphite, with China producing 99% of natural graphite and 91% of global processing [3][28]. The Commerce Department has proposed antidumping duties up to 721% on certain Chinese graphite imports [3].

**FEOC Rules:** The Inflation Reduction Act's "foreign entity of concern" (FEOC) rules prohibit battery components manufactured or assembled by a FEOC (starting 2024) and critical minerals extracted or processed by a FEOC (starting 2025) from qualifying for the EV tax credit [12][18]. The OBBBA extended FEOC restrictions to six additional clean energy tax credits and created two new FEOC categories: Specified Foreign Entities (SFE) and Foreign Influenced Entities (FIE) [15][16].

## 5. Regulatory and Policy Environment

### 5.1 Federal Emissions Standards

**EPA Endangerment Finding Rescinded:** On February 12, 2026, EPA Administrator Lee Zeldin signed a final rule rescinding the 2009 Greenhouse Gas Endangerment Finding and repealing all motor vehicle greenhouse gas emission standards under the Clean Air Act [EPA, Holland & Knight]. This action, described as "the single largest deregulatory action in U.S. history," eliminates EPA's statutory authority to set GHG standards for vehicles [EPA]. A lawsuit challenging the repeal was filed on February 18, 2026 by a coalition of health and environmental groups [CATF].

**EPA Tier 4 Delay:** On May 14, 2026, the EPA proposed to delay the Biden-era Tier 4 emissions standards for light- and medium-duty vehicles by two model years, extending current Tier 3 standards through MY 2027 and 2028 [BDLaw, Federal Register].

**CAFE Rollback Proposal:** On December 3, 2025, NHTSA proposed to reset Corporate Average Fuel Economy (CAFE) standards to 34.5 mpg by 2031, well below the Biden administration's 50.4 mpg target [Sidley, DLA Piper]. The proposal would also eliminate tradeable compliance credits between manufacturers starting in model year 2028 [Sidley].

**CAFE Penalties Eliminated:** The OBBBA eliminated civil penalties for CAFE noncompliance, resetting the maximum penalty to $0.00 [Sidley].

### 5.2 California Advanced Clean Cars II (ACC II)

**Requirements:** California's ACC II regulation, adopted August 25, 2022, requires 100% of new passenger car sales to be zero-emission vehicles by 2035, with a phase-in timeline of 35% ZEVs by model year 2026, 68% by 2030, and 100% by 2035 [CARB]. PHEVs with a minimum 50-mile electric range are capped at 20% of total sales [CARB].

**Adopting States:** 12 states and Washington D.C. have adopted ACC II regulations: California, Oregon, Washington, New York, Massachusetts, Vermont, Rhode Island, Connecticut, Maine, New Jersey, Colorado, and Delaware [Atlas EV Hub, NCEL, ICCT]. Massachusetts announced in May 2025 that it will not enforce ACC II ZEV sales requirements for MY 2026 and 2027 [NCEL].

**Legal Status:** The Supreme Court ruled in June 2025 in *Diamond Alternative Energy LLC v. EPA* that fuel producers have standing to challenge EPA's approval of California's vehicle emissions standards, but the ruling did not address the merits of California's standards [EDF]. California's vehicle standards remain in full effect.

### 5.3 Tariffs on Chinese EVs

**Current Tariff Structure:** Chinese EVs face an effective tariff of 102.5% to 125%, consisting of a baseline 2.5% tariff, 25% Section 301 tariff (from 2018), increased to 100% by the Biden administration in 2024 [49][50]. Additional restrictions include the Imported Vehicle Safety Compliance Act and new restrictions on Chinese connected vehicle software/hardware [49].

**Impact:** These tariffs have effectively blocked Chinese EVs from the U.S. market, though Chinese automakers are entering via Mexico (where they hold 25% of sales and 89% of EV market share) and Canada [50][61]. The Alliance for Automotive Innovation reported that entry of Chinese state-backed vehicles could constitute "an extinction-level event for the U.S. auto sector" [61].

## 6. Conclusion

The U.S. electric vehicle market in mid-2026 is characterized by significant short-term turbulence and long-term uncertainty. The expiration of federal tax credits has caused a dramatic market contraction, with EV market share falling from a peak of 10.5% in Q3 2025 to 5.8% in Q2 2026. However, signs of stabilization are emerging, with Q2 2026 sales rebounding 14.2% from Q1.

The competitive landscape is shifting, with Tesla's relative position strengthening as legacy automakers struggle in the post-incentive environment. The charging infrastructure is expanding rapidly, with over 253,000 public ports and growing NACS adoption, though reliability remains a concern. Policy headwinds are substantial, including the elimination of federal incentives, the rescission of EPA emissions authority, and proposed CAFE rollbacks.

Long-term fundamentals remain supportive of EV growth: battery costs continue to decline (projected to reach $82/kWh by 2026, enabling ownership cost parity), state-level incentives are expanding (particularly California's new MyFirstEV program), and new affordable models are entering the market. However, the timeline for EV adoption has clearly shifted, and the U.S. market faces significant structural challenges, including critical mineral dependence on China, political uncertainty around federal policy, and the potential entry of low-cost Chinese EVs.
