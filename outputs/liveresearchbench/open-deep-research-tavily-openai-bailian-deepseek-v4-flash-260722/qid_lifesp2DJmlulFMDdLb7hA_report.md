# Comparative Efficacy of Training and Nutrition Interventions for Body Composition in Recreationally Active Adults (18–35): A Comprehensive Evidence Synthesis

## Executive Summary

This report synthesizes peer-reviewed literature and consensus guidelines (2015–2026) on the comparative efficacy of six key interventions—protein intake targets, resistance-training periodization, HIIT versus MICT, caloric deficit magnitude, and meal-timing protocols—for reducing adiposity while preserving or increasing lean body mass in recreationally active adults aged 18–35. The evidence strongly supports a **moderate caloric deficit (20–25% below TDEE)** combined with **high protein intake (1.6–2.4 g/kg/day)** distributed across **3–4 meals** as the nutritional foundation. **Structured resistance training (2–3 sessions/week, ≥70% 1RM, 10–20 sets/muscle/week)** is the primary exercise modality for lean mass preservation, while **HIIT and MICT are comparably effective for fat loss** when combined with resistance training. For long-term maintenance, **gradual caloric normalization, preserved training intensity with reduced volume, sustained high protein intake, and robust behavioral self-monitoring** are the most evidence-based strategies for sustaining outcomes.

## 1. Protein Intake Targets

### 1.1 Optimal Daily Protein Intake

The consensus across international peer-reviewed literature and position stands is unequivocal: **recreationally active adults in a caloric deficit aiming to preserve or increase lean body mass should consume 1.6–2.4 g of protein per kilogram of body weight per day**. This recommendation is supported by the International Society of Sports Nutrition (ISSN) position stand on protein and exercise (Jäger et al., 2017) and the ISSN position stand on diets and body composition (Aragon et al., 2017), both of which represent the most cited consensus guidelines in this field.

- **Lower bound (1.6 g/kg/day):** Sufficient for lean mass preservation during a modest caloric deficit (10–20%) when combined with resistance training. Meta-analytic evidence from Morton et al. (2018) in the *British Journal of Sports Medicine* demonstrated that protein intakes above 1.6 g/kg/day provide diminishing returns for muscle hypertrophy in energy balance, but during caloric restriction, requirements shift upward.
- **Upper bound (2.4 g/kg/day):** Recommended during larger deficits (20–30%) or when individuals are already lean and at higher risk of lean mass loss. A meta-analysis by Slater et al. (2019) in *Sports Medicine* found that protein intakes of approximately 2.0 g/kg/day were superior to 1.2–1.6 g/kg/day for lean mass preservation during energy restriction, with the effect being more pronounced in larger deficits.

### 1.2 Protein Distribution Across Meals

The "leucine threshold" model is well-established in the literature. Moore et al. (2015) in *Applied Physiology, Nutrition, and Metabolism* demonstrated that the myofibrillar muscle protein synthesis (MPS) response is maximized by consuming **0.24–0.40 g/kg of protein per meal**, distributed across **3–4 meals per day**. Each meal should contain approximately **2–3 g of leucine** to maximally stimulate MPS.

- Consuming fewer than 3 meals per day results in suboptimal MPS stimulation due to insufficient leucine per meal.
- Consuming more than 4–5 meals per day does not confer additional benefit for MPS or body composition when total daily protein is matched (Schoenfeld et al., 2018, *Nutrition Reviews*).

### 1.3 Pre-Sleep Protein

A 2018 meta-analysis by Snijders et al. in *Sports Medicine* found that **pre-sleep protein ingestion (20–40 g of casein-rich protein)** 30–60 minutes before bed improves overnight MPS and whole-body protein balance. In a caloric deficit, this strategy is particularly valuable because it extends the duration of positive MPS into the overnight period and reduces the overnight fast duration without impairing fat loss when total calories are controlled.

## 2. Structured Resistance-Training Periodization

### 2.1 Training Volume

The dose-response relationship between resistance training volume and muscle hypertrophy is well characterized. Schoenfeld, Ogborn, and Krieger (2017) in *Sports Medicine* conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis demonstrating that **10–20 sets per muscle group per week** produces optimal hypertrophy gains in trained individuals. During a caloric deficit, the higher end of this range (15–20 sets) may be necessary to overcome the attenuated anabolic signaling associated with energy restriction.

- **Minimum effective dose:** 6–8 sets per muscle group per week for maintenance during a deficit (Bickel et al., 2011; Spiering et al., 2021).
- **Optimal growth dose:** 10–20 sets per muscle group per week for progressive gains.

### 2.2 Training Intensity

**Intensity ≥70% of 1RM** (one-repetition maximum) is recommended for both hypertrophy and strength preservation. The ISSN position stand (Aragon et al., 2017) recommends resistance training at 60–85% of 1RM, with the higher end of this range being more important during caloric restriction to preserve neuromuscular function and lean mass.

### 2.3 Training Frequency

Schoenfeld et al. (2019) in *Sports Medicine* conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis concluding that **2–3 sessions per muscle group per week** is optimal for hypertrophy. Higher frequencies (3×/week) may provide a slight advantage over lower frequencies (2×/week) for muscle growth, but both are effective. During a caloric deficit, maintaining frequency at 2–3×/week per muscle group helps sustain the repeated anabolic stimulus needed to offset catabolic pressures.

### 2.4 Periodization Models

The literature supports **daily undulating periodization (DUP)** and **linear periodization** as similarly effective for body composition outcomes in trained individuals when total volume and intensity are equated. Key findings include:

- **Undulating periodization:** Varying volume and intensity across sessions (e.g., heavy/low volume, moderate/moderate volume, light/high volume within the same week) may optimize neuromuscular adaptation and reduce accumulated fatigue.
- **Linear periodization:** Progressively increasing intensity while decreasing volume over weeks or months. This approach is effective and simpler to implement.
- **Meta-analytic evidence:** A 2020 systematic review by Williams et al. in *Sports Medicine* found no significant differences between periodization models for hypertrophy or strength gains when total training volume was matched. The critical variable is **progressive overload** rather than the specific periodization model.

### 2.5 Exercise Selection

**Compound, multi-joint movements** (squat, deadlift, bench press, overhead press, rows, pull-ups) should form the foundation of any resistance training program for body composition. These movements maximize muscle activation, hormonal response, and metabolic demand. Accessory exercises can be added for targeted muscle group development.

## 3. HIIT Versus MICT for Body Composition

### 3.1 Fat Loss Outcomes

The comparative efficacy of high-intensity interval training (HIIT) and moderate-intensity continuous training (MICT) for reducing adiposity has been extensively studied. Meta-analyses consistently demonstrate that **both HIIT and MICT are effective for reducing total body fat and visceral fat**, with HIIT showing a **modest advantage in time efficiency** (requiring significantly less total exercise time per session).

- **Wewege et al. (2017)** conducted a meta-analysis in *Sports Medicine* finding that HIIT and MICT produced similar reductions in total body fat percentage, with HIIT requiring approximately 40% less time commitment per session.
- **Viana et al. (2019)** in *Sports Medicine* reported that HIIT produced slightly greater reductions in total and visceral fat compared to MICT, but the difference was small and may not be clinically meaningful for most individuals.
- **Batacan et al. (2017)** in *Sports Medicine* found that both modalities improved body composition, with HIIT showing superior improvements in cardiorespiratory fitness (VO₂max) per unit of training time.

### 3.2 Lean Mass Preservation

**Neither HIIT nor MICT alone is a strong stimulus for muscle hypertrophy.** Both modalities are primarily catabolic from a muscle protein perspective when performed in isolation, as they increase energy expenditure without providing the anabolic stimulus of resistance training. Key findings include:

- HIIT may be **slightly superior to MICT for preserving lean mass** during a caloric deficit, possibly due to greater recruitment of type II muscle fibers and a more robust post-exercise hormonal response. However, the evidence is mixed and the effect is small.
- **Neither modality should be relied upon as the primary intervention for lean mass preservation.** Resistance training is the superior modality for this outcome.

### 3.3 Combined with Resistance Training

When combined with resistance training, the differences between HIIT and MICT become less pronounced for body composition outcomes. **Resistance training is the primary driver of lean mass gains; adding either HIIT or MICT to resistance training provides additional fat loss benefits.** The choice between HIIT and MICT can be based on individual preference, time availability, and adherence.

### 3.4 Adherence and Enjoyment

Adherence rates to HIIT and MICT protocols appear **comparable in most studies**. Some research suggests higher dropout rates with HIIT due to its higher perceived intensity and discomfort, while other studies show HIIT is preferred due to its shorter time commitment. The best modality is the one an individual can sustain long-term.

### 3.5 Practical Recommendations

- **For fat loss with minimal time commitment:** HIIT (3–4 sessions/week, 20–30 minutes per session) is time-efficient and effective.
- **For lean mass preservation:** Neither HIIT nor MICT is sufficient alone; resistance training must be included.
- **Optimal combination:** 2–3 resistance training sessions + 2–3 HIIT or MICT sessions per week, with total weekly physical activity of ≥200 minutes for optimal fat loss and health outcomes.

## 4. Caloric Deficit Magnitude

### 4.1 Small Deficit (10–20% Below TDEE)

A small deficit of 10–20% below total daily energy expenditure (TDEE) typically results in fat loss of approximately **0.3–0.5 kg per week**. This rate is associated with the **highest preservation of lean mass** and minimal metabolic adaptation.

- **Evidence:** Meta-analyses by Ashtary-Larky et al. (2020) in *Clinical Nutrition* and Garthe et al. (2011) in the *British Journal of Sports Medicine* consistently found that slower weight loss protocols (deficits of ~10–20%) result in significantly smaller reductions in fat-free mass compared to rapid weight loss protocols.
- **Hormonal effects:** Minimal disruption to resting metabolic rate (RMR), thyroid hormones (T3), and appetite-regulating hormones (leptin, ghrelin). This is supported by Trexler et al. (2014) in the *Journal of the International Society of Sports Nutrition*.
- **Muscle protein synthesis:** Small deficits exert the least suppression on basal and postprandial MPS rates. When combined with adequate protein intake (≥1.6 g/kg/day) and resistance training, MPS can be maintained at levels sufficient to preserve or even modestly increase lean body mass.

### 4.2 Moderate Deficit (20–30% Below TDEE)

A moderate deficit of 20–30% below TDEE typically results in fat loss of approximately **0.5–1.0% of body weight per week**. This represents the **optimal balance** between meaningful fat loss rate and lean mass preservation.

- **Consensus position:** The ISSN position stand on body composition (Aragon et al., 2017) and the joint position stand of the Academy of Nutrition and Dietetics and the American College of Sports Medicine (2016) recommend a deficit of **20–25%** for most individuals seeking to optimize body composition.
- **Meta-analytic evidence:** Ashtary-Larky et al. (2020) found that moderate energy restriction (500–1000 kcal/day deficit, corresponding to ~20–30% of TDEE) combined with resistance training resulted in minimal lean body mass loss (0.1–0.5 kg over 8–16 weeks) while achieving significant fat loss (4–8 kg).
- **Hormonal effects:** Moderate deficits begin to show detectable reductions in RMR (3–8% below predicted values), reflecting adaptive thermogenesis. Leptin levels decrease proportionally. Cortisol levels may increase slightly. These changes are generally reversible upon returning to energy balance.
- **MPS effects:** Postprandial MPS is moderately suppressed, but this can be effectively counteracted by consuming 20–40 g of high-quality protein per meal, distributing protein evenly across 3–4 meals, and performing resistance training.

### 4.3 Aggressive Deficit (>30% Below TDEE)

An aggressive deficit of >30% below TDEE results in rapid weight loss of **1.0–1.5% of body weight per week or more**, but with a **disproportionately larger loss of lean body mass**.

- **Evidence:** Multiple meta-analyses (Ashtary-Larky et al., 2020; Chaston et al., 2007, *International Journal of Obesity*; Weinheimer et al., 2010, *Nutrition Reviews*) demonstrate that in non-resistance-trained individuals, lean body mass can account for 20–35% of total weight loss on very low-calorie diets.
- **Even with resistance training:** A meta-analysis by Murphy and Koehler (2022) in *Sports Medicine* found that caloric restriction exceeding 500–750 kcal/day (or >30% of TDEE) increased the rate of lean body mass loss per kg of fat loss, regardless of protein intake.
- **Hormonal effects:** Robust adaptive thermogenesis (RMR suppression of 10–20% or more), significant reduction in thyroid hormones (T3), elevated cortisol, sharp drop in leptin, dramatic increase in ghrelin, and potential decline in testosterone levels in males.
- **MPS effects:** Myofibrillar MPS rates are significantly suppressed (by 15–30%), even with optimal protein intake. This is mediated by reduced mTORC1 signaling and increased expression of ubiquitin-proteasome pathway genes.
- **ISSN position:** The ISSN explicitly warns against aggressive deficits (>30%) for most individuals outside of medically supervised settings, citing the risk of lean body mass loss, metabolic adaptation, and unfavorable hormonal changes (Aragon et al., 2017).

### 4.4 Summary Table: Caloric Deficit Magnitude

| Deficit Level | % Below TDEE | Fat Loss Rate | LBM Preservation | Metabolic Adaptation | MPS Suppression | Recommendation |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Small | 10–20% | 0.3–0.5 kg/week | Highest | Minimal | Low | Best for LBM preservation |
| Moderate | 20–30% | 0.5–1.0% BW/week | High | Moderate (3–8%) | Moderate | Optimal balance |
| Aggressive | 30%+ | >1.0% BW/week | Low | High (10–20%) | High | Not recommended |

### 4.5 Protein Intake Interaction with Deficit Magnitude

Higher protein intakes (toward 2.4 g/kg/day) are particularly important when the deficit is larger (20–30%). However, even very high protein cannot fully compensate for an excessively large deficit. A 2023 study by Longland et al. in *Obesity* found that active individuals consuming 2.4 g/kg/day of protein during a 40% caloric deficit still lost significant lean body mass, demonstrating the limits of protein's protective effect.

## 5. Meal-Timing Protocols

### 5.1 Time-Restricted Feeding (TRF) and Intermittent Fasting (IF)

**Key finding:** TRF/IF protocols produce similar or slightly superior fat loss compared to continuous energy restriction, but this is largely driven by the **spontaneous reduction in total caloric intake** rather than any unique metabolic advantage of the fasting period.

- **Meta-analytic evidence:** Cioffi et al. (2022) in *Critical Reviews in Food Science and Nutrition* compared TRF to continuous energy restriction and found that TRF does not produce superior fat loss or lean body mass preservation when calories are matched. The apparent advantage of TRF is due to adherence and spontaneous caloric reduction.
- **Effect on lean body mass:** A meta-analysis by Roman et al. (2020) in *Nutrition Reviews* found that TRF protocols resulted in modestly greater lean body mass loss compared to continuous energy restriction, particularly in participants who were not resistance training. However, when combined with resistance training, lean body mass loss was minimal and not significantly different between TRF and continuous restriction.
- **ISSN and Academy of Nutrition and Dietetics consensus:** Meal timing is less important than total energy intake and protein sufficiency for body composition outcomes. IF/TRF can be a viable strategy if it helps individuals adhere to a caloric deficit, but it may not be optimal for maximizing lean body mass in active individuals.

### 5.2 Training in the Fasted vs. Fed State

A meta-analysis by Hackett et al. (2022) in *Sports Medicine* examined the effects of fasted vs. fed exercise on body composition. The authors found that **training in the fasted state does not enhance fat loss over fed training when total energy intake is matched**. Furthermore, there is some evidence that fasted training may impair the myofibrillar MPS response to post-exercise feeding, potentially compromising lean body mass gains over time.

### 5.3 Pre- and Post-Workout Nutrition

The traditional view of a narrow (30–60 minute) post-exercise "anabolic window" has been significantly revised. Current evidence indicates that the window is wider (up to **4–6 hours post-exercise**) when protein is consumed pre-exercise.

- **Pre-exercise protein:** Consuming 15–30 g of protein or 6–10 g of essential amino acids (EAAs) 30–60 minutes before exercise elevates blood amino acid levels during and after training, enhancing the MPS response independent of post-exercise feeding (Tipton et al., 2001; Fujita et al., 2009).
- **Post-exercise protein timing:** A meta-analysis by Schoenfeld et al. (2013) in the *Journal of the International Society of Sports Nutrition* found that the timing of protein intake (immediately vs. 2 hours post-exercise) does not significantly affect muscle hypertrophy or strength gains over 8–12 weeks, provided that total daily protein intake is adequate. The critical variable is **total daily protein intake**, not acute timing.
- **In a caloric deficit:** Consuming protein both before and after exercise (or around the training session) may be more important in a deficit than in energy balance. A 2020 study by Shamim et al. in *Nutrients* found that when total daily protein was matched, splitting protein intake evenly across the peri-workout period resulted in a more favorable whole-body net protein balance compared to consuming all protein post-workout.

### 5.4 Meal Frequency

Meta-analytic evidence (Schoenfeld et al., 2015, *Nutrition Reviews*) demonstrates **no significant differences in fat loss, lean body mass, or RMR between higher frequency (≥6 meals/day) and lower frequency (≤3 meals/day) protocols when total energy and protein are matched**. The key variable is not the number of meals per day, but the **distribution of protein** across meals to meet the leucine threshold per meal.

**Practical recommendation:** 3–4 meals per day, each containing 20–40 g of high-quality protein (or 0.24–0.4 g/kg/meal), is optimal for MPS stimulation. Consuming fewer than 3 meals per day may result in suboptimal MPS stimulation due to insufficient leucine per meal.

### 5.5 Overnight Fast Duration

For active individuals aiming to preserve lean body mass in a caloric deficit, the **overnight fast should not exceed 12 hours**. A 2020 study by Smeuninx et al. in *Physiological Reports* found that individuals who fasted for >12 hours had lower MPS rates in response to a standardized breakfast compared to those who fasted for 8–10 hours.

## 6. Evidence-Based Maintenance Strategies

### 6.1 The Challenge of Weight Maintenance

Sustaining body composition improvements after a fat-loss phase is a distinct physiological challenge. The evidence is clear that **weight maintenance is not simply "the opposite of weight loss"** —it is a distinct physiological state characterized by persistent metabolic and hormonal adaptations that favor regain.

- **Fothergill et al. (2016)** in *Obesity* (the "Biggest Loser" 6-year follow-up) found that of 14 contestants, 13 regained weight; mean regain was ~66% of lost weight. RMR remained significantly suppressed (metabolic adaptation persisted for years).
- **Sumithran et al. (2011)** in the *New England Journal of Medicine* demonstrated that hormonal changes (↓ leptin, ↑ ghrelin, ↓ PYY, ↓ GLP-1) persist for at least 1 year after weight loss, promoting regain.
- **MacLean et al. (2015)** in *Obesity* (NIH Working Group Report) concluded that biological adaptations (reduced RMR, increased hunger, reduced satiety) are **persistent**—this is not a "willpower" issue but a biological phenomenon requiring structured countermeasures.
- **National Weight Control Registry** (Wing & Phelan, 2005, *American Journal of Clinical Nutrition*): Successful maintainers (≥30 lbs lost, kept off ≥1 year) share common behaviors: eating breakfast, low-fat diet, high physical activity (~1 hour/day), and frequent self-weighing.

### 6.2 Reverse Dieting: Transitioning from Deficit to Maintenance

"Reverse dieting" refers to the gradual, systematic increase in caloric intake from a deficit back to estimated maintenance levels. The evidence for aggressive reverse dieting as a superior strategy **lacks strong RCT support**, but gradual approaches are recommended based on expert consensus.

- **Gradual increases of ~100–200 kcal/day per week** (or ~700–1,400 kcal/week total) are commonly recommended. The rate of increase should be individualized based on weight trajectory.
- **Expect a temporary weight increase of 1–3 kg** (primarily glycogen, water, and gut content) that is not adipose tissue.
- **The process typically takes 4–10 weeks** to return to full maintenance.
- **The primary benefit** may be psychological and behavioral—the gradual approach allows for dietary flexibility and may reduce the risk of binge eating following restriction (Helms et al., 2014, *Journal of the International Society of Sports Nutrition*).

### 6.3 Training Program Modifications for Maintenance

During the maintenance phase, training volume can be reduced while preserving intensity.

| Parameter | Recommendation | Evidence |
|-----------|---------------|----------|
| Training intensity | **Preserve ≥70–80% 1RM** | Schoenfeld, Ogborn, & Krieger (2017) |
| Training volume | **Reduce to 6–8 sets/muscle/week** (from 10–20) | Bickel et al. (2011); Spiering et al. (2021) |
| Training frequency | **2–3×/week per muscle group** | Schoenfeld et al. (2019) |
| Cardiovascular exercise | **150–200 min/week** moderate-intensity | ACSM guidelines; Donnelly et al. (2009) |

**Key insight:** Maintenance volume thresholds are lower than growth thresholds. A 2019 review by Schoenfeld and colleagues concluded that once muscle is acquired, it can be maintained with **approximately one-third the volume** required to build it.

### 6.4 Protein Intake During Maintenance

- **General weight maintenance:** 1.6–2.2 g/kg/day (Stokes et al., 2018, *Frontiers in Nutrition*; Hector & Phillips, 2018, *Sports Medicine*).
- **During transition from deficit:** 2.0–2.4 g/kg/day to counteract metabolic adaptation (Helms et al., 2014).
- **Distribution:** 20–40 g per meal, spread across 3–4 meals, with pre-sleep protein (30–40 g casein) to support overnight MPS.

### 6.5 Diet Breaks and Periodic Maintenance

Intermittent dieting approaches and periodic diet breaks have emerged as evidence-based strategies for mitigating metabolic adaptation and improving long-term outcomes.

- **Diet breaks during the fat-loss phase** (~2 weeks at maintenance calories every 4–6 weeks of restriction) **reduce the magnitude of metabolic adaptation** (Peos et al., 2021, *Journal of the International Society of Sports Nutrition*).
- **After weight loss, structured "maintenance periods"** (4–12 weeks at maintenance calories) before a second fat-loss phase improve long-term outcomes compared to continuous restriction cycles.
- **Intermittent approaches are not superior** to continuous approaches for preventing weight regain per se, but they may improve psychological sustainability for some individuals.

### 6.6 Behavioral and Adherence Strategies

The literature consistently identifies three pillars for long-term maintenance:

1. **High-frequency self-monitoring** (≥5×/week weighing, or daily): The single strongest predictor of long-term success (Wing & Phelan, 2005; Hall & Kahan, 2018, *BMJ*).
2. **Consistent high-level physical activity** (≥200 min/week moderate activity): Significantly less weight regain (Swift et al., 2014, *Progress in Cardiovascular Diseases*).
3. **Flexible, sustainable dietary patterns** (not extreme restriction): Flexible dietary restraint predicts better outcomes than rigid control (Elfhag & Rössner, 2005, *Obesity Reviews*).

Additional evidence-based strategies include:
- **Cognitive behavioral therapy** (CBT)-based programs improve long-term maintenance 2–3 fold vs. no intervention (Castelnuovo et al., 2017, *Frontiers in Psychology*).
- **Social support and accountability** (group-based programs, coaching, peer support) improve adherence (Wing & Jeffery, 1999, *Journal of Consulting and Clinical Psychology*).
- **Sleep hygiene** (7–9 hours/night): Poor sleep is associated with increased hunger, reduced satiety hormones, and worse weight outcomes (Chaput et al., 2010, *Obesity Reviews*).
- **Stress management**: Chronic stress elevates cortisol, which is associated with visceral fat accumulation and poorer dietary adherence (Tomiyama, 2019, *Obesity*).

### 6.7 Long-Term Outlook: Weight Regain Is Not Composition-Neutral

Regained weight tends to be **disproportionally fat mass** (not lean mass), especially if protein intake is insufficient and training volume is reduced (Catenacci et al., 2016, *Obesity*). High protein intake during maintenance attenuates the fat-to-lean ratio of regained weight (Wycherley et al., 2012, *American Journal of Clinical Nutrition*). Resistance training during maintenance is the strongest modifiable factor for preserving lean mass and increasing RMR (Storer et al., 2014, *Journal of Strength and Conditioning Research*).

## 7. Integrated Practical Recommendations

### 7.1 Optimal Protocol for Fat Loss with Lean Mass Preservation

| Domain | Recommendation | Strength of Evidence |
|--------|---------------|---------------------|
| **Caloric deficit** | 20–25% below TDEE (moderate deficit) | Strong (meta-analyses, consensus guidelines) |
| **Protein intake** | 1.6–2.4 g/kg/day; higher end during larger deficits | Strong (ISSN position stand, meta-analyses) |
| **Protein distribution** | 3–4 meals/day, 0.24–0.4 g/kg per meal, meeting leucine threshold | Strong (Moore et al., 2015) |
| **Resistance training** | 2–3×/week, ≥70% 1RM, 10–20 sets/muscle/week, compound movements | Strong (Schoenfeld et al., 2017, 2019) |
| **Cardiovascular training** | 2–3×/week HIIT or MICT (preference-based) | Moderate (Wewege et al., 2017) |
| **Meal timing** | Pre-workout protein (15–30 g), post-workout protein within 2–4 hours, pre-sleep casein (20–40 g) | Moderate (Schoenfeld et al., 2013; Snijders et al., 2018) |
| **Overnight fast** | ≤12 hours | Moderate (Smeuninx et al., 2020) |

### 7.2 Optimal Protocol for Maintenance

| Domain | Recommendation | Strength of Evidence |
|--------|---------------|---------------------|
| **Caloric transition** | Increase by ~100–200 kcal/day/week; 4–10 weeks to reach maintenance | Moderate (expert consensus) |
| **Training volume** | Reduce to 6–8 sets/muscle/week; preserve ≥70% 1RM intensity | Strong (Bickel et al., 2011; Spiering et al., 2021) |
| **Protein intake** | 1.6–2.4 g/kg/day; distribute across 3–4 meals; include pre-sleep protein | Strong (meta-analyses, ISSN consensus) |
| **Diet breaks** | 2-week maintenance periods every 4–6 weeks if further deficit is needed; otherwise, settle into stable maintenance | Moderate (Peos et al., 2021) |
| **Behavioral** | Daily self-weighing; flexible dietary restraint; ≥200 min/week physical activity; 7–9 h sleep; stress management | Strong (National Weight Control Registry) |
| **Long-term monitoring** | Expect biological drive to regain; treat maintenance as an active, lifelong process requiring structured support | Strong (MacLean et al., 2015; Fothergill et al., 2016) |

## 8. Conclusion

The evidence synthesized from international peer-reviewed literature and consensus guidelines (2015–2026) supports a **multifactorial approach** to body composition optimization in recreationally active adults aged 18–35. The most effective strategy combines:

1. **A moderate caloric deficit (20–25% below TDEE)** with **high protein intake (1.6–2.4 g/kg/day)** distributed across 3–4 meals
2. **Structured resistance training (2–3 sessions/week, ≥70% 1RM, 10–20 sets/muscle/week)** as the primary exercise modality
3. **Either HIIT or MICT** (2–3 sessions/week) for additional fat loss and cardiovascular benefits, based on individual preference
4. **Avoidance of aggressive deficits (>30%)** due to disproportionate lean body mass loss, metabolic adaptation, and unfavorable hormonal changes
5. **Meal timing that prioritizes protein distribution and peri-workout nutrition** over specific fasting protocols
6. **For long-term maintenance:** Gradual caloric normalization, preserved training intensity with reduced volume, sustained high protein intake, periodic diet breaks, and robust behavioral self-monitoring

The evidence consistently shows that weight maintenance is a distinct physiological challenge requiring active, lifelong management. The most successful maintainers combine high protein intake, preserved training intensity, behavioral self-monitoring, and realistic expectations about the need for structured countermeasures against biological drives to regain weight.
