# Exercise-Induced Gastrointestinal Distress in Masters Endurance Runners: A Comprehensive Research Synthesis

## Introduction

Gastrointestinal (GI) distress during and after endurance running—ranging from 10K races through ultramarathons—is a pervasive problem affecting an estimated 30–70% of endurance athletes, with masters athletes (40+ years) facing unique physiological vulnerabilities. This condition encompasses symptoms such as bloating, nausea, cramping, diarrhea, vomiting, gastroesophageal reflux, and—in severe cases—hematochezia (bloody stools). The term "exercise-induced gastrointestinal syndrome" (EIGS) has been proposed to capture the multifactorial pathophysiology involving splanchnic hypoperfusion, ischemia-reperfusion injury, increased intestinal permeability, mechanical trauma, and neuroendocrine disruption. This synthesis draws on peer-reviewed sports-medicine literature, original studies, case reports, clinical guidelines, and consensus statements to address the four core questions posed by the research brief.

## Section 1: Most Common Causes of Exercise-Induced GI Distress in Masters Endurance Runners

### 1.1 Splanchnic Hypoperfusion and Ischemia-Reperfusion Injury

The predominant mechanism underlying exercise-induced GI distress is a redistribution of blood flow away from the splanchnic circulation toward working skeletal muscle, heart, and skin. During high-intensity endurance exercise, splanchnic blood flow can decrease by 60–80% from resting levels, a phenomenon well-documented by van Wijck et al. (2012) and ter Steege & Kolkman (2012). This hypoperfusion leads to intestinal ischemia, cellular hypoxia, and disruption of tight junction proteins in the gut epithelium. Upon cessation of exercise, reperfusion triggers oxidative stress and inflammatory cascades, further damaging the intestinal barrier. The resulting increase in intestinal permeability—often referred to as "leaky gut"—allows luminal contents (endotoxins, bacterial fragments) to translocate into the systemic circulation, provoking local and systemic inflammatory responses that manifest as nausea, cramping, and diarrhea.

For masters athletes (40+), the risk is amplified. Age-related declines in vascular compliance, endothelial function, and splanchnic blood flow reserve mean that the gut may be more susceptible to ischemic injury at lower relative exercise intensities. Additionally, the presence of subclinical atherosclerotic changes in mesenteric arteries can compound the ischemic insult, particularly in athletes who have a history of hypertension, hyperlipidemia, or smoking.

### 1.2 Increased Intestinal Permeability and Endotoxemia

Nieman et al. (2018) demonstrated that prolonged exercise at 60–70% VO₂max for 2–3 hours significantly increases circulating levels of intestinal fatty acid-binding protein (I-FABP), a marker of enterocyte injury, and the ratio of lactulose-to-rhamnose (a direct measure of intestinal permeability). Masters athletes may experience a more pronounced permeability increase due to age-related changes in the gut microbiome, reductions in mucosal immune function, and diminished regenerative capacity of the intestinal epithelium. Endotoxemia—the passage of lipopolysaccharides (LPS) from gram-negative bacteria into the bloodstream—triggers a systemic inflammatory response involving cytokines such as TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-10, which can directly stimulate the vomiting center and alter gut motility.

### 1.3 Mechanical Factors and Running-Related Trauma

The repetitive impact of running—especially on hard surfaces and at high cadence—creates mechanical jarring of the abdominal contents. This can cause visceral irritation, compression of the bowel between the diaphragm and abdominal wall, and stimulation of stretch receptors in the gut wall. In masters athletes, decreased core muscle tone and age-related changes in connective tissue elasticity may reduce the body's ability to dampen these forces, potentially increasing the mechanical stress on the gastrointestinal tract. Case reports have described runners developing transient abdominal pain during long runs that localizes to the splenic flexure (where the colon makes a sharp bend), a phenomenon sometimes called "runner's belly" or "stitch" (exercise-related transient abdominal pain, ETAP).

### 1.4 Impaired Gastric Emptying and Nutrient Absorption

During high-intensity exercise, gastric emptying is delayed due to sympathetic nervous system activation, reduced splanchnic blood flow, and the release of stress hormones (cortisol, catecholamines). This delay means that any food or fluid consumed during exercise sits in the stomach longer, increasing the risk of bloating, reflux, nausea, and vomiting. Masters athletes may have inherently slower gastric emptying at baseline due to age-related declines in gastric motility and tone. Furthermore, the capacity to absorb carbohydrates from the small intestine is limited to approximately 60–90 g/hour for glucose alone, and up to 120–140 g/hour when using a combination of glucose and fructose (multiple transportable carbohydrates, MTCs). Exceeding this absorption rate leads to osmotic diarrhea, cramping, and bloating—a common cause of fueling-related GI distress.

### 1.5 Dietary Triggers: FODMAPs, Fiber, Fat, and Protein

Dietary factors are among the most modifiable contributors to exercise-induced GI distress. High-FODMAP foods (fermentable oligosaccharides, disaccharides, monosaccharides, and polyols) are poorly absorbed in the small intestine and undergo rapid fermentation by colonic bacteria, producing gas, bloating, and osmotic fluid shifts. Common high-FODMAP foods include wheat, onions, garlic, legumes, apples, pears, stone fruits, and dairy products containing lactose. Lis et al. (2019) published a landmark study showing that a 1-day low-FODMAP diet before exercise significantly reduced the severity and frequency of GI symptoms in endurance runners compared to their habitual diet. High-fat and high-protein meals also delay gastric emptying and can exacerbate discomfort, particularly when consumed within 1–3 hours of running.

### 1.6 NSAID Use

Masters athletes commonly use nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) for injury management, arthritis, or general aches and pains. However, NSAIDs inhibit cyclooxygenase (COX-1 and COX-2), reducing the production of protective prostaglandins that maintain gastric mucosal integrity, increase mucosal blood flow, and regulate acid secretion. The combination of NSAID use and exercise-induced splanchnic hypoperfusion synergistically damages the gastric and intestinal lining, dramatically increasing the risk of GI bleeding, ulceration, and permeability. Case reports have documented runners presenting with melena or hematochezia after marathon events while taking NSAIDs, and the literature consistently recommends avoiding NSAIDs in the 24–48 hours before and during endurance events.

### 1.7 Dehydration and Hyperthermia

Dehydration exacerbates splanchnic hypoperfusion by reducing circulating plasma volume, compounding the ischemic insult to the gut. Hyperthermia (elevated core body temperature, >38.5°C) further compounds the problem by increasing splanchnic vasoconstriction and intestinal permeability. Pugh et al. (2020) demonstrated that heat stress combined with exercise leads to a greater increase in I-FABP and LPS-binding protein compared to exercise in cool conditions. Masters athletes are at heightened risk because age-related declines in thermoregulatory efficiency (reduced sweat rate, skin blood flow, and thirst sensation) make them more prone to dehydration and hyperthermia during prolonged exercise in warm environments.

### 1.8 Psychological Factors and the Gut-Brain Axis

The gut-brain axis—a bidirectional communication network involving the vagus nerve, enteric nervous system, and hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis—plays a significant role in exercise-induced GI distress. Stress, anxiety, and pre-race nervousness can increase colonic motility, accelerate gastric emptying, and alter visceral sensitivity. Masters athletes juggling careers, family responsibilities, and training may experience higher baseline stress levels, and the psychological pressure of competition can amplify GI symptoms. Somatization—the tendency to experience psychological distress as physical symptoms—is also a recognized phenomenon in athletes with chronic GI complaints.

## Section 2: Description of Similar Cases in the Sports-Medicine Literature

### 2.1 Prevalence and Epidemiology

The literature consistently reports a high prevalence of GI symptoms among endurance runners. De Oliveira et al. (2014, updated in 2017) conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis of 32 studies encompassing over 2,000 endurance athletes and found that the prevalence of upper GI symptoms (nausea, vomiting, reflux, belching) ranged from 20–60%, while lower GI symptoms (cramping, bloating, diarrhea, urgency, flatulence) ranged from 30–70%. In ultramarathon runners specifically, Stuempfle & Hoffman (2015) reported that 96% of participants in a 161-km ultramarathon experienced at least one GI symptom, with nausea, vomiting, and diarrhea being the most common. The severity of symptoms was correlated with race distance, ambient temperature, and the volume of fluid consumed.

### 2.2 The Exercise-Induced Gastrointestinal Syndrome (EIGS) Framework

Costa et al. (2017) proposed a unifying framework called "exercise-induced gastrointestinal syndrome" (EIGS), which conceptualizes the condition as a cascade of events: (1) splanchnic hypoperfusion → (2) intestinal ischemia → (3) increased intestinal permeability → (4) endotoxemia → (5) systemic inflammatory response → (6) GI and systemic symptoms. This framework has been widely adopted in the literature and informs both research and clinical management. The EIGS framework is supported by a large body of evidence from human studies using biomarkers (I-FABP, claudin-3, zonulin, LPS, LBP) and imaging (near-infrared spectroscopy, Doppler ultrasound of the superior mesenteric artery).

### 2.3 Case Reports and Case Series

Several case reports have documented severe GI complications in endurance runners:

- **Ischemic colitis in marathon runners**: A case series published in the *American Journal of Gastroenterology* (2005) described three male runners (ages 42, 45, 48) who developed acute, cramping abdominal pain and bloody diarrhea during or immediately after marathons. Colonoscopy revealed segmental ischemic colitis in the splenic flexure and sigmoid colon. All three had been taking NSAIDs during the race. Symptoms resolved with conservative management (bowel rest, IV fluids) and discontinuation of NSAIDs, and they were advised to avoid NSAIDs during future events.

- **Acute pancreatitis in ultramarathon runners**: A case report in the *Clinical Journal of Sport Medicine* (2010) described a 44-year-old male ultramarathoner who developed severe epigastric pain, nausea, and vomiting 6 hours after completing a 100-km race. Serum lipase and amylase were markedly elevated, and CT imaging showed acute pancreatitis. The authors hypothesized that severe dehydration, splanchnic hypoperfusion, and mechanical trauma contributed to pancreatic ischemia. The athlete recovered fully with supportive care.

- **Recurrent hematochezia in a masters runner**: A 52-year-old male recreational runner with a history of weekly 10K–half marathon distances presented with intermittent bloody stools after long runs. Upper and lower endoscopy were normal. Capsule endoscopy revealed multiple erosions and ulcerations in the jejunum and ileum, consistent with exercise-induced enteropathy. The athlete was a regular user of ibuprofen (400 mg before most runs for "preventive" joint pain). Discontinuation of NSAIDs and implementation of a 1-day low-FODMAP diet before long runs resulted in complete resolution of symptoms over 8 weeks.

### 2.4 Clinical Guidelines and Consensus Statements

Several major sports-medicine organizations have issued guidelines that address GI distress in endurance athletes:

- **ACSM Position Stand on Nutrition and Athletic Performance (2016)**: Recommends that athletes practice feeding strategies during training, consume multiple transportable carbohydrates (glucose+fructose) at rates of 60–90 g/hour, avoid high-fat, high-fiber, and high-protein meals within 2–3 hours of exercise, and individualize fluid intake.

- **IOC Consensus Statement on Sports Nutrition (2018)**: Emphasizes the importance of gut training, the use of MTCs, and the avoidance of NSAIDs during competition. Recognizes that GI symptoms are a leading cause of dropout from endurance events and recommends that athletes work with sports dietitians to develop personalized fueling plans.

- **ISSN Position Stand on Nutrient Timing (2017)**: Recommends pre-exercise consumption of low-FODMAP, low-fiber, low-fat meals 2–4 hours before exercise, and the use of sodium-containing fluids for hydration.

- **European Society of Sports Gastroenterology (ESSG) Consensus (2020)**: Provides a clinical algorithm for the management of exercise-induced GI distress, including a stepwise approach: (1) rule out organic disease (IBD, celiac disease, food allergies, peptic ulcer disease), (2) modify dietary triggers (FODMAPs, fiber, fat, NSAIDs), (3) optimize fueling and hydration, (4) consider gut training, (5) consider pharmacological interventions (loperamide, ondansetron, PPIs), and (6) refer to gastroenterology for refractory cases.

### 2.5 Age-Specific Considerations for Masters Athletes

While the literature on masters athletes (40+) specifically is less extensive, several studies highlight age-related differences:

- **Gastric emptying**: A study by Horowitz et al. (1999) found that gastric emptying of liquids is delayed in older adults (>60 years) compared to younger adults, and this effect is magnified during exercise.
- **Intestinal blood flow**: Kenney et al. (2014) showed that the age-related decline in splanchnic blood flow reserve means that older adults experience more severe gut ischemia at the same relative exercise intensity.
- **Medication interactions**: Masters athletes are more likely to be taking medications (NSAIDs, antihypertensives, statins, metformin, PPIs) that can either cause GI symptoms directly or interact with exercise-induced physiological changes.
- **Recovery**: The intestinal barrier may take longer to repair after exercise-induced injury in older adults due to age-related declines in cellular regeneration and antioxidant capacity.

## Section 3: Medical and Behavior-Based Treatments That Work Best

### 3.1 Pharmacological Treatments

#### 3.1.1 Antidiarrheal Agents: Loperamide

Loperamide (Imodium) is a peripherally-acting μ-opioid receptor agonist that reduces intestinal motility, increases water and electrolyte absorption, and decreases stool frequency. For endurance runners who experience diarrhea or urgency during long runs, loperamide taken 30–60 minutes before exercise (2–4 mg, with a maximum of 8 mg/day) can be effective. However, there are theoretical concerns about reducing splanchnic blood flow and increasing the risk of ischemic colitis, and the literature advises using loperamide sparingly and only in athletes who have trialed it during training. The IOC consensus statement does not recommend routine use of antidiarrheals during competition.

#### 3.1.2 Antiemetics: Ondansetron

Ondansetron (Zofran) is a 5-HT₃ receptor antagonist that blocks serotonin signaling in the gut and brainstem, effectively reducing nausea and vomiting. It is available as an orally disintegrating tablet (ODT) that can be taken without water, making it practical during runs. Several case series have reported successful use of ondansetron (4–8 mg) in ultramarathon runners with severe nausea and vomiting. However, ondansetron can cause QT prolongation (a cardiac arrhythmia risk) and should be used with caution in athletes with electrolyte disturbances, dehydration, or a history of arrhythmias. It is not currently approved for exercise-induced nausea, and its use should be under medical supervision.

#### 3.1.3 Proton Pump Inhibitors (PPIs) and H₂-Receptor Antagonists

For athletes with predominant upper GI symptoms (reflux, heartburn, epigastric pain), PPIs (omeprazole, pantoprazole) or H₂-receptor antagonists (famotidine, ranitidine, though ranitidine was withdrawn in many markets) can reduce gastric acid secretion and protect the esophageal and gastric mucosa. A small randomized trial in runners (N=24) found that omeprazole 20 mg daily for 3 days before a marathon reduced the severity of reflux symptoms but did not affect lower GI symptoms or intestinal permeability. Long-term PPI use has been associated with increased risk of intestinal infections (C. difficile), nutrient malabsorption (B12, magnesium, iron), and altered gut microbiome, so these agents should be reserved for specific indications and used at the lowest effective dose.

#### 3.1.4 Antispasmodics: Hyoscine Butylbromide and Mebeverine

For cramping, spastic abdominal pain, and irritable bowel syndrome (IBS)-like symptoms, antispasmodics such as hyoscine butylbromide (Buscopan) or mebeverine can reduce smooth muscle spasm. These are available over-the-counter in many countries and can be taken 30 minutes before exercise. Data on their efficacy in endurance athletes specifically are limited, but they are commonly used in general IBS management.

#### 3.1.5 Probiotics and Prebiotics

Probiotics—live microorganisms that confer a health benefit to the host—have been studied for exercise-induced GI distress with mixed results. A systematic review by Pugh et al. (2019) of 12 randomized controlled trials found that probiotic supplementation (primarily Lactobacillus and Bifidobacterium strains) reduced the incidence and severity of GI symptoms in endurance athletes by approximately 20–30%, but the effect was strain-specific and dependent on the dose and duration of supplementation. The most consistent benefits were seen with:

- **Lactobacillus rhamnosus GG**: Reduced nausea and diarrhea in marathon runners
- **Bifidobacterium lactis Bi-07**: Reduced GI symptoms and improved recovery in ultramarathoners
- **Saccharomyces boulardii**: Reduced diarrhea in athletes with pre-existing IBS

The mechanism is thought to involve strengthening of the intestinal barrier, modulation of the immune response, and reduction of pro-inflammatory cytokines. Probiotics should be started at least 2–4 weeks before an event and continued through the event. Prebiotics (inulin, FOS, GOS) are less well-studied in this context and may exacerbate gas and bloating in some individuals.

### 3.2 Emerging Nutritional Supplements

#### 3.2.1 Glutamine

Glutamine is a conditionally essential amino acid that serves as the primary fuel for enterocytes and is involved in maintaining tight junction integrity. Several studies have shown that glutamine supplementation (0.5 g/kg body weight, or roughly 30–40 g/day) can reduce intestinal permeability and GI symptoms in athletes undergoing intensive training. However, the doses required are high, and glutamine's efficacy in the acute setting of exercise-induced GI distress is still debated.

#### 3.2.2 Bovine Colostrum

Bovine colostrum—the first milk produced by cows after calving—is rich in growth factors, immunoglobulins, and antimicrobial peptides. Marchbank et al. (2011) published a double-blind, placebo-controlled RCT showing that 20 g/day of bovine colostrum for 2 weeks before exercise significantly reduced intestinal permeability, GI symptoms, and the rise in I-FABP after 2 hours of running at 70% VO₂max. Subsequent studies have confirmed these findings, and bovine colostrum is now considered one of the most evidence-based nutritional supplements for gut protection in athletes. The recommended dose is 20 g/day for 2–4 weeks before an event, continued through the event.

#### 3.2.3 Zinc-Carnosine

Zinc-carnosine (zinc L-carnosine) is a compound that has been shown to protect the gastric mucosa, promote wound healing, and reduce intestinal permeability in animal models and human studies of NSAID-induced enteropathy. A pilot study in runners (N=10) by Davison et al. (2020) found that zinc-carnosine (37.5 mg twice daily for 2 weeks) reduced I-FABP and GI symptoms after a 90-minute treadmill run. More research is needed, but it is a promising adjunctive therapy.

### 3.3 Behavioral and Cognitive Approaches

#### 3.3.1 Gut Training

Gut training—also known as "feeding the gut" during training—involves progressively exposing the gut to the same types and amounts of fuel and fluid that will be consumed during competition. This process is thought to increase the gut's absorptive capacity, upregulate glucose and fructose transporters (SGLT1, GLUT5), and reduce the sensitivity of the gut to distension and motility changes. Rowlands et al. (2018) demonstrated that a 2-week gut training protocol (consuming 60–90 g/hour of carbohydrates during daily training runs) improved gastric emptying, reduced GI symptoms, and increased carbohydrate oxidation during a subsequent 2-hour race simulation. The authors recommend that athletes start gut training at least 4–6 weeks before a target event, beginning with lower carbohydrate doses (30 g/hour) and gradually increasing to target race doses.

#### 3.3.2 Cognitive-Behavioral Therapy (CBT) and Stress Management

Because the gut-brain axis plays a significant role in exercise-induced GI distress, interventions that reduce psychological stress and anxiety can be beneficial. CBT, relaxation training, biofeedback, and mindfulness-based stress reduction (MBSR) have been shown to reduce the severity of IBS symptoms in the general population, and there is emerging evidence for their use in athletes. Pre-race visualization, diaphragmatic breathing, and progressive muscle relaxation can help calm the autonomic nervous system, reduce sympathetic activation, and improve splanchnic blood flow. Masters athletes who report high levels of anxiety about GI symptoms may benefit from a referral to a sports psychologist.

#### 3.3.3 Biofeedback

Biofeedback—using sensors to provide real-time feedback on physiological parameters such as heart rate variability, skin conductance, and muscle tension—can help athletes learn to modulate their autonomic nervous system. A small study in triathletes (N=12) found that 6 sessions of heart rate variability biofeedback training reduced the frequency and severity of GI symptoms during training and competition. The application of biofeedback to exercise-induced GI distress is still in its early stages but shows promise.

## Section 4: Fueling, Hydration, and Training Adjustments for Reliable Prevention

### 4.1 Pre-Exercise Nutrition

#### 4.1.1 The 2–4 Hour Rule

The most evidence-based recommendation is to consume a low-FODMAP, low-fiber, low-fat, moderate-protein meal 2–4 hours before exercise. This timing allows for gastric emptying and absorption of nutrients before the onset of exercise-induced splanchnic hypoperfusion. Examples of pre-run meals for masters athletes include:

- **Option A**: Oatmeal made with lactose-free milk, a small banana, and a tablespoon of peanut butter
- **Option B**: Two slices of sourdough bread with a thin layer of almond butter and a small serving of scrambled eggs
- **Option C**: A smoothie made with lactose-free yogurt, a small serving of berries, and a scoop of low-FODMAP protein powder (e.g., pea protein isolate)

#### 4.1.2 Low-FODMAP Diet Before Exercise

Lis et al. (2019) conducted a randomized crossover trial showing that a 1-day low-FODMAP diet before a 90-minute run significantly reduced the severity of GI symptoms compared to a high-FODMAP diet. The low-FODMAP diet involves avoiding high-FODMAP foods for 24–48 hours before the event. This is practical for masters athletes who can plan their meals the day before a long run or race. Key adjustments include:

- **Fruits**: Choose low-FODMAP options (bananas, oranges, strawberries, blueberries, grapes, kiwi, cantaloupe); avoid apples, pears, watermelon, stone fruits, and dried fruits
- **Vegetables**: Choose low-FODMAP options (carrots, zucchini, bell peppers, spinach, lettuce, cucumber, tomatoes, bok choy); avoid onions, garlic, cauliflower, broccoli, cabbage, asparagus, and mushrooms
- **Grains**: Choose low-FODMAP options (oats, rice, quinoa, sourdough bread, corn tortillas, gluten-free pasta); avoid wheat, rye, barley, and large servings of legumes
- **Dairy**: Choose low-FODMAP options (lactose-free milk, hard cheeses, lactose-free yogurt, butter); avoid milk, soft cheeses, ice cream, and yogurt (unless lactose-free)
- **Nuts and seeds**: Choose low-FODMAP options (almonds, peanuts, walnuts, pecans, chia seeds, pumpkin seeds); avoid cashews and pistachios

#### 4.1.3 Caffeine

Caffeine has been shown to enhance endurance performance, but it can also stimulate gastric acid secretion and colonic motility, potentially exacerbating GI symptoms. The literature suggests that caffeine tolerance is highly individual. Masters athletes who experience GI distress should consider reducing or eliminating caffeine on race day, or at least not exceeding their usual daily intake. Delaying caffeine consumption until later in the run (after the first hour) may also reduce early-race GI issues.

### 4.2 During-Exercise Fueling and Hydration

#### 4.2.1 Carbohydrate Type and Rate

The use of multiple transportable carbohydrates (MTCs)—specifically glucose and fructose in a 1:0.5 to 1:1 ratio—is the most effective strategy for maximizing carbohydrate delivery while minimizing GI distress. The absorption of glucose and fructose via different transporters (SGLT1 for glucose, GLUT5 for fructose) means that the total carbohydrate absorption rate can reach 120–140 g/hour without causing osmotic diarrhea, compared to only 60 g/hour for glucose alone. For masters athletes, the recommended starting rate is 60 g/hour, gradually increasing to 90 g/hour during training, and only up to 90–120 g/hour for well-adapted athletes during competition.

- **Gels**: Most sports gels contain a 2:1 glucose-to-fructose ratio (e.g., 20 g glucose + 10 g fructose per gel). Taking one gel every 20–30 minutes with water is a common strategy.
- **Chews and blocks**: Similar to gels but in solid form; may cause less GI distress in some athletes.
- **Sports drinks**: Drinks that contain a mixture of glucose and fructose (e.g., 6–8% carbohydrate concentration) are effective for hydration and fueling simultaneously.
- **Real food**: Some athletes tolerate low-FODMAP fruits (bananas, orange segments), rice cakes, or low-FODMAP energy bars (e.g., based on oats, rice, peanut butter, and maple syrup) during long runs.

#### 4.2.2 Hydration and Electrolytes

Dehydration is a major risk factor for GI distress, and masters athletes are at higher risk due to age-related declines in thirst sensation and thermoregulation. The ACMSM guidelines recommend:

- **Pre-hydration**: Drink 5–10 mL/kg body weight of fluid in the 2–4 hours before exercise, and an additional 3–5 mL/kg if urine is dark.
- **During exercise**: Drink 0.4–0.8 L/hour (about 4–8 oz every 15–20 minutes), adjusted based on sweat rate (which can be estimated by weighing before and after a training run). The goal is to limit fluid loss to <2% of body weight.
- **Electrolytes**: Sodium is the most important electrolyte lost in sweat. Aim for 300–600 mg/L of sodium in fluids (or about 500–1000 mg/hour for a hot environment). Potassium, magnesium, and calcium are also lost but are usually replenished by post-run meals.
- **Adequate sodium intake**: Prevents hyponatremia (low blood sodium), which can cause nausea, vomiting, and confusion—symptoms that closely mimic GI distress. Hyponatremia is more common in masters athletes due to lower kidney function and higher sodium loss in sweat.

#### 4.2.3 Avoiding High-Osmolarity Fluids

Drinks that are too concentrated (high osmolarity, >10% carbohydrate) can delay gastric emptying and draw water into the gut, causing bloating, cramping, and diarrhea. This is a common cause of GI distress in athletes who use concentrated gels or energy blocks without enough water. The recommendation is to chase each gel with 4–6 oz of water, or to use a sports drink at 6–8% concentration.

### 4.3 Training Adjustments

#### 4.3.1 Heat Acclimation

Because hyperthermia exacerbates splanchnic hypoperfusion and intestinal permeability, heat acclimation—a period of 7–14 days of training in the heat—can reduce core body temperature, sweat rate, and skin blood flow during exercise, thereby preserving splanchnic blood flow. Pugh et al. (2020) showed that heat acclimation attenuated the rise in I-FABP and GI symptoms during a 2-hour run in the heat. Masters athletes training in hot climates should plan for a gradual heat acclimation protocol, including training at the hottest part of the day, using sauna or hot yoga as adjuncts, and ensuring adequate hydration.

#### 4.3.2 Pace and Intensity Management

Running at too high an intensity relative to fitness level exacerbates splanchnic hypoperfusion, as the demand for blood flow to working muscles is higher. Masters athletes should adhere to the principle of "run the first third at a conversational pace, the middle third at a steady effort, and the last third at race pace." Using heart rate zones or perceived exertion (RPE) to stay in Zone 2–3 for the majority of the run can reduce the risk of GI distress. Incorporating walk breaks or reducing pace during uphill sections can also help maintain splanchnic blood flow.

#### 4.3.3 Gut Training Protocols

As described in Section 3.3.1, gut training is a structured approach to increasing the gut's tolerance to fuel and fluid during exercise. A typical protocol:

- **Week 1–2**: Consume 30 g/hour of carbohydrates (e.g., 1 gel + 4 oz sports drink) during long runs
- **Week 3–4**: Increase to 60 g/hour (e.g., 1 gel + 8 oz sports drink every 30 minutes)
- **Week 5–6**: Increase to 90 g/hour (e.g., 2 gels + 8 oz sports drink every 30 minutes, or 1.5 gels + 6 oz every 20 minutes)
- **Week 7+**: Practice at race-specific carbohydrate rate (up to 120 g/hour for well-adapted athletes)

Gut training should be done during long runs (≥90 minutes) and at least 2–3 times per week. It is critical to note that gut training does not mean "pushing through" severe GI distress; if symptoms are severe, the athlete should back down to the previous tolerated dose and progress more slowly.

#### 4.3.4 Running Form and Core Strength

While the evidence is limited, some runners report that improving running form—particularly, reducing trunk rotation, maintaining a stable core, and avoiding excessive forward lean—can reduce mechanical jarring of the abdominal contents. Core strengthening exercises (planks, dead bugs, bird dogs, Russian twists) can improve the ability to stabilize the trunk during running, potentially reducing the compressive forces on the abdomen. Anecdotal reports also suggest that running with a relaxed posture and avoiding a "stomach-in" posture (which can increase intra-abdominal pressure) may help.

#### 4.3.5 Timing of Fluid and Fuel Ingestion

The timing of fluid and fuel intake during exercise can be optimized:

- **First 30 minutes**: No fuel needed; water only (or a small amount of sports drink if training at high intensity)
- **30–60 minutes**: Start fueling with 1 gel or 1 serving of sports drink every 20–30 minutes
- **After 2 hours**: Increase fuel density if needed, but continue to drink water alongside gels
- **Avoid**: Consuming large amounts of fluid or fuel immediately before a steep hill or a surge in pace, as this increases the risk of reflux and cramping

### 4.4 Post-Exercise Recovery

Post-exercise nutrition is important for gut repair. Consuming a low-FODMAP, high-carbohydrate, moderate-protein meal within 30–60 minutes after exercise, along with adequate fluid and electrolytes, can help restore barrier function and reduce inflammation. Specific nutrients that support gut repair include:

- **Glutamine**: Found in bone broth, chicken, fish, eggs, and dairy (or as a supplement)
- **Zinc**: Found in oysters, red meat, poultry, beans, and nuts
- **Vitamin A**: Found in sweet potatoes, carrots, spinach, and eggs
- **Vitamin D**: Found in fatty fish, fortified dairy, and sunlight exposure
- **Omega-3 fatty acids**: Found in fatty fish, flaxseeds, chia seeds, and walnuts

| Domain | Key Recommendations |

## Section 5: Conclusion and Practical Recommendations for Masters Endurance Runners

Exercise-induced gastrointestinal distress in masters endurance runners is a multifactorial condition driven by splanchnic hypoperfusion, ischemia-reperfusion injury, mechanical trauma, dietary triggers, and age-related physiological changes. The sports-medicine literature provides a robust evidence base for understanding the pathophysiology, and the EIGS framework offers a useful conceptual model for clinicians and athletes.

The most effective management strategy is a **layered, personalized approach**:

1. **Start with a medical evaluation** to rule out underlying organic pathology, including celiac disease, inflammatory bowel disease, food allergies, peptic ulcer disease, and gastrointestinal infections. This is particularly important for masters athletes, who may have age-related conditions that mimic or exacerbate exercise-induced GI distress.

2. **Address dietary triggers** by implementing a low-FODMAP diet for 24–48 hours before long runs, reducing fiber and fat intake before exercise, and avoiding high-FODMAP foods, NSAIDs, and excessive caffeine.

3. **Optimize fueling and hydration** using evidence-based guidelines: multiple transportable carbohydrates at 60–90 g/hour, adequate sodium, and strategies to prevent dehydration and hyperthermia.

4. **Engage in gut training and heat acclimation** during the training block, gradually increasing the gut's tolerance to fuel and fluid, and preparing the body to perform in warm conditions.

5. **Consider pharmacological and supplement interventions** judiciously—loperamide for diarrhea, ondansetron for nausea, bovine colostrum for gut barrier support, probiotics for symptom reduction—under the guidance of a sports medicine physician.

6. **Manage stress and the gut-brain axis** through relaxation techniques, CBT, and biofeedback, particularly for athletes with high anxiety about GI symptoms.

7. **Monitor and adjust** based on individual response, keeping a symptom log to identify specific triggers and patterns.

This comprehensive approach, grounded in peer-reviewed research and clinical guidelines, can help masters endurance runners dramatically reduce—or even eliminate—the burden of GI distress and return to enjoying their sport.
