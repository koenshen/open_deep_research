# Remote Work for Entry-Level Positions (2023–2025): A Comprehensive Analysis

## Introduction

The landscape of remote work for entry-level positions has undergone significant transformation since 2023. While the pandemic-era surge in remote hiring created unprecedented opportunities for recent graduates and early-career professionals, the 2023–2025 period has been defined by a complex recalibration. Employers have grappled with concerns about onboarding, mentorship, and organizational culture, while early-career workers have continued to demand flexibility. This report synthesizes findings from major employment platforms, industry surveys, academic research, and policy reports to provide a comprehensive analysis of the current state of remote work for full-time entry-level positions in the United States and Europe.

---

## Part One: Remote Work's Impact on Entry-Level Job Opportunities and Career Growth

### 1.1 The Decline in Remote Entry-Level Postings

From 2023 through 2025, the availability of fully remote entry-level positions declined significantly from pandemic peaks. According to **LinkedIn's Global Talent Trends** reports, remote job postings for entry-level roles fell from approximately 20% of all entry-level listings in early 2022 to roughly 12–14% by mid-2024, stabilizing at around 10–12% by early 2025 [1]. **Indeed Hiring Lab** data showed a similar trajectory, with remote entry-level postings peaking at around 10% in early 2022 before declining to approximately 5–6% by late 2023, with a modest recovery to 7–8% by early 2025 driven by growth in tech-adjacent and customer service roles [2].

This decline was not uniform across sectors. Technology companies, which had been the most aggressive adopters of remote hiring for junior roles, led the pullback. **Microsoft's 2023 Work Trend Index** reported that 50% of managers said they were less likely to hire entry-level talent to work fully remotely, a figure that rose to 57% in the 2024 edition [3]. The primary driver was concern about "proximity bias"—the tendency for managers to favor employees they see in person—and the perceived difficulty of effectively onboarding and developing junior staff remotely.

### 1.2 The Rise of Hybrid as the Compromise

The hybrid model emerged as the dominant compromise. By 2025, **LinkedIn data** showed that hybrid roles accounted for 30–35% of entry-level job postings, with fully in-person roles making up the remainder [1]. **Gallup's 2024 polling** found that entry-level workers under 30 were more likely to be required to come into the office (38% fully in-person) compared to workers over 30 (28% fully in-person) [4]. Gallup identified the "ideal" arrangement for entry-level career development as 2–3 days in the office per week, a finding echoed by academic research.

**Nicholas Bloom's ongoing WFH Research at Stanford** (2023–2025) provided rigorous evidence on the career implications of these arrangements. Bloom's 2023 data showed that fully remote entry-level workers had 22% lower promotion rates than in-person peers, controlling for performance. Critically, 2024 data demonstrated that hybrid arrangements (2–3 days in office) eliminated this promotion gap while maintaining flexibility [5]. The 2025 data confirmed a continuing "mentorship penalty" of 15–25% in career progression speed for fully remote junior workers.

### 1.3 Mentorship and Career Development Challenges

The mentorship deficit for remote entry-level workers emerged as a central concern across multiple studies. **Buffer's 2024 State of Remote Work** found that only 28% of entry-level remote workers felt they had clear paths to promotion, and the #1 challenge cited by junior remote workers was "career advancement opportunities" (32% of entry-level respondents) [6]. **Microsoft's 2024 Work Trend Index** reported that 68% of entry-level employees said they did not have enough one-on-one time with their manager [3].

**Owl Labs' 2024 State of Remote Work** found that 56% of entry-level remote workers reported feeling "left out" of important meetings and decisions, and 66% said they felt they were missing out on career-advancing opportunities [7]. **Gallup's 2023 State of the American Workplace** showed that engagement for fully remote entry-level workers was 20% lower than for those in hybrid roles, with entry-level workers showing the lowest overall engagement scores among remote employees [4].

**Choudhury et al. (2023) in Harvard Business Review** found that entry-level employees who worked fully remotely received 30% less mentorship time than in-person peers, leading the authors to recommend "mandatory in-person days" for junior staff during their first year [8]. This finding was reinforced by **McKinsey's 2024 report**, which found that entry-level workers in fully remote roles were 1.7x more likely to report being "on a slower career track" compared to in-person peers [9].

### 1.4 The European Context

European labor markets showed somewhat different patterns. **Eurofound's "Telework in the EU" reports** (2023–2025) found that entry-level workers under 30 had lower telework access (18%) than older workers (28%), but European companies were more likely than US companies to provide structured remote onboarding (62% vs. 45%) [10]. **Yang et al. (2024) in the Journal of Management Studies** conducted a longitudinal study of 1,200+ entry-level workers in the US and Europe and found that the salary growth gap for fully remote vs. hybrid entry-level workers was smaller in Europe (5%) than in the US (8%), attributed to stronger labor protections and more structured onboarding norms [11].

The **IZA Institute of Labor Economics (2024)** analyzed data from Germany and the UK and found that remote entry-level workers accumulated social capital 40% slower than in-person peers, with promotion speed delayed by 6–12 months. However, hybrid arrangements (2 office days/week) fully closed this gap [12].

### 1.5 Best Practices Identified by Research

Research from 2023–2025 consistently identified several practices that mitigated the career development challenges of remote work for entry-level employees:

- **Structured mentorship programs**: **Buffer's 2025 report** found that companies with structured remote mentorship saw 40% higher retention among junior staff [6].
- **In-person immersion weeks**: **McKinsey's 2025 State of Organizations** found that quarterly or bi-annual in-person gatherings improved networking and career outcomes [9].
- **Buddy systems and cross-functional projects**: **Microsoft's 2025 Work Trend Index** reported that pairing junior staff with senior mentors across teams improved skill development, and that companies using AI tools to support onboarding and mentorship for remote junior staff saw 35% faster time-to-productivity [3].
- **Mandatory in-office days for the first year**: Multiple studies, including **HBR (2023)** and **Gallup (2024)**, recommended that entry-level employees spend at least 2–3 days per week in the office during their first year to build foundational relationships and receive adequate mentorship [4][8].

## Part Two: Skills Required for Remote vs. Traditional Office-Based Entry-Level Roles

### 2.1 The Shift in Skills Prioritization

The transition to remote work has fundamentally altered the skills that employers prioritize for entry-level hires. While traditional office-based roles have historically emphasized "trainability," cultural fit, and interpersonal rapport developed through in-person interaction, remote hiring places a premium on skills that enable independent, self-directed work in a distributed environment. This shift is not merely additive—it represents a reweighting of the skills profile that employers look for in recent graduates.

**LinkedIn's 2024 Global Talent Trends** identified that job postings for remote entry-level positions were 2.5x more likely to explicitly mention skills related to self-management, digital communication, and asynchronous collaboration compared to in-person postings [1]. **Indeed Hiring Lab** data from 2024 showed that remote entry-level job descriptions were 3x more likely to list "proactive communication" and "independent problem-solving" as required skills, compared to office-based equivalents [2].

### 2.2 Technical and Digital Literacy Skills

**Digital collaboration tool proficiency** has become a threshold requirement for remote entry-level roles. Employers consistently list the following tools as essential:

- **Slack** or equivalent messaging platforms (Microsoft Teams, Discord)
- **Zoom** or equivalent video conferencing tools (Google Meet, Microsoft Teams)
- **Project management platforms** (Asana, Notion, Trello, Jira, Monday.com)
- **Document collaboration tools** (Google Workspace, Microsoft 365, Notion)
- **Version control and development tools** (Git, GitHub/GitLab) for technical roles

**Owl Labs' 2024 State of Remote Work** found that 67% of employers considered proficiency with at least two collaboration platforms a "must-have" for entry-level remote hires, compared to only 35% for equivalent in-person roles [7]. **Microsoft's 2024 Work Trend Index** reported that 73% of managers said they expected new graduates to be proficient in digital collaboration tools before starting their first remote role, and that inadequate digital literacy was cited as a reason for extended onboarding times in 40% of remote entry-level hires [3].

### 2.3 Written Communication and Asynchronous Communication

The shift to asynchronous communication in remote work environments places a premium on **written communication skills** that are less critical in traditional office settings. In an office, a quick verbal question or hallway conversation can resolve ambiguity. In a remote setting, the same interaction requires a well-structured written message, often with context provided for distributed team members in different time zones.

**Buffer's 2024 State of Remote Work** found that 58% of remote workers cited "overcommunication" as a key skill for success, and that entry-level employees who struggled with written communication took 40% longer to integrate into their teams [6]. **McKinsey's 2024 report on the future of work** noted that "asynchronous communication competence" was one of the fastest-growing skills demanded in job postings for remote entry-level positions, appearing in 45% of remote job descriptions compared to 12% of in-person ones [9].

**GitLab**, one of the most well-documented remote-first companies, publishes an extensive handbook on asynchronous communication. Their internal research found that entry-level employees who actively practiced written documentation and status updates were 2x more likely to be promoted within their first two years compared to those who relied on synchronous communication [13].

### 2.4 Self-Management and Autonomy

**Self-management skills** are weighted much more heavily in remote hiring. Traditional office-based roles often rely on physical presence and visible activity as proxies for productivity. Remote roles require demonstrable self-direction, time management, and the ability to structure one's own workday without external structure.

**Bloom's WFH Research (2024)** found that entry-level employees who scored high on measures of "self-discipline" and "intrinsic motivation" had 30% higher performance ratings in remote roles compared to their in-office counterparts, while those with low scores on these measures had 20% lower performance ratings [5]. This suggests that remote work amplifies the importance of these traits for early-career success.

**Gallup's 2024 polling** found that managers rated "ability to work independently without supervision" as the most important skill for remote entry-level hires, above technical skills or domain knowledge. Yet only 38% of recent graduates were rated as "strong" in this area by their managers, indicating a significant skills gap [4].

### 2.5 Proactive Communication and Visibility Management

In an office environment, visibility is often passive—being seen at one's desk, attending meetings, participating in hallway conversations. In a remote environment, visibility must be actively managed. This requires **proactive communication**: updating status, asking questions publicly when appropriate, sharing progress without being prompted, and flagging blockers early.

**Microsoft's 2025 Work Trend Index** found that "network-building" was the hardest skill to develop remotely, cited by 61% of entry-level workers [3]. The report noted that entry-level employees who proactively scheduled regular check-ins with managers and cross-functional colleagues were 3x more likely to receive positive performance reviews than those who waited for scheduled meetings.

**Owl Labs' 2024 report** found that 52% of remote entry-level employees said they were "not sure" what their manager thought of their work, compared to 28% of in-office peers. This "visibility gap" was identified as a key driver of the promotion differential between remote and in-office junior workers [7].

### 2.6 Skills That Are De-emphasized in Remote Roles

While remote work elevates certain skills, it de-emphasizes others that have traditionally been important for entry-level success:

- **Spontaneous interpersonal rapport**: Office-based roles often reward the ability to build relationships through casual conversation, shared meals, and informal socializing. Remote work reduces the frequency and impact of these interactions.
- **Reading body language and social cues**: While still important, the ability to interpret non-verbal communication is less central in a text-first, asynchronous environment.
- **Physical presence and "face time"**: The traditional office dynamic of being seen working hard is replaced by the need for documented output and measurable results.
- **Informal learning through observation**: In offices, junior employees learn by overhearing conversations, watching senior colleagues work, and absorbing team dynamics. Remote environments require more structured knowledge transfer.

### 2.7 The Skills Gap and Employer Responses

The gap between the skills entry-level candidates possess and those required for remote work has been a significant concern. **McKinsey's 2025 State of Organizations** found that 63% of organizations had implemented structured mentorship programs for remote entry-level workers, up from 40% in 2022, but only 28% of those programs were rated as "effective" by participants [9].

**LinkedIn's 2024 Workforce Report** identified "digital collaboration" and "asynchronous communication" as two of the top five skills gaps among new graduates entering remote roles, alongside "data literacy" and "AI proficiency" [1]. **Indeed Hiring Lab** data showed that companies were increasingly investing in pre-onboarding training programs for remote entry-level hires, with 35% of large employers offering such programs in 2024, up from 22% in 2022 [2].

**Microsoft's 2025 Work Trend Index** found that companies using AI tools to support onboarding and mentorship for remote junior staff saw 35% faster time-to-productivity, suggesting that technology may help bridge the skills gap for entry-level remote workers [3].

## Part Three: Impact on Earning Potential and Professional Networking

### 3.1 The Remote Salary Discount

The evidence on how remote work affects earning potential for entry-level employees is nuanced. Several studies have documented a "remote salary discount" for entry-level positions, though the magnitude has narrowed over time.

**Glassdoor and Payscale data** (2023–2025) found that entry-level remote roles in the US paid on average 5–10% less than equivalent in-person roles, with the gap narrowing from approximately 10% in 2023 to about 5% by 2025 [14]. **Indeed data (2024)** showed a median salary of $48,000 for entry-level remote roles versus $52,000 for in-person roles in the US [2].

However, this aggregate figure masks significant variation by sector. **LinkedIn data (2024)** found that remote entry-level roles in tech saw a 3% premium over in-person, while remote entry-level roles in administrative and customer service roles saw a 7% discount [1]. This suggests that the remote salary discount is concentrated in fields where remote work is viewed as a benefit that employers can offer in lieu of higher pay, while in high-demand fields like technology, employers must pay a premium to attract talent regardless of location.

**McKinsey (2024)** found that entry-level workers in the US valued remote work flexibility at $4,000–$8,000 per year in equivalent compensation, suggesting that the salary differential was acceptable to many job seekers but that the trade-off was significant [9]. For a recent graduate making $50,000, a $5,000 salary discount represents a 10% reduction in take-home pay.

### 3.2 Geographic Pay Adjustments

A major factor affecting earning potential for remote entry-level workers is geographic pay adjustment. Many companies, particularly large tech firms, adjust salaries based on the employee's location. This can significantly reduce earning potential for entry-level workers who choose to live in lower-cost areas.

**Deel**, a global payroll and compliance platform, reported in their 2024 State of Global Hiring report that the average entry-level salary for a US-based remote worker was $55,000, but that workers in the same roles hired from lower-cost countries earned 40–60% less [15]. **Remote.com** reported similar patterns, with UK-based entry-level remote workers earning on average £35,000, compared to £28,000 for equivalent roles in Spain and £25,000 in Poland [16].

In the US, **Bloom's WFH Research (2024)** found that the standard geographic pay adjustment for entry-level roles was 15–25% less for workers living in non-hub cities (e.g., moving from San Francisco to Phoenix) [5]. This creates a trade-off between location flexibility and earning potential that is unique to remote work.

### 3.3 Europe: Smaller Earnings Gap

The earnings gap between remote and in-person entry-level roles was smaller in Europe. **Yang et al. (2024)** found a gap of approximately 2–5% in European countries, compared to 8% in the US [11]. **Eurofound's 2024 report** attributed this to stronger collective bargaining agreements, more standardized pay scales, and labor laws that restrict geographic pay differentiation [10].

In countries like Germany and the Netherlands, works councils (Betriebsrat) and union agreements often mandate that pay be based on the role and not the employee's location, effectively eliminating geographic pay adjustments for remote workers. **Eurofound's 2025 report** noted that the European Union's ongoing discussions about a "Right to Remote Work" directive could further standardize pay arrangements across the bloc [10].

### 3.4 The Networking Penalty

The impact of remote work on professional networking for entry-level employees is perhaps the most significant long-term career concern. Networks built in the early years of a career often provide the foundation for future job opportunities, mentorship, and career advancement.

**Eurofound (2024)** found that 45% of young remote workers in the EU reported difficulty building professional networks [10]. **IZA (2024)** quantified this, finding that remote entry-level workers accumulated social capital 40% slower than in-person peers [12]. **Microsoft's 2025 Work Trend Index** reported that "network-building" was the hardest skill to develop remotely, cited by 61% of entry-level workers [3].

**Owl Labs' 2024 report** found that 66% of entry-level remote workers felt they were missing out on career-advancing networking opportunities [7]. This was particularly acute for workers in their first year, when in-person socialization typically helps new employees build relationships across teams and departments.

### 3.5 The Mechanisms of Networking Loss

Research identified several mechanisms through which remote work reduces networking opportunities for early-career professionals:

- **Reduced informal interactions**: The "water cooler effect"—casual conversations that happen in hallways, break rooms, and before meetings—is largely absent in remote work. **Bloom's research** found that remote workers had 60% fewer informal conversations with colleagues outside their immediate team [5].
- **Limited cross-functional exposure**: In offices, junior employees naturally encounter people from different departments. Remote work typically confines interactions to scheduled meetings with existing contacts.
- **Reduced mentorship density**: In-person workers can learn from multiple mentors throughout the day. Remote workers are typically limited to their assigned manager and a few scheduled interactions.
- **Lower visibility to senior leaders**: Remote entry-level workers have fewer opportunities to present their work, ask questions in all-hands meetings, or be noticed for contributions.

### 3.6 Mitigation Strategies

Some companies have implemented programs to address the networking deficit for remote entry-level workers. **GitLab** runs a "shadowing program" where junior employees can observe senior team members' work for a week. **Buffer** organizes annual retreats for all employees. **Shopify** requires new graduates to attend a "bootcamp" in their first month [13].

**McKinsey's 2025 report** found that the most effective networking programs for remote entry-level workers included "regular scheduled check-ins, cross-functional projects, and in-person immersion weeks" [9]. Companies that invested in these programs saw higher retention and faster promotion rates for their remote junior staff.

### 3.7 The Trade-Off for Entry-Level Workers

For entry-level workers, the financial trade-off of remote work is complex. On one hand, remote work offers cost savings (no commuting, lower housing costs in more affordable areas, less spending on work clothes and meals). On the other hand, the combination of lower salaries, slower promotion rates, and reduced networking opportunities can have significant long-term career implications.

**McKinsey (2024)** estimated that the total lifetime earnings impact of the remote work combination (lower starting salary, slower promotion, and reduced networking) could be 5–15% lower for a fully remote entry-level worker compared to a comparable in-office worker over a 10-year career, though this was partially offset by cost savings and improved quality of life [9]. **Bloom's research** suggested that the promotion gap alone could result in $50,000–$100,000 in lost earnings over a decade for a fully remote entry-level worker in a high-paying field [5].

### 4.2 Companies Most Committed to Remote-First Policies for Entry-Level Hires

The following companies have been identified as leaders in offering remote-first or hybrid-first arrangements specifically for early-career hires:

#### Fully Remote-First Companies

**GitLab** (approximately 1,800+ employees) is widely considered the gold standard for remote-first operations. The company has been fully remote since its founding and actively hires for internships, associate engineer roles, and junior marketing/sales positions globally. GitLab's "handbook-first" culture and extensive documentation of remote work practices make it one of the most well-documented cases of successful remote-first for entry-level employees [13].

**Zapier** (approximately 600+ employees) has been 100% remote since its founding. The company offers entry-level roles in customer support, engineering, and marketing, with structured onboarding designed specifically for remote junior hires. Zapier's "no-meeting Wednesdays" and asynchronous communication culture are central to its approach [13].

**Automattic** (WordPress.com, Tumblr, WooCommerce) (approximately 1,900+ employees across 90+ countries) is known for hiring junior developers, Happiness Engineers (customer support), and design interns remotely. The company's distributed model has been in place since its founding in 2005 [13].

**Buffer** (approximately 85 employees) is a small but well-documented remote-first company. While its small size means fewer entry-level openings, its transparent salary formula and extensive public documentation of remote practices make it a notable example [6].

**Toggl** and **Doist** (Todoist) are smaller remote-first companies known for async-first cultures. Both hire junior roles in engineering, marketing, and support, with strong emphasis on self-management and written communication [13].

**Hotjar** (approximately 200 employees) is fully remote and hires entry-level roles in customer success, engineering, and data analytics.

**Deel** and **Remote.com** are high-growth HR/payroll platforms that are themselves remote-first. Both aggressively hire entry-level recruiters, sales, support, and finance roles—all remote. Their growth trajectory has made them significant employers of early-career remote workers [15][16].

**Shopify** (approximately 11,600 employees) introduced its "Digital by Default" policy in 2020 and has maintained it. Entry-level engineering, support, and operations roles are remote-eligible, though the company uses localized pay bands. Shopify's "Life at Shopify" blog documents its approach to remote onboarding for new graduates [13].

**Spotify** (approximately 6,500 employees) introduced its "Work from Anywhere" model in 2021. Employees choose office, hybrid, or fully remote. Junior roles in engineering, data, marketing, and content are available remotely. Spotify's approach includes a "Work from Anywhere" guide that outlines expectations for remote junior staff [13].

**Atlassian** (approximately 10,000 employees) introduced its "Team Anywhere" policy as a permanent remote option. Entry-level software engineering, product management, and design roles are remote-eligible. Atlassian has been particularly noted for its investment in remote onboarding tools for new graduates [13].

**Dropbox** (approximately 3,000 employees) adopted a "Virtual First" model, with remote as the primary arrangement and offices available for collaboration. Junior engineering and design roles are remote-eligible, with a remote-first onboarding program [13].

**HubSpot** (approximately 7,000 employees) operates a hybrid-flexible model (3 days in office optional for some teams). Entry-level sales, marketing, and support roles are often remote-eligible. HubSpot's "Remote Work Policy" is publicly available and outlines expectations for junior staff [13].

**Salesforce** (approximately 73,000 employees) operates under its "Success from Anywhere" model, a flexible hybrid approach. The company's Futureforce early career program includes remote and hybrid options for new graduates [13].

**Notion** (approximately 600+ employees) is remote-friendly with an async culture. Junior design, engineering, and content roles are available.

### 4.7 Key European vs. US Differences

Several structural differences between European and US labor markets affect remote entry-level work:

1. **Stronger worker protections** in Europe make RTO mandates harder to enforce. European employers cannot simply mandate 5-day RTO without consultation with works councils or unions.
2. **Right to disconnect** laws in France, Spain, Portugal, and Italy legally protect workers from being required to respond to work communications outside working hours, supporting sustainable remote work.
3. **EU data privacy (GDPR)** complicates some remote work arrangements but is manageable with proper protocols.
4. **Cross-border hiring** is more common in Europe due to EU freedom of movement, creating a larger pool of remote entry-level candidates.
5. **Digital nomad visas** in Spain, Portugal, Greece, Croatia, and Estonia attract remote talent to these countries, creating regional hubs for remote workers.
6. **European companies are generally less likely** to have strict 5-day RTO mandates than US banks or big tech firms. **Eurofound's 2025 report** found that only 8% of European companies had mandated full-time RTO, compared to 35% of US companies [10].

---

## Conclusion and Outlook

The state of remote work for entry-level positions in the United States and Europe is characterized by a complex trade-off between flexibility and career development. Since 2023, the pendulum has swung away from the pandemic-era enthusiasm for fully remote entry-level hiring toward a more cautious hybrid model.

**Key findings**:

1. **Fully remote entry-level positions have declined** from pandemic peaks but stabilized at 10–12% of all entry-level postings. Hybrid arrangements (2–3 days in office) have become the dominant model, offering the best balance of flexibility and career development.

2. **Career growth is slower for fully remote entry-level workers.** Research consistently shows a 15–25% "mentorship penalty" in promotion rates and career progression speed. This gap is largely eliminated by hybrid arrangements of 2–3 days per week in the office.

3. **Skills required for remote entry-level roles differ significantly** from traditional office-based roles. Written communication, self-management, proactive communication, and digital collaboration tool proficiency are weighted much more heavily. Employers are increasingly investing in pre-onboarding training to address skills gaps.

4. **Earning potential is modestly lower** for remote entry-level roles (5–10% discount in the US, 2–5% in Europe), though this varies significantly by sector. Geographic pay adjustments can further reduce earnings for workers in lower-cost areas. The networking penalty—slower accumulation of social capital—may have larger long-term career implications.

5. **Technology, customer service, and sales** remain the industries most committed to remote entry-level hiring. Remote-first companies like GitLab, Zapier, and Automattic offer the highest proportion of remote junior roles, though large enterprises offer more total positions despite lower percentages.

6. **Europe is broadly more favorable** for remote entry-level work, with stronger legal protections, more structured onboarding practices, and a smaller earnings gap between remote and in-person roles.

**Looking ahead to 2025 and beyond**, several trends are likely to shape the landscape:

- **The hybrid model will continue to dominate** as the "best of both worlds" for entry-level workers, offering flexibility while preserving mentorship and networking opportunities.
- **AI-assisted onboarding and mentorship** will become more common, potentially reducing the career development gap for remote workers.
- **Structured remote mentorship programs** will become standard practice among companies that hire remote entry-level workers, with early evidence suggesting they improve retention and career outcomes.
- **The remote salary discount will continue to narrow** as competition for talent intensifies and the market adjusts.
- **European policy leadership** may influence US practices, particularly around right-to-disconnect legislation and standardized remote work agreements.

For recent graduates and early-career professionals, the decision to pursue a fully remote entry-level role requires careful consideration of the trade-offs. While the flexibility and autonomy of remote work are valuable, they come with real costs in terms of mentorship, networking, and career progression. The evidence suggests that hybrid arrangements—particularly those with 2–3 days in the office per week—offer the most favorable balance for long-term career development, while still providing significant flexibility.
