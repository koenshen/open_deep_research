## 1. Market Overview

### 1.1 North America Market Size and Growth

The North American food delivery market is measured at multiple scopes, reflecting different definitions of the market boundary. According to IMARC Group, the **North America online food delivery market** reached **USD 42.4 billion in 2025** and is forecast to reach USD 112.5 billion by 2034, at a CAGR of 11.11% [1]. Mordor Intelligence estimates the **North America Food Platform-to-Consumer (P2C) Delivery market** at **USD 37.91 billion in 2025**, expected to reach USD 54.98 billion by 2030 at a CAGR of 7.72% [2]. ResearchAndMarkets projects the broader North American online food delivery services market at **USD 38.0 billion in 2024**, growing to **USD 105.8 billion by 2033** at a CAGR of 11.57% [3].

**United States** is the dominant market within North America. Key estimates include:

- **Narrow definition (platform-to-consumer delivery):** USD 34.88 billion in 2025, projected to reach USD 75.36 billion by 2034 at 8.94% CAGR (IMARC Group) [4]. Statista-based estimates place U.S. online food delivery revenue at $36.32 billion in 2025, with user penetration of 44.5% and average spend per user of $299.60 annually [5].
- **Broader definition (including grocery and all segments):** USD 119.46 billion in 2025, anticipated to reach USD 130.28 billion in 2026 and USD 242.87 billion by 2034 at 9.05% CAGR (Market Data Forecast) [6].
- **Broadest definition (total online food delivery revenue at GMV):** Statista notes the U.S. generated an estimated $432 billion in online food delivery revenue in 2025, with over $100 billion from the meal delivery segment alone [7].

**Canada** is a smaller but rapidly growing market, projected to grow from **USD 9.8 billion in 2025** to USD 25.7 billion by 2031, registering a robust CAGR of 17.5% (Mobility Foresights) [8]. Key growth drivers include rising smartphone penetration, changing urban lifestyles, and the lasting impact of COVID-19 on consumer behavior.

### 1.3 Segmentation by Delivery Type

**Restaurant Meal Delivery** remains the dominant segment in both regions. In North America, the meal delivery segment generated over $100 billion in 2025 [7]. Platform-to-consumer channels hold 58% of market share globally for meal delivery [11]. In Europe, restaurant-to-consumer delivery dominates with a 62.0% share [13], while ready-to-eat meals held 44.30% of the P2C delivery market in 2025 [9].

**Grocery Delivery** is the fastest-growing segment. The U.S. online grocery market is valued at **USD 203.0 billion in 2025**, forecast to reach USD 712.2 billion by 2034 at 14.97% CAGR [16]. In Europe, the online grocery market was valued at **USD 319.97 billion in 2025**, growing at 18.65% CAGR to USD 1,491.16 billion by 2034 [15]. Quick commerce (under 30-minute delivery) is the fastest-growing sub-segment at 24.5% CAGR in Europe [15].

**Convenience Store Delivery** is a growing vertical. DoorDash's "New Verticals" (grocery, convenience, retail) are a key growth area, with non-restaurant delivery now accounting for **18% of Uber Eats gross bookings** [17]. Quick commerce (10-30 minute delivery of convenience items) is the fastest-growing slice of online grocery at a **20.3% CAGR** in North America [18].

### 1.4 Segmentation by Platform Type

**Aggregator/Marketplace Platforms** (third-party) dominate the market. In North America, aggregators account for approximately 67% of P2C delivery revenue, with DoorDash alone holding 65-68% market share [19]. In Europe, aggregators held **64.30% of the P2C delivery market** in 2025 [9]. Globally, platform-to-consumer channels hold 58% of market share [11].

**First-Party/Restaurant-Owned Delivery** represents a significant share. According to EMARKETER, 53.0% of food delivery orders are placed directly through restaurants [20]. In the U.S., restaurant-to-consumer accounted for $14.21 billion in 2025, compared to $22.11 billion for platform-to-consumer [5]. First-party delivery is faster (average 30m53s vs. 34m11s for third-party) and more accurate (90% accuracy vs. 85%) [21].

### 1.5 Geographic Sub-Region Segmentation (U.S.)

The **South region** holds the largest share of the U.S. online food delivery market at **28.7%** in 2025, followed by the **West (26.1%)** , **Northeast (23.4%)** , and **Midwest (21.8%)** [4]. The South's dominance reflects demographic tailwinds from Texas, Florida, and Georgia—the three fastest-growing state economies by population. DoorDash leads in suburban and western regions, Uber Eats dominates in dense cities, and Grubhub remains strong in the Northeast [5].

## 2. Demand Drivers

### 2.1 Urbanization

Urbanization is a powerful structural driver of food delivery adoption. **Over 80% of North America's population** lives in urban areas, while **over 75% of Europe's population** is urbanized [22]. By 2050, an estimated 70% of the global population will live in cities [23]. Urban dwellers order delivery far more than rural residents—39.2% of urban residents use food delivery apps weekly compared to only 15.3% in rural areas [24].

The World Economic Forum projects a **36% increase in delivery vehicles** in the world's top 100 cities by 2030 compared to 2019 levels [25]. Urbanization drives changes in consumption habits, with urban households tending to purchase more prepared and convenience foods. Approximately **70% of global food is consumed in cities**, and urban populations rely heavily on purchasing the foods they consume [26].

### 2.2 Consumer Convenience Trends

Post-pandemic behavioral shifts have permanently embedded food delivery into consumer lifestyles. Industry surveys indicate that **more than 68% of first-time users during the pandemic continue to place weekly or more frequent orders as of 2025** [27]. Key statistics include:

- **52% of U.S. consumers say food delivery is an "essential" part of their lifestyle** [28]
- **67% of Millennials and 63% of Gen Z rely on food delivery regularly** [28]
- **40% of Gen Z and Millennials order from food delivery apps at least once a week** [29]
- Gen Z orders **2.3 times per week** versus older generations' 1.1 [28]
- **60% of Americans order weekly** [28]
- **73% of consumers stick with the same app for delivery** [30]
- **79% of delivery orders come from restaurants customers have ordered from before** [30]
- **95% of DoorDash orders** placed in the past six months came through mobile [30]

The average American spends over **$1,566 annually on food delivery**, with each order costing about $35.42 [29]. Restaurants partnering with delivery apps see an average **42% incremental revenue increase** in the first year, with average order values **32% higher than dine-in** [28].

### 2.3 Inflation Pressures on Food Costs

Inflation has reshaped consumer behavior and restaurant pricing strategies. Overall food prices rose **3.1% in 2025**, much lower than the 11.8% inflation in 2022 but slightly higher than the 1.8% in 2024 [31]. The USDA revised its 2026 outlook: overall food prices projected to rise **3%** , with restaurant prices forecast to accelerate to **4.6%** [32]. Food prices are up **34.6% since 2019** [33].

Key category-level inflation (2026 forecasts):
- **Beef and veal**: predicted to rise 7.5% [32]
- **Egg prices**: predicted to decrease 30.4% as HPAI outbreaks ease [32]
- **Fresh vegetables**: forecast up 7.7% [32]
- **Sugar/sweets**: up 6.9% [32]

Restaurant operators are adopting data-driven pricing strategies including fractional price adjustments, shrinkflation/portion redesign, anchor pricing, and digital menu agility [34]. McDonald's launched "Under $3 Menu" and "$4 Breakfast Meal Deal" starting April 2026 as a data-driven approach to value [35]. Consumers are shifting behavior toward value-oriented options, with 82% of customers saying affordability shapes their likelihood to order [21].

### 2.4 Shift Toward Online and Mobile Ordering

Mobile applications dominate the platform landscape. In 2025:
- **Mobile applications** held **72.3% of the U.S. online food delivery platform share** [4]
- In Europe, mobile applications accounted for **89.20% of orders** [9]
- **Online payments** held **81.6% of the U.S. market** [4]
- **Digital wallets** are used by 58% of U.S. adults [36]

Technology adoption is accelerating: **95% of restaurants use AI-assisted inventory management, menu optimization, and other forms of AI** [28]. By 2025, artificial intelligence is expected to handle **50% of all restaurant customer interactions** [37]. Drone delivery is projected to handle 8% of orders by end of 2025 [28], and the autonomous last-mile delivery market is projected to grow to $51 billion by 2028 [38].

## 3. Major Challenges

### 3.1 Profitability Margins

The food delivery industry has historically struggled with profitability. Of nearly twenty companies analyzed, only four publicly reported positive Free Cash Flow in 2024—after approximately ten years of operation [39]. Industry gross margins improved from 26% to 37%, but most platforms continue to burn cash when measured by FCF rather than EBITDA [39].

**"At its simplest, delivery is a business that moves low-margin, low-ticket physical goods, to a dispersed customer base, on-demand. It uses variable, often under-optimised labour, at peak times, when the system is most stressed. It's a high-friction, low-leverage logistics layer stapled to an industry with little room to share."** (Shane O'Neill, cited by Peter Backman) [39]

### 3.2 Labor Regulation

**California Prop 22**: Proposition 22, passed in November 2020, classified app-based drivers as independent contractors while providing benefits including minimum earnings (120% of minimum wage) and healthcare stipends. In March 2023, the California Court of Appeal ruled Prop 22 unconstitutional, but the California Supreme Court agreed to hear the appeal in 2024. As of 2025-2026, Prop 22 remains in effect pending final resolution [47]. The law has been a model for other states and has contributed to the operational viability of the gig economy model in California.

**EU Platform Work Directive**: The European Parliament and Council reached a provisional agreement on the Platform Work Directive in December 2023, with final adoption in 2024. The directive establishes an employment relationship when a platform controls work performance, with member states required to implement by 2026. Key provisions include algorithmic transparency, requirement to inform workers about automated decision-making, and prohibition of processing certain personal data [48]. The directive impacts food delivery platforms operating in Europe, potentially requiring reclassification of workers and increasing operational costs.

**UK Worker Status Cases**: The UK Supreme Court ruled in 2021 that Uber drivers were "workers" entitled to minimum wage and holiday pay. In June 2024, the UK Supreme Court ruled that Deliveroo riders were not in an "employment relationship" for collective bargaining purposes, but in February 2025, the UK government's Employment Rights Bill proposed a new "dependent contractor" status that would extend rights to gig workers [49]. The UK's new Labour government has committed to implementing these reforms, which would require platforms to provide minimum earnings, holiday pay, and sick pay.

### 3.3 Competitive Intensity

The competitive landscape is characterized by intense rivalry and consolidation. DoorDash holds **65-68% of the U.S. food delivery market**, followed by Uber Eats at **23-25%** and Grubhub at **6-8%** [19]. In the UK, Just Eat leads with **45%** , followed by Deliveroo and Uber Eats at **27% each** [50].

Major consolidation events in 2025-2026 include:
- **DoorDash acquired Deliveroo** for $3.86 billion (completed October 2025) [51]
- **Prosus acquired Just Eat Takeaway** for €4.1 billion (February 2025) [52]
- **Wonder Group acquired Grubhub** for $650 million (January 2025)—a 90% discount on the $7.3 billion Just Eat Takeaway paid in 2020 [53]
- **Uber agreed to acquire Delivery Hero** for $14.8 billion (July 2026) [54]

Commission structures remain a source of tension. Effective commission rates range from **15% to 30%** for advertised rates, but true all-in costs can reach **35-48%** when delivery fees, service fees, and marketing costs are included [55]. The average independent restaurant runs on a net margin of **3% to 5%** , making these commissions an existential cost pressure [56]. Commission caps exist in NYC (43% max as of May 2025), San Francisco, Denver, Seattle, and Washington D.C. [57].

### 4.3 Commission Structures

Commission structures are complex and vary by plan, order volume, and geographic market. The table below summarizes the key structures:

**Table 3: Commission Structure Comparison**

| Platform | Commission Range | Tiered Plans | Key Details |
|----------|-----------------|--------------|-------------|
| **DoorDash** | 15-30% | Basic (15%), Plus (25%), Premier (30%) | True all-in cost: 35-48% with fees; Pickup: 6% |
| **Uber Eats** | 20-30% | Lite (20%), Plus (25%), Premium (30%), Self-Delivery (15%) | 5% Uber One surcharge on Plus; Webshop: 2.5% + $0.29 |
| **Grubhub** | 15-40% | Variable stacking fees ($2.50-$8/order) | Dropped delivery/service fees on orders >$50 (Feb 2026) |
| **Deliveroo** | 25-35% | Volume-based; Self-delivery: ~10% | Marketplace+: lower fee if partner uses own riders |

**Detailed Breakdown:**

**DoorDash** launched a three-tiered Partnership Plan in April 2021 [62]:
- **Basic (15%)** : Delivery commission only, excludes in-app marketing, reduced delivery radius
- **Plus (25%)** : Includes DashPass access and larger delivery radius
- **Premier (30%)** : Includes Growth Guarantee refunding full commissions if orders fall below 20/month
- All plans: Pickup fees lowered from 15% to 6%
- **True all-in cost**: The actual cost of a $50 order on mid-tier plans can reach 41-43.5% when all fees are included [57]

**Uber Eats** offers three main pricing tiers [63]:
- **Lite (20%)** : 7% pickup fee, basic discoverability
- **Plus (25%)** : 0% intro rate for 30 days, then 25%, 7% pickup fee, 5% additional fee for Uber One orders (effectively 30%)
- **Premium (30%)** : 0% intro rate for 30 days, then 30%, 7% pickup fee, no additional Uber One fee, ad credit matching up to $100/month
- **Self-Delivery (15%)** : 7% pickup fee, option to use Uber network for 25%
- **Uber Direct**: starts at $7.99 per delivery
- **Webshop**: 2.5% + $0.29 per order, commission-free

**Grubhub** commissions range from **15% to 40%** due to variable stacking fees ($2.50-$8 per order) [64]. In February 2026, Grubhub announced a permanent policy waiving delivery and service fees on restaurant orders over $50—fees on such orders typically average $13 per transaction across food delivery apps [65].

**Deliveroo** typically charges **25-35%** depending on volume and contract terms. If the restaurant uses its own riders, the fee is around **10%** [66].

**Industry Context**: The effective third-party delivery cost lands between **35-48%** of gross order revenue, not the advertised 15-30% [55]. Advertising revenue is growing rapidly as a high-margin revenue stream for platforms—DoorDash earned over $1 billion in advertising revenue in 2024 [57].

The combined North American market is valued at approximately $42-47 billion (platform-to-consumer) and growing at 7-11% CAGR, while Europe's market is valued at $34-78 billion (depending on scope) and growing at 7-9% CAGR.

**Key Findings:**

1. **Market Structure**: The U.S. market is dominated by DoorDash (65-68% share), followed by Uber Eats (23-25%) and Grubhub (6-8%). The European market is more fragmented, with Just Eat Takeaway, Deliveroo, and Uber Eats competing, though consolidation is accelerating.

2. **Demand Drivers**: Urbanization (80%+ of North America urbanized), post-pandemic convenience habits (52% of consumers say delivery is essential), and mobile ordering dominance (72-89% of orders via mobile apps) are powerful structural drivers. Inflation (food prices up 34.6% since 2019) is reshaping consumer behavior toward value-oriented options.

3. **Challenges**: Profitability remains elusive for most platforms—only four of nearly twenty companies achieved positive free cash flow by 2024. Labor regulation continues to evolve, with California's Prop 22 remaining in effect, the EU Platform Work Directive requiring implementation by 2026, and UK reforms proposing new "dependent contractor" status. Competitive intensity is driving consolidation, with major acquisitions totaling over $20 billion in 2025-2026.

4. **Competitive Differentiation**: DoorDash leads in delivery speed (26-28 minutes average) and market share, Uber Eats leads in global footprint (45+ countries) and autonomous delivery, Grubhub focuses on East Coast density and Wonder integration, and Deliveroo (now part of DoorDash) brings strong UK grocery partnerships.

5. **Partnerships**: Grocery delivery is the fastest-growing vertical, with all major platforms building extensive grocery partnerships. DoorDash has over 115,000 non-restaurant retailers, Uber Eats has partnerships with Carrefour, Albertsons, and FreshDirect, and Deliveroo has partnerships with all major UK supermarkets. The convenience store segment is growing rapidly, with quick commerce growing at 20-25% CAGR.

**Outlook for 2026-2027**: The industry is trending toward a duopoly structure in North America (DoorDash vs. Uber Eats) and increasing consolidation in Europe. Profitability is improving—DoorDash achieved its first full-year GAAP profit of $935 million in 2025, while Deliveroo and Just Eat Takeaway became free cash flow positive in 2024. Autonomous delivery, AI-driven logistics, and subscription models (DashPass, Uber One) will be key competitive battlegrounds. The Delivery Hero acquisition by Uber (announced July 2026) would create a truly global delivery giant, reshaping the competitive landscape across both continents.
