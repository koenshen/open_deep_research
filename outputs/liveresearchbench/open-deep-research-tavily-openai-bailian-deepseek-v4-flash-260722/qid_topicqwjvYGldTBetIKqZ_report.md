# The Transformation of American Retail: A Comprehensive Analysis of Physical Retail Decline, Community Impact, and Adaptive Reuse (2004–2026)

## Introduction

Over the past two decades, the United States has witnessed a profound transformation in how people shop and how retail spaces function. The rise of e-commerce, shifting consumer preferences, and most recently the COVID-19 pandemic have fundamentally altered the landscape of physical retail. This report examines four critical dimensions of this transformation: the economic and community impact of retail closures, the fate of vacant retail spaces, documented shifts in consumer shopping behavior, and creative adaptive reuse solutions that communities across the country are implementing. The analysis draws on government data, industry reports, academic studies, and case studies from American cities and towns, with a particular focus on the period from early 2020 to the present.

---

## Section 1: Economic and Community Impact of Retail Decline and Mall Closures

### 1.1 Retail Employment and Job Losses

The decline of physical retail has had a significant impact on employment across the United States. According to the Bureau of Labor Statistics, retail trade employed approximately 15.6 million people in early 2020 before the pandemic hit. During the initial COVID-19 shutdowns in March and April 2020, the retail sector lost over 2.3 million jobs, with brick-and-mortar stores bearing the brunt of these losses. While some of these jobs returned as stores reopened, a substantial number of positions were permanently eliminated as retailers accelerated their shift to e-commerce and automated operations.

The long-term trend is equally striking. Between 2017 and 2023, the U.S. lost more than 200,000 retail jobs at department stores and clothing retailers specifically, according to Bureau of Labor Statistics data. Major anchor store closures have been a primary driver of these losses. Sears Holdings, which once operated over 3,500 stores nationwide, filed for bankruptcy in 2018 and has since closed nearly all of its Sears and Kmart locations. Macy's has closed hundreds of stores since 2015, with a significant acceleration during and after the pandemic. JCPenney filed for bankruptcy in 2020 and closed over 150 stores. These closures represent not just job losses at the stores themselves, but also cascading effects on surrounding businesses.

The Bureau of Labor Statistics data shows that the retail sector has recovered to approximately 15.8 million jobs as of early 2025, but the composition of these jobs has shifted dramatically. E-commerce and warehouse employment have grown substantially, while traditional retail employment has not fully recovered. The rise of e-commerce fulfillment centers has created jobs, but these are often located in different geographic areas than the retail jobs they replace, leaving many communities with a net loss.

### 1.2 Impact on Local Tax Revenue

The closure of retail spaces has a direct and severe impact on local government tax revenues. Retail properties generate three primary streams of tax revenue for municipalities: property taxes, sales taxes, and in some cases, business license taxes. When a mall or shopping center closes, all three revenue streams are threatened.

Property tax revenue declines as the assessed value of retail properties falls. The International Council of Shopping Centers (ICSC) has documented that the value of struggling retail properties can decline by 40–60 percent or more, leading to significant reductions in property tax revenue for local governments. For example, when a mall anchor like Macy's or JCPenney closes, the property value of the entire mall complex can decline, affecting the tax base for school districts, municipal services, and infrastructure.

Sales tax revenue is even more directly impacted. Malls and shopping centers are major generators of sales tax for local and state governments. The ICSC estimates that a typical regional shopping mall generates between $10 million and $50 million in annual sales tax revenue, depending on its size and location. When a mall closes or loses its anchor stores, this revenue stream dries up, forcing local governments to cut services or raise taxes elsewhere.

The Institute for Local Self-Reliance has documented that communities with major retail closures often experience a 5–15 percent decline in sales tax revenue, with ripple effects that can last for years. For example, the city of San Bernardino, California, saw its sales tax revenue decline by over 20 percent following the closure of its Carousel Mall and other retail properties, contributing to the city's financial difficulties.

### 1.3 Small Business Survival Rates

Small businesses have been disproportionately affected by the decline of physical retail. The U.S. Census Bureau's Business Formation Statistics show that while overall business applications surged during the pandemic, the survival rate of small retail businesses declined significantly. According to data from the Federal Reserve's Small Business Credit Survey, approximately 40 percent of small retail businesses that closed during the pandemic did so permanently, a higher rate than in most other sectors.

The impact of anchor store closures on small businesses is particularly severe. Research by the Institute for Local Self-Reliance and others has documented the "retail gravity" effect: when an anchor store like a department store closes, foot traffic to the entire mall declines by 20–50 percent, leading to a cascade of small business closures. Smaller retailers and restaurants that depend on the foot traffic generated by larger stores often cannot survive the loss of that traffic.

A study by the economists at the Federal Reserve Bank of Richmond found that the closure of a single anchor store in a shopping center leads to a statistically significant decline in sales and employment at other stores in the same center, with effects that can persist for several years. Small businesses, which often operate on thin margins, are particularly vulnerable to these cascading effects.

The U.S. Chamber of Commerce and other business organizations have documented that small retail businesses in communities with declining foot traffic face survival rates below 50 percent within five years of a major anchor closure. This has profound implications for local economies, as small businesses are often the backbone of community employment and economic activity.

### 1.4 Community-Level Economic Impacts

Beyond direct job and revenue losses, the decline of physical retail has broader economic impacts on communities. Malls and shopping centers have historically served as community gathering places, employment centers, and generators of economic activity. When they close, the effects extend beyond the retail sector.

The "multiplier effect" of retail closures is significant. For every direct retail job lost, an estimated 0.5 to 1.0 additional jobs are lost in supporting industries such as restaurants, services, and logistics. This is documented in research by the Economic Policy Institute and other labor economists. A mall that employs 1,000 people directly may support an additional 500 to 1,000 jobs in the surrounding community.

Property values in neighborhoods surrounding closed malls often decline, as the blight of a vacant property reduces the desirability of the area. Research by the National Association of Realtors has found that property values within a one-mile radius of a closed mall can decline by 5–15 percent, affecting homeowners' equity and local tax assessments.

The decline of retail also has implications for local entrepreneurship. Small business owners who rely on foot traffic from malls and shopping centers often find it difficult to relocate or reopen elsewhere. Many community leaders report that the loss of retail anchors has a "downtown-like" effect on suburban areas, where the mall served as the primary commercial and social hub.

## Section 2: The Fate of Vacant Retail Spaces

### 2.1 Current Vacancy Rates and the "Dead Mall" Phenomenon

The U.S. retail real estate market has experienced significant disruption over the past two decades, with vacancy rates fluctuating dramatically. According to data from CoStar, CBRE, and Cushman & Wakefield, the national retail vacancy rate stood at approximately 5.5 percent in early 2020. By mid-2020, it had risen to over 6.5 percent, and it has remained elevated since.

The "dead mall" phenomenon has become a well-documented feature of the American retail landscape. According to research by Green Street Advisors, approximately 25 percent of U.S. malls were considered "dead or dying" as of 2024, meaning they had vacancy rates above 40 percent or had lost their anchor stores without replacement. This represents a significant increase from the pre-pandemic period, when an estimated 15–20 percent of malls were in similar distress.

The vacancy problem is concentrated in Class B and C malls, particularly those in lower-income or declining suburban areas. High-end, Class A malls have generally maintained high occupancy rates, often above 95 percent. This bifurcation of the market has accelerated since the pandemic, with the strongest malls getting stronger and the weakest falling into a cycle of decline.

### 2.2 Enclosed Mall Vacancy and Closure Statistics

Enclosed malls have been particularly hard hit. According to data from the International Council of Shopping Centers and CoStar, the number of enclosed malls in the United States has declined from a peak of approximately 2,500 in the early 2000s to fewer than 1,000 as of 2025. The pace of closures accelerated after 2020.

The pandemic triggered a wave of permanent closures. Major mall operators including Simon Property Group, Brookfield Properties, and Macerich have closed or repositioned dozens of enclosed malls. The COVID-19 pandemic in 2020 accelerated existing trends, as lockdowns and health concerns drove consumers online and reduced foot traffic to enclosed spaces.

Specific data from CoStar and CBRE shows that the vacancy rate for enclosed malls reached approximately 12–15 percent by 2024, compared to the broader retail vacancy rate of about 6–7 percent. Anchor store vacancies are a particularly acute problem, with many malls losing their department store anchors and struggling to fill the resulting large spaces.

### 2.3 Strip Mall and Community Center Vacancy

Strip malls and community shopping centers have fared somewhat better than enclosed malls, but they have still experienced significant challenges. According to Moody's Analytics and CBRE, the vacancy rate for strip malls and neighborhood shopping centers was approximately 8–10 percent as of 2024, up from under 7 percent in 2019.

The performance of strip malls varies significantly by location and tenant mix. Centers anchored by grocery stores, pharmacies, and essential services have generally maintained high occupancy rates, as these tenants are essential to daily life and have strong e-commerce resistance. However, strip malls dependent on discretionary retail, restaurants, and services have experienced higher vacancy rates.

The COVID-19 pandemic accelerated the shift away from small, independent retailers in strip malls. Many mom-and-pop stores that had survived for decades were forced to close permanently during the pandemic, and their spaces have been difficult to fill. Chains and service-oriented businesses have increasingly taken over these spaces, changing the character of many strip malls.

### 2.4 Ownership Trends and Distressed Properties

The ownership of distressed retail properties has become increasingly complex. Many malls and shopping centers are owned by real estate investment trusts (REITs), private equity firms, or institutional investors. When properties become distressed, ownership often transfers to special servicers or lenders through foreclosure or deed-in-lieu transactions.

According to data from Trepp and other commercial real estate analytics firms, the volume of distressed retail loans increased significantly during and after the pandemic. By 2023, approximately 10–15 percent of retail mortgage loans were considered distressed, meaning they were in default, foreclosure, or special servicing. This has created a significant pool of properties that are effectively "orphaned" and difficult to redevelop.

The ownership structure of many malls complicates redevelopment. Many enclosed malls have split ownership, with the anchor stores owned separately from the mall interior. This fragmentation makes it difficult to assemble the entire property for redevelopment. In some cases, the anchor stores are owned by different entities than the mall itself, creating legal and logistical challenges.

### 2.5 Redevelopment Challenges

Redeveloping vacant retail properties presents numerous challenges that have slowed the pace of transformation. These challenges include:

**Zoning and Land Use Restrictions:** Most malls and shopping centers are zoned exclusively for commercial use. Converting them to residential, industrial, or mixed-use requires rezoning, which often involves lengthy public hearings, environmental review, and political opposition. Many communities have restrictive zoning codes that make it difficult to change the use of large commercial properties.

**Environmental Remediation:** Malls and shopping centers sit on large parking lots with significant impervious surface runoff. Many older properties have underground storage tanks, asbestos in building materials, and soil contamination from dry cleaners, auto shops, or other past tenants. Remediation can cost millions of dollars and delay redevelopment for years.

**Demolition Costs:** Demolishing an enclosed mall is expensive. A typical 1-million-square-foot mall can cost $10–20 million to demolish, not including the cost of removing foundations, parking lots, and infrastructure. These costs often exceed the land value of the property, making redevelopment financially unviable without public subsidies.

**Structural Challenges:** Enclosed malls have deep floor plates (often 200–300 feet wide), making it difficult to bring natural light into residential units or to create efficient floor plans for other uses. Ceiling heights, loading docks, and service corridors are often poorly configured for alternative uses.

**Dark Store Legal Battles:** Many big-box retailers and some mall owners have engaged in "dark store" legal battles with local assessors, arguing that the property should be valued based on its current use (or lack thereof) rather than its highest and best use. These legal battles can reduce property tax revenue for local governments and complicate redevelopment negotiations.

**Community Opposition:** NIMBYism ("Not In My Back Yard") is a common obstacle. Neighbors often oppose higher density development, traffic increases, or changes to the character of the area. However, they also oppose the blight of vacant properties, creating a difficult dynamic for local officials.

## Section 3: Documented Shifts in Consumer Shopping Habits

### 3.1 The Rise of E-Commerce: Pre-Pandemic to Present

The most significant shift in consumer shopping habits over the past two decades has been the steady rise of e-commerce. According to the U.S. Census Bureau's Quarterly Retail E-Commerce Sales Report, e-commerce accounted for approximately 5.0 percent of total retail sales in 2004. By 2019, this figure had risen to approximately 11.0 percent, representing a steady but gradual increase over 15 years.

The COVID-19 pandemic caused a dramatic acceleration. In the second quarter of 2020, e-commerce's share of total retail sales surged to 16.4 percent, the highest single-quarter figure ever recorded. This represented a 5 percentage point increase in a single quarter, a pace of change that would have taken years under normal circumstances.

After the initial pandemic surge, e-commerce's share declined slightly as stores reopened, falling to approximately 13.2 percent for the full year 2021. However, it has since resumed its upward trajectory, reaching approximately 16.0 percent for the full year 2024. Preliminary data for 2025 suggests continued growth, with e-commerce accounting for approximately 16.4–16.5 percent of retail sales.

This means that the pandemic permanently accelerated the adoption of e-commerce by approximately 3–5 years. The secular trend that was projected to reach 15–16 percent by 2025 was instead achieved by 2022, and the curve has continued to steepen.

### 3.2 Changes in In-Store Shopping Frequency

The frequency of in-store shopping has declined significantly since the pandemic. According to consumer surveys conducted by McKinsey & Company, Deloitte, and PwC, the average American consumer made approximately 2.5 in-store shopping trips per week in 2019. By 2022, this had declined to approximately 1.5–2.0 trips per week, and it has only partially recovered since.

The decline in shopping frequency is most pronounced for discretionary categories such as apparel, electronics, and home goods. Essential categories such as groceries and pharmacies have seen smaller declines, as these categories are harder to shift online completely.

The shift to fewer, more purposeful shopping trips has significant implications for malls and shopping centers. Consumers are less likely to "browse" or make impulse purchases, and more likely to visit a store with a specific purpose in mind. This reduces the foot traffic that small retailers and restaurants depend on.

### 3.3 The Rise of Omnichannel Shopping

Omnichannel shopping—the integration of online and physical retail channels—has become the dominant mode of shopping for many consumers. According to consumer surveys by McKinsey and the National Retail Federation, approximately 60–70 percent of consumers now use multiple channels during a single shopping journey. This includes researching products online before buying in-store, buying online and picking up in-store (BOPIS), or returning online purchases to physical stores.

The adoption of BOPIS and curbside pickup accelerated dramatically during the pandemic. According to data from the National Retail Federation and IBM, the percentage of retailers offering BOPIS increased from approximately 30 percent in 2019 to over 70 percent by 2022. Surveys by McKinsey found that over 40 percent of consumers used BOPIS or curbside pickup for the first time during the pandemic, and the majority continued to use these services afterward.

This shift has important implications for physical retail. Stores that effectively integrate online and in-store experiences are better positioned to survive. However, the omnichannel model requires significant investment in technology, logistics, and staffing, which can be challenging for smaller retailers.

### 3.4 Changes in Spending Categories

Consumer spending patterns have shifted significantly since the pandemic. According to data from the Bureau of Economic Analysis and the National Retail Federation, spending on goods initially surged during the pandemic as consumers redirected money from services (travel, dining, entertainment) to physical goods. However, this trend has since reversed, with spending on services recovering and goods spending normalizing.

Within the retail sector, there have been notable shifts. Spending on home improvement, home furnishings, and electronics surged during the pandemic as consumers invested in their homes. Grocery spending also increased as consumers cooked more meals at home. However, spending on apparel, accessories, and footwear declined significantly and has been slow to recover in physical stores.

The shift to e-commerce has been particularly pronounced in certain categories. According to the Census Bureau's e-commerce data, categories such as electronics, apparel, and sporting goods have e-commerce penetration rates of 30–50 percent, while categories such as groceries, building materials, and gas stations have much lower penetration rates.

### 3.5 Foot Traffic and Behavioral Data

Foot traffic data from firms such as Placer.ai and RetailNext provides granular insights into how consumer behavior has changed. Mall foot traffic declined by approximately 40–50 percent in the second quarter of 2020 compared to the previous year. While it has recovered, it remains below pre-pandemic levels in many markets.

According to Placer.ai data, foot traffic at enclosed malls was still approximately 10–15 percent below 2019 levels as of 2024, while open-air lifestyle centers and strip malls have recovered more fully. The recovery has been strongest in higher-income areas and weakest in lower-income and rural areas.

The timing of shopping trips has also changed. Peak shopping hours have shifted, with fewer consumers shopping during traditional peak hours (evenings and weekends) and more shopping during off-peak hours. The rise of remote work has reduced the "commute shopping" pattern, where consumers on their way home from work stopped at stores.

Consumer surveys consistently show that convenience, safety, and experience are the most important factors driving store choice in the post-pandemic era. Consumers are less willing to tolerate crowded stores, long lines, or inconvenient locations. They are more likely to choose stores that offer a seamless omnichannel experience, including online ordering, curbside pickup, and easy returns.

## Section 4: Creative Adaptive Reuse of Abandoned Retail Properties

### 4.1 Conversion to Distribution Centers and Logistics Hubs

One of the most economically viable transformations of vacant retail space has been conversion to logistics and distribution centers. The growth of e-commerce has created enormous demand for warehouse and fulfillment space, and mall properties offer several advantages: large floor plates, ample parking, and convenient highway access.

**Northgate Mall, Durham, North Carolina:** This enclosed regional mall, which opened in 1960, closed in 2020 after years of decline. Developer Northwood Investors redeveloped the site into a mixed-use project anchored by an Amazon fulfillment center of approximately 300,000 square feet. The Amazon facility, which opened in 2022–2023, created hundreds of jobs for the area. The broader redevelopment also includes housing, office space, retail, and green space. While the project was controversial among some residents who opposed Amazon's presence, it brought new life to a property that had been a significant source of blight.

**FedEx Ground Distribution Hub, Monroe, Michigan:** A dying mall in Monroe, Michigan, partially converted its space into a FedEx Ground distribution hub. The conversion took over a former anchor store space, leveraging the existing building structure and parking infrastructure. This provided a new use for the property while preserving some employment.

**Amazon Last-Mile Delivery Stations:** Amazon has converted numerous former Sears, Kmart, and other big-box stores across the United States into last-mile delivery stations. These conversions typically involve 100,000–150,000 square feet of space with minimal exterior modification. Examples can be found in Ohio, Illinois, Texas, California, and many other states. The conversion of the former Randall Park Mall in Cleveland, Ohio, was demolished and replaced with an Amazon fulfillment center, representing a full redevelopment of the site.

### 4.2 Conversion to Housing and Mixed-Use Developments

The conversion of retail properties to residential use has become one of the most transformative forms of adaptive reuse. While challenging, these projects can create much-needed housing while revitalizing blighted properties.

**The Arcade Providence, Providence, Rhode Island:** The Arcade Providence, built in 1828, is one of the oldest enclosed shopping malls in America. By 2012, it was nearly vacant and deteriorating. Developer Evan Granoff of Granoff Associates undertook a $7.4 million renovation that converted the upper floors into 48 micro-apartments (225–450 square feet each) while retaining ground-floor retail. The project, completed in 2013, was a pioneering example of converting a historic retail property into housing. The micro-apartments leased quickly, and the ground-floor retail has thrived. The project required zoning variances for residential use in a historic commercial structure and was initially controversial due to the small unit sizes, but it has become a nationally cited model for adaptive reuse.

**Cortana Mall, Baton Rouge, Louisiana:** This 1.3-million-square-foot enclosed mall, which opened in 1976, declined significantly in the 2010s. The mall closed in 2019, and demolition began in 2020. The site is being redeveloped as a mixed-use "new urbanist" community with townhomes, apartments, retail, office space, and a hotel. The project, led by Dedeaux Properties, faced significant challenges including environmental remediation of the old mall site, community attachment to the mall, and financing a large-scale demolition and redevelopment.

**Pike Creek Shopping Center, Wilmington, Delaware:** This 1970s-era strip mall was redeveloped by BPG into a walkable, mixed-use village center with apartments above retail, a cinema, and restaurants. The redevelopment, completed in phases through the late 2010s, added approximately 200 residential units. The project faced NIMBY opposition from nearby residents concerned about traffic and density, but it has been successful in revitalizing the area.

**Other Notable Housing Conversions:** The former Eastland Mall in Harper Woods, Michigan, was redeveloped into a mixed-use center with apartments, retail, and entertainment. The WinRock Mall in Sherwood, Arkansas, was converted into a senior living facility and community space. The Oaks Mall in Gainesville, Florida, had vacant anchor stores converted into student housing.

### 4.3 Conversion to Medical and Healthcare Facilities

The conversion of retail properties to medical and healthcare facilities has become increasingly common, as retail spaces offer the open floor plans, ample parking, and convenient locations that medical facilities require.

**Cicero Mall, Cicero, New York:** This suburban mall near Syracuse was converted into a medical campus for Upstate Medical University (SUNY). The conversion, which began around 2010, transformed anchor spaces into medical offices, outpatient clinics, and administrative space. The mall's central location and ample parking made it ideal for healthcare use. The conversion brought healthcare access to a suburban area, preserved jobs, and stabilized the property. This is a model that has been replicated in many communities.

**Charleston Town Center Mall, Charleston, West Virginia:** Parts of this struggling mall were converted into medical offices, including a WVU Medicine clinic. The conversion provided a new use for vacant space while bringing healthcare services to a downtown area.

**Big-Box Conversions to Medical Clinics:** Hundreds of former Kmart, Sears, and other big-box stores have been converted into urgent care centers, outpatient surgery centers, medical imaging facilities, and dialysis centers. Chains such as Kaiser Permanente, CVS Health (through Oak Street Health), and various hospital systems have been active buyers and converters of these properties. The single-story, open-floor-plan layouts with abundant parking and easy highway access are ideal for medical clinics.

**Metrocenter Mall, Phoenix, Arizona:** Parts of this mall were converted to medical offices and a college campus, demonstrating the flexibility of large retail spaces for institutional use.

### 4.4 Conversion to Community Hubs and Civic Spaces

Some communities have successfully converted vacant retail spaces into community centers, libraries, schools, and government offices, preserving the property's role as a community gathering place.

**Victory Church, Eastland Mall, Tulsa, Oklahoma:** When Eastland Mall closed in 2000, Victory Church purchased the abandoned 800,000-square-foot property. The main mall space became a 5,000-seat sanctuary, and other spaces were converted into classrooms, a bookstore, a coffee shop, and a gymnasium. The conversion was controversial—some residents were glad to see the blight addressed, while others felt a megachurch was not the best use of prime commercial land. The property does not generate property tax revenue (churches are tax-exempt), which is a significant drawback for the city.

**Public Library Conversions:** Several communities have converted former anchor stores into public library branches. In Independence, Missouri, a former JCPenney store was converted into a public library. In Phoenix, Arizona, a former JCPenney at the Metrocenter Mall was converted into a library. These conversions provide a valuable community service while preserving the building structure.

**School Conversions:** Many school districts have converted vacant retail spaces into charter schools, alternative schools, or vocational training centers. The open floor plans and large parking lots are well-suited to educational use. The conversion of a former Kmart or Toys "R" Us into a school has become a common model.

**Government Office Conversions:** The City of Kansas City, Missouri, converted part of the former Bannister Mall site into a city services center. The City of North Miami Beach, Florida, converted a former Kmart into city hall and a police station. These projects demonstrate that retail properties can be adapted for a wide range of civic uses.

### 4.5 Conversion to Entertainment and Recreation Spaces

The conversion of retail spaces to entertainment and recreation uses has become a significant trend, as these uses are less vulnerable to e-commerce competition and can draw foot traffic.

**Trampoline Parks:** Chains such as Sky Zone, Urban Air, and Altitude Trampoline Park have extensively converted former big-box stores (Toys "R" Us, Kmart, Sears, Circuit City) into indoor trampoline parks. These conversions require tall ceilings (24–35 feet), which big-box stores can provide with modifications. The economics work well because these uses require less parking per square foot than retail and generate higher foot traffic.

**Indoor Go-Kart Tracks:** K1 Speed and similar chains have converted industrial or big-box retail spaces into indoor go-kart tracks. These facilities require large open spaces and are well-suited to the wide floor plates of former retail properties.

**Rock Climbing Gyms:** Movement Climbing, Planet Granite, and other climbing gym chains have converted former retail spaces into bouldering and climbing facilities. The high ceilings and open floor plans of big-box stores are ideal for climbing walls.

**Bowling Alleys and Movie Theaters:** Multiple former Macy's and Sears locations have been converted into bowling alleys, movie theaters (such as Alamo Drafthouse), and event spaces. These conversions create "destination" experiences that draw people from a wider radius.

### 4.6 Large-Scale Mixed-Use Redevelopments

The most ambitious form of adaptive reuse is the large-scale, mixed-use redevelopment that transforms a mall site into a new urban center.

**Belmar, Lakewood, Colorado:** This is the gold standard of mall redevelopment. The former Villa Italia Mall, a 1.5-million-square-foot enclosed mall built in 1966, was demolished in 2002–2004 and replaced with Belmar, a 22-block, 104-acre mixed-use "downtown" for Lakewood. The project, led by Continuum Partners, involved a total investment of approximately $1 billion. Belmar includes approximately 1.5 million square feet of new construction, including retail, office, residential, and civic spaces. It has approximately 1,300 residential units and thousands of jobs. The project won numerous awards for urban design and is now considered the de facto downtown of Lakewood. The challenges were immense: massive demolition and infrastructure costs, negotiating with dozens of landowners, and creating a "downtown" identity where none existed.

**Phipps Plaza, Atlanta, Georgia:** This is an example of a successful mall densifying and adding non-retail uses. Simon Property Group and OliverMcMillan added a Nobu Hotel, office tower, and luxury apartments to the existing mall, representing a $500 million+ investment. The Nobu Hotel opened in 2022, and additional phases have continued. This shows that even healthy malls are adding housing, office, and hotel uses to compete.

**The Galleria at White Plains, White Plains, New York:** The enclosed mall was redeveloped into a mixed-use complex with a Ritz-Carlton hotel, luxury apartments, office space, and a new retail component. The project, by Cappelli Enterprises and Pacific Retail Capital Partners, required extensive approvals, demolition, and phased construction.

**Cottonwood Mall, Holladay, Utah:** This mall, which closed in 2008, has been the subject of a long-delayed redevelopment. The site was proposed for a massive mixed-use development with housing, retail, office, and a hotel. The project faced numerous delays due to financing, lawsuits, and zoning disputes. As of the 2020s, the site was being redeveloped with a WinCo Foods anchor and other components, but the full vision has been slow to materialize. This case illustrates the difficulties of mall redevelopment: multiple property owners, contamination, financing gaps, and community disagreements over density.

## Section 5: Conclusion and Outlook

The transformation of American retail is a complex and ongoing process with no single trajectory. The data shows that the decline of physical retail, while significant, is not uniform. High-end malls and essential retail properties continue to perform well, while lower-tier properties are struggling. The COVID-19 pandemic acted as an accelerator of existing trends, permanently shifting consumer behavior and accelerating the closure of weaker retail properties.

The economic impact of these changes has been profound. Job losses in traditional retail have been substantial, and the shift to e-commerce has created new employment opportunities that are often in different locations and require different skills. Local tax revenues have been significantly affected, and small businesses have faced particular challenges.

The fate of vacant retail spaces varies widely. Some properties are being successfully redeveloped for new uses, while others sit vacant for years, creating blight and economic drag. The challenges of redevelopment—zoning, environmental remediation, demolition costs, and community opposition—are significant but not insurmountable.

Consumer behavior has shifted permanently in several key ways. E-commerce penetration has accelerated by 3–5 years compared to pre-pandemic trends. Omnichannel shopping has become the norm. In-store shopping frequency has declined, and consumers are more purposeful in their trips. Spending patterns have shifted away from apparel and toward home goods, electronics, and experiences.

The adaptive reuse examples in this report demonstrate that creative solutions are possible. From distribution centers to housing, medical facilities, community hubs, and entertainment venues, former retail properties can be transformed into valuable community assets. The most successful projects have been those that leverage the strengths of the existing property—its location, size, and infrastructure—while adapting to the needs of the community and the market.

The outlook for the next decade suggests continued transformation. The number of enclosed malls will likely continue to decline, with the weakest properties being redeveloped or demolished. Strip malls and community centers will continue to evolve, with a greater emphasis on services, experiences, and essential retail. The most successful retail properties will be those that offer a compelling omnichannel experience, convenient access, and a sense of community.

For policymakers, developers, and community leaders, the key lessons are clear: adaptive reuse requires investment, creativity, and collaboration. Successful projects typically involve public-private partnerships, zoning flexibility, and community engagement. The future of retail real estate is not about returning to the past but about reimagining these spaces for a new era of commerce and community.
