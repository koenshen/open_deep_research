# Performance-Enhancing Drugs and Technologies in Elite Sports: A Comprehensive Global Analysis

## Introduction

The debate over whether performance-enhancing drugs (PEDs) and technologies should be permitted in elite sports represents one of the most contentious and consequential issues in contemporary athletics. From the ancient Olympic athletes who consumed animal hearts and hallucinogenic mushrooms to modern controversies involving gene doping, advanced prosthetics, and state-sponsored doping programs, the question of what constitutes legitimate enhancement has persisted for millennia [Britannica ProCon.org](https://www.britannica.com/procon/sports-and-drugs-debate). The World Anti-Doping Agency (WADA), established in 1999 following the Festina cycling scandal, currently oversees a global anti-doping regime that tests hundreds of thousands of athletes annually, with 271,775 samples collected in 2023 alone [WADA 2023 ADRV Report](https://www.wada-ama.org/en/news/wada-publishes-2023-anti-doping-rule-violations-report). Despite this expansive enforcement apparatus, academic research estimates that 14-57% of elite athletes may be doping, while WADA's official positive test rate remains at 1-2% annually [PMC Risk Environments](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7402240). This profound disconnect between official statistics and actual prevalence underscores the complexity of the issue and the limitations of the current prohibition model.

This report presents a comprehensive analysis of whether PEDs and performance-enhancing technologies should be allowed in elite sports, examining the question through ethical, medical, legal, competitive fairness, and cultural dimensions. The analysis draws on a wide range of sources including WADA reports, IOC policies, peer-reviewed research, notable case studies (Lance Armstrong, Maria Sharapova, Ben Johnson, Oscar Pistorius, the East German doping program), expert testimony, government reports, and news media coverage. The report presents six arguments in favor of allowing PEDs and six arguments against, each supported by substantial evidence and rigorous analysis.

## Part I: Arguments for Allowing Performance-Enhancing Drugs and Technologies

### Argument 1: Athlete Autonomy and Bodily Freedom

**Thesis Statement:** Elite athletes are autonomous adults who should have the right to make informed decisions about what substances and technologies they use to enhance their own bodies, and prohibiting PEDs represents an unjustified, paternalistic restriction on personal liberty that is inconsistent with the values of free societies.

**Supporting Points and Evidence:**

**1.1 The Right to Bodily Autonomy Under International Human Rights Law**

The Enhanced Games, launched in Las Vegas on May 24, 2026, is a privately funded multi-sport event where athletes are permitted to use performance-enhancing substances that are legal under FDA rules, offering winners up to $250,000 with a $1 million bonus for world records. According to a legal analysis published on Verfassungsblog (July 30, 2026), International Human Rights Law does not necessarily prohibit such competitions. The key legal tension identified is between athletes' right to bodily autonomy and the State's obligation to protect citizens' health. The analysis notes that "the idea that athletes should compete substance-free is a 20th-century invention, not a timeless moral principle" (from the Enhanced Games vision statement). Furthermore, while international instruments (UNESCO Convention, Council of Europe Anti-Doping Convention, WADA Code) emphasize doping's health risks, States are not required to prohibit such events outright. The analysis draws from General Comment 14 on the right to health to propose three principles for state decision-making: decisions should be (1) evidence-based, assessing health impacts on athletes and the public; (2) subject to ongoing review and monitoring; and (3) adopted through transparent, participatory processes involving stakeholders [Verfassungsblog](https://verfassungsblog.de/the-enhanced-games-from-a-human-rights-perspective).

**1.2 The Prohibition Model and Its Paternalism**

The Britannica "Sports and Drugs" pro-con analysis presents the argument that "PEDs should be a personal choice for adults; legalization could lead to safer, regulated drugs and reduce stigma around medical treatments." The article traces PED use back to ancient Olympic athletes who consumed animal hearts, hallucinogenic mushrooms, and other substances, noting that ancient Olympic champions were professionals who competed for huge cash prizes and "most forms of what we would call cheating were perfectly acceptable to them, save for game-fixing" (Sally Jenkins) [Britannica ProCon.org](https://www.britannica.com/procon/sports-and-drugs-debate).

**1.3 WADA's Criteria Are Problematic**

The Southern California Interdisciplinary Law Journal analysis of WADA's policies argues that WADA's policies are overly broad, using vague justifications of "fairness," "health," and "spirit of sport" to justify an exhaustive list of banned substances and strict sanctions that often disregard actual performance enhancement or intent. The article critiques the three criteria for banning substances (potential to enhance performance, contrary to spirit of sport, health risk), noting that an athlete can be sanctioned for a non-performance-enhancing substance like marijuana. The article recommends narrowing WADA's goals and improving proportionality between sanctions and offenses. It also notes: "It has been explicitly held that American athletes have limited recourse to federal law, as courts lack jurisdiction over sports federation contracts and arbitration decisions, and will intervene in only the most extraordinary circumstances" [USC Gould School of Law](https://gould.usc.edu/why/students/orgs/ilj/assets/docs/19-3%20Hard.pdf).

**1.4 The Pistorius Precedent: Autonomy and the Right to Compete**

Oscar Pistorius, the South African double amputee sprinter, faced a high-profile dispute with the IAAF over whether his carbon-fiber prosthetic blades (Flex-Foot Cheetah) gave him an unfair advantage. He was initially banned by the IAAF from competing against able-bodied runners but successfully overturned the ban in 2008 at the Court of Arbitration for Sport (CAS) — case number CAS 2008/A/1480, decided on May 16, 2008 [CAS](https://www.tas-cas.org). Key evidence from the CAS case: Dr. Robert Gailey (University of Miami) argued Pistorius was at a disadvantage because human feet provide ~250% energy return while his blades provide only ~80%. "You only get 80 percent energy return," Gailey said. "We knew that the human foot, because it has muscles, gives you about 250% energy return, and your calves are a major energy generator. He was at a disadvantage from the beginning." Pistorius himself questioned why other Paralympic athletes weren't breaking world records if the blades were so advantageous. At the 2012 Summer Olympics, Pistorius became the first double-leg amputee participant. The article notes: "Oscar has broken the glass ceiling for athletes with disabilities in the respect that kids today are going to be able to see Oscar competing, and hopefully they'll be children at home and young adults saying, 'Hey listen, I've lost a limb and if Oscar can do this, maybe I can compete as well'" (Gailey) [WBUR](https://www.wbur.org/onlyagame/2012/08/04/pistorius).

**1.5 The Privacy and Autonomy Costs of Anti-Doping**

The philosophical analysis by Sarah Teetzel, published in Frontiers in Sports and Active Living (2022), examines the ethical implications of applying standard anti-doping sanctions to young athletes (ages 15-17). The paper argues that age should matter more in doping cases than it currently does. Key cases discussed include: the 2010 Youth Olympic Games where two 17-year-old wrestlers tested positive and received 2-year suspensions with public naming despite being minors; 16-year-old Romanian gymnast Andreea Răducan losing her 2000 Olympic gold medal for cold medicine given by her team physician; 16-year-old swimmer Rick DeMont's 1972 disqualification for asthma medication; the 2022 Kamila Valieva case (15-year-old figure skater); and 12-year-old Polish driver Igor Walilko receiving an 18-month ban for a banned substance from an energy bar. The paper argues that drug testing is inherently an invasion of privacy, citing the Privacy Commissioner of Canada (1990): "The principal privacy issue flowing from drug testing is not whether testing is intrusive. It is. Urinalysis is particularly intrusive... The principal issue is in what circumstances the intrusions occasioned by testing are justified" [PMC Philosophical Perspectives](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8971751).

**Analysis:** The autonomy argument rests on a fundamental principle of liberal democracies: that competent adults should be permitted to make decisions about their own bodies, even if those decisions involve risk. The current anti-doping regime imposes significant restrictions on athlete liberty — including mandatory urine testing under observation, whereabouts reporting requirements, and severe sanctions for violations — that would be difficult to justify in other contexts. Critics of the autonomy position argue that sport is a social institution with its own rules, and participation is voluntary, but this overlooks the reality that for many athletes, elite sport is not a freely chosen hobby but their primary livelihood. The Pistorius case demonstrates that the line between permissible and impermissible enhancement is not fixed by nature but is instead determined by social negotiation and scientific evidence. The Enhanced Games, while controversial, represents a real-world experiment in an alternative model that respects athlete autonomy while maintaining competitive structure.

### Argument 2: Harm Reduction — Medically Supervised Doping Is Safer Than Prohibition

**Thesis Statement:** The current prohibition model forces doping underground, creating far greater risks to athlete health than a regulated, medically supervised system would, and a harm reduction approach analogous to successful drug policy reforms would substantially reduce the physical harms, psychological damage, and exploitation currently inherent in the system.

**Supporting Points and Evidence:**

**2.1 The Prohibition Model Creates a Risk Environment**

The academic article published in PMC (May 2021) examines doping in sport through the lens of risk and enabling environment frameworks, drawing parallels between sport doping and non-sport recreational drug use. The authors argue that anti-doping policies create risk environments that drive doping underground, increasing harms to athletes. Key findings: Anti-doping, led by WADA, takes a zero-tolerance, detect-and-punish approach similar to the "war on drugs." WADA consistently returns a positive test rate of 1-2% per year, while researchers using more sophisticated survey and modeling methods have estimated much higher rates, varying between 14% and 57%. The authors apply Rhodes' risk environment framework (physical, social, economic, policy factors at micro and macro levels) to sport, showing how anti-doping policies paradoxically increase risks for athletes by forcing practices underground, making safe supply difficult, and creating detection avoidance pressures. Systematic doping cases (e.g., Lance Armstrong's US Postal team, Russia's state-sponsored system) are analyzed as enabling environments that reduced certain harms — providing medical supervision, secure supply, and detection avoidance through organized efforts like micro-dosing, omerta (code of silence), and centralized distribution. The authors argue that athletes suffered most when these systems were exposed, facing bans, reputational damage, and loss of livelihood. The article contrasts sport's resistance to harm reduction with broader trends in recreational drug policy (e.g., heroin-assisted treatment, syringe exchange programs), suggesting that harm reduction approaches like medically supervised doping could reduce athlete health risks. "Though systematic doping is often done for collective performance enhancement, related concerns include avoiding detection and ensuring athletes remain healthy enough to compete. As such, systematic doping may also be a way of managing risks (e.g. safe supply, dosing oversight, side effect management) that would be greater if each athlete were to undertake doping individually." "We argue that risks within these systems (i.e. extortion, bullying) resulted from the broader prohibitive sport environment that forces doping underground and allows such abuses to occur" [PMC Risk Environments](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7402240).

**2.2 Athlete Interest in Medically Supervised Doping**

The research paper by Marie Overbye, published in the International Journal of Drug Policy (Volume 55, May 2018, Pages 14-30), investigated how elite athletes might react to medically supervised 'doping' if it were legalized. The study surveyed 775 Danish elite athletes (mean age 21.73, 60% male, 40% female) from 40 sports via a web-based questionnaire (51% response rate), presenting a scenario of legalized, medically supervised doping. Key findings: 58% of athletes reported interest in one or more of the 13 proposed substances/methods under medical supervision. Vitamin and mineral injections were most popular (39%), diuretics least (10%). 23% showed interest in EPO, anabolic-androgenic steroids (AAS), blood transfusions, and/or Growth Hormone if medically supervised. Male speed/power sports athletes of increasing age had the highest likelihood of AAS interest (41% at age 36); female motor-skill sports athletes had the lowest (<1% at age 16). 59% feared side-effects. This fear prevented 39% of all athletes from being interested in specific substances, while 18% declared interest despite fearing the side-effects. The conclusion suggests that legalizing certain doping substances under medical supervision would create other/new types of harms, and this "trade-off of harms and benefits" would be undesirable for most athletes' occupational health, working conditions, and well-being [ScienceDirect Overbye](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S095539591730381X).

**2.3 The "War on Doping" Critique and Lessons from Drug Policy**

The UK Government review of criminalisation of doping in sport (October 2017), commissioned by the Minister for Sport Tracey Crouch MP, assessed whether the UK's existing anti-doping framework remains robust. Key findings: No case for criminalisation: The review found no compelling case to criminalise the act of doping in sport in the UK. This was a unanimous view among all stakeholders interviewed, including WADA. The existing framework (Misuse of Drugs Act 1971, Medicines Act 1968) already captures trafficking and supply of banned substances, with penalties up to 14 years imprisonment. WADA data shows 1.5-2% of drug tests have adverse results. Academic research suggests this only catches "the dopey dopers," with some estimates suggesting 30-50% of elite athletes may have been involved in doping. "Criminalising the act of doping in sport in the UK would be disproportionate in the battle to keep sport clean." "None of those interviewed were in favour of criminalising doping in sport. This was a unanimous view." "Academic research, which was echoed in several of the interviews conducted, suggests, 'we only catch the dopey dopers.'" From a deterrence perspective, "the strongest deterrent for athletes and their support staff is being banned from their involvement in sport along with the inevitable loss of earnings" [UK Government Review](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/5a82b2ece5274a2e8ab58e27/Review_of_Criminalisation_of_Doping_in_Sport.doc).

**2.4 Harm Reduction Evidence from Broader Drug Policy**

The Reason Foundation policy brief advocates for integrating harm reduction into the U.S. federal response to the drug overdose crisis. Despite $2.7 trillion in public spending, the U.S. faces persistently high overdose death rates, with opioid overdoses projected to claim 543,000–842,000 lives between 2020 and 2032. Current enforcement-centered and abstinence-only approaches are seen as inadequate, with relapse rates of 40-60% and treatment dropout rates around 30%. Harm reduction includes proven tools such as naloxone distribution, syringe service programs (SSPs), fentanyl test strip access, and supervised consumption sites (SCSs). One illustrative example is Taiwan's 2005 needle exchange program, launched during a surge in HIV among intravenous drug users, which reduced new HIV infections by 90% within four years [Reason Foundation](https://reason.org/policy-brief/harm-reduction-an-evidence-based-approach-to-the-drug-war). The RecoveryAnswers.org resource documents that syringe access is known to reduce drug use, with participating individuals 5 times more likely to enter addiction treatment, and overall increasing rates of reducing or stopping intravenous drug usage. In 2017, the Insite supervised injection site in Vancouver recorded 175,464 visits (an average of 415 injection room visits per day) by 7,301 unique users; 2,151 overdoses occurred with no fatalities, due to intervention by medical staff [RecoveryAnswers](https://www.recoveryanswers.org/resource/drug-and-alcohol-harm-reduction).

**2.5 The Stanford Enterprise Liability Approach**

The Stanford Law School article by Katherine Wu, published May 22, 2026, analyzes the Enhanced Games and proposes shifting responsibility from individual athletes to enterprises (federations, promoters, teams, sponsors, parent companies). Drawing on the GDR's state-sponsored doping program and Russia's four-year ban, the article argues that punishing only athletes misidentifies the source of harm. The author proposes an enterprise liability-shifting framework: maintaining WADA's four-year ban for serious intentional doping while adding enterprise duties of disclosure, independent testing, medical monitoring, insurance, and compensation. The U.S. 9th Circuit's decision in *Hunt v. Zuffa* is cited as pointing toward this path by allowing legal theories tied to the promoter's failure to disclose an opponent's doping status. "Instead of asking only whether the athlete should be banned, regulators should ask what duties should fall on the league, promoter, federation, team, sponsor, or parent company that designs the competitive environment." "By shifting the legal focus from 'catching individual cheats' to 'regulating enterprise designs,' sports law can finally hold the true profit-makers accountable for the physiological risks they help create" [Stanford Law School](https://law.stanford.edu/2026/05/22/analyzing-doping-enhanced-sport-and-enterprise-responsibility).

**2.6 Stigma and Unintentional Doping**

The review article by Harry Grimes and Luke Thomas Joseph Cox, published in Performance Enhancement & Health (Volume 14, Issue 2, May 2026), critically examines how the World Anti-Doping Agency (WADA) frames PED use as a moral transgression and cheating, creating a "clean" vs. "dirty" athlete dichotomy. Drawing on Erving Goffman's (1963) sociological work on stigma, the paper argues that this moralized discourse leads to public shaming, exclusion, and reputational damage for athletes found to have violated anti-doping rules. The authors focus particularly on the harm caused to unintentional dopers — athletes whose infractions arise from contaminated supplements, mislabelled medications, or inadvertent exposure. These individuals face similar treatment as deliberate PED users, despite the lack of intent. The paper calls for greater awareness and reflection on doping stigma and the language used to discuss doping [ScienceDirect Grimes & Cox](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2211266925000957).

**Analysis:** The harm reduction argument draws on decades of evidence from public health approaches to drug policy. The core insight is that prohibition does not eliminate the behavior it seeks to suppress — it merely drives it underground, where it becomes more dangerous. The East German program, with its motto "You eat the pills, or you die," represents the extreme endpoint of unregulated, secret doping without athlete consent or medical oversight focused on athlete welfare [Ethics Unwrapped](https://ethicsunwrapped.utexas.edu/case-study/east-germanys-doping-machine). The current system, while well-intentioned, has created a situation where athletes who choose to dope must do so without medical supervision, using potentially contaminated black-market substances, and in secrecy that prevents monitoring of side effects. The Overbye study demonstrates that a substantial proportion of athletes would prefer a regulated system, and the Stanford enterprise liability proposal offers a concrete framework for implementing such a system. The primary objection to this argument is that it normalizes drug use and may increase participation rates, but the evidence from broader drug policy suggests that well-regulated systems can reduce overall harm even if use rates remain stable or increase modestly.

### Argument 3: Leveling the Playing Field — Ending Hypocrisy and Honesty

**Thesis Statement:** Since doping is already endemic and pervasive across elite sports — with the vast majority of top competitors in many sports engaging in it — the current prohibition regime does not create a "level playing field" but instead punishes honest athletes for being caught while rewarding those who successfully evade detection, and legalization would actually create a fairer, more transparent system.

**Supporting Points and Evidence:**

**3.1 The Pervasive Nature of Doping: Statistical Evidence**

The Britannica "Sports and Drugs" pro-con analysis presents the argument that "PED use is so prevalent that banning only disadvantages clean athletes (e.g., Russia doping while others stay clean)" [Britannica ProCon.org](https://www.britannica.com/procon/sports-and-drugs-debate). Julian Savulescu's Aeon essay provides key data points: Of 21 podium finishers in the Tour de France for the period 1999-2005, 20 are suspected or proven to have used illegal substances. For 1996-2010, 36 out of 45 podium finishers. 107 athletes tested positive ahead of London 2012, with many more later found doping [Aeon Savulescu](https://aeon.co/essays/why-we-should-legalise-most-doping-in-sport). The academic article on doping prevalence and attitudes towards doping in Dutch elite sports (published January 28, 2023 in the Scientific Journal of Sport and Performance) surveyed 272 elite athletes online using the randomized response method. Key findings: The estimated prevalence of doping use among Dutch elite athletes in the last 12 months was 12.5% (95% CI 3.0–24.7%). The prevalence of using non-prohibited medication without medical necessity was 15.4% (95% CI 7.1–23.7%). One in four athletes (23%) accepts the use of prescription medication without medical necessity to enhance performance [Scientific Journal of Sport and Performance](https://sjsp.aearedo.es/index.php/sjsp/article/view/doping-prevalence-attitudes-towards-doping-dutch-elite-sports). The Physiopedia article reports: "Current data reports 14-39% of athletes are utilizing PEDs with a high prevalence of AAS use" [Physiopedia](https://www.physio-pedia.com/Recreational_and_Performance_Enhancing_Drugs_in_Sport). The PMC article on risk environments (May 2021) notes: "WADA consistently returns a positive test rate of 1-2% per year — with exceptions for years in which retesting has occurred — while researchers using more sophisticated survey and modeling methods have estimated much higher rates, varying between 14 and 57%" [PMC Risk Environments](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7402240). The UK Government review (October 2017) notes: "WADA data shows 1.5-2% of drug tests have adverse results. Academic research suggests this only catches 'the dopey dopers', with some estimates suggesting 30-50% of elite athletes may have been involved in doping" [UK Government Review](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/5a82b2ece5274a2e8ab58e27/Review_of_Criminalisation_of_Doping_in_Sport.doc).

**3.2 Lance Armstrong's "Level Playing Field" Argument**

Lance Armstrong, who was stripped of his seven Tour de France titles and banned for life, consistently argued that he did not view his doping as cheating because he was simply leveling the playing field in a sport where doping was pervasive. As Armstrong told Oprah Winfrey: "The definition of 'cheat' is to gain an advantage on a rival or foe that they don't have. I didn't view it that way. I viewed it as a level playing field." During the seven years that Armstrong won the Tour de France (1999-2005), 20 out of the 21 top three finishers were also found to have doped at some point in their careers. In a later interview, Armstrong stated: "If I was racing in 2015, no I wouldn't do it again because I don't think you have to do it again. If you take me back to 1995, when it was completely and totally pervasive, I would probably do it again. People don't like to hear that" [Ethics Unwrapped Armstrong](https://ethicsunwrapped.utexas.edu/video/armstrongs-doping-downfall). In the $100 million lawsuit with the U.S. federal government, Armstrong was allowed to use the defense that "everyone was doping." U.S. District Judge Christopher Cooper ruled that "the endemic nature of performance-enhancing drug use in cycling creates a possible inference that the Government was aware of the widespread use of PEDs" and that USPS may not have cared about Armstrong's doping when making sponsorship decisions [GranFondoGuide](https://www.granfondoguide.com/Contents/Index/3389/lance-armstrong-allowed-to-use-everyone-was-doping-in-defence).

**3.3 The Mitchell Report: Pervasive Use in Baseball**

The Mitchell Report (formally titled "Report to the Commissioner of Baseball of an Independent Investigation into the Illegal Use of Steroids and Other Performance Enhancing Substances by Players in Major League Baseball") is a 409-page document released on December 13, 2007, resulting from former U.S. Senator George J. Mitchell's 20-month investigation. The report names 89 MLB players alleged to have used performance-enhancing drugs, including prominent figures like Roger Clemens, Andy Pettitte, Miguel Tejada, and Éric Gagné. The report noted that at least one player from each of the 30 Major League Baseball teams was involved. Mitchell reported that during the random testing in 2003, 5 to 7 percent of players tested positive for steroid use. According to the report, after mandatory random testing began in 2004, HGH became the substance of choice among players, as it was not then detectable in tests. The report was downloaded over 1,100,000 times [Wikipedia Mitchell Report](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mitchell_Report). The House Committee on Oversight and Government Reform hearing on January 15, 2008, noted: "The illegal use of steroids and performance-enhancing drugs was pervasive for more than a decade. Major League Baseball was slow and ineffective in responding to the scandal, and the use of Human Growth Hormone has been rising" (Chairman Waxman). The report also noted that "the medical director for Major League Baseball actually led a presentation in 1998 on the benefits that could be obtained from testosterone" [Congress.gov](https://www.congress.gov/event/110th-congress/house-event/LC7395/text).

**3.4 Ben Johnson's Treatment and the Selective Enforcement Problem**

The Globe and Mail article (excerpt from Mary Ormsby's book *World's Fastest Man: The Incredible Life of Ben Johnson*) examines the 1988 Seoul Olympics doping case against Canadian sprinter Ben Johnson. After winning the 100-metre gold medal, Johnson failed a drug test for an anabolic steroid and was stripped of his medal. The article argues that Johnson was denied due process, citing multiple irregularities: his drug test analysis contained unsigned handwritten alterations including a changed athlete-identification code; a second unofficial urine test was dropped on Canadian officials without challenge; and the IOC medical commission gave the benefit of the doubt to some athletes but not others. American forensic toxicologist Dr. David Black called the testing documentation "unacceptable." Canadian lawyer John Barnes noted that Johnson's hearing featured conflicts of interest, surprise evidence (an endocrine profile), and that the IOC officials acted as testers, prosecutors, and judges. The excerpt contrasts Johnson's treatment with that of other sprinters with doping histories who retained medals, including Carl Lewis, Linford Christie, Dennis Mitchell, and Justin Gatlin. Ben Johnson's aunt, Laura Case, said: "They sentenced him to life before they tried him" [The Globe and Mail](https://www.theglobeandmail.com/sports/article-trouble-in-the-lab-the-ben-johnson-doping-case-was-anything-but-open). The CBC News report from September 24, 2013, on the 25th anniversary of the scandal found that despite 25 years of testing, doping in sports has worsened due to higher financial stakes and more sophisticated drugs. While tens of thousands of athletes are tested yearly with only 2% positive, an anonymous survey of 2011 World Championships athletes found 29% admitted to doping in the previous year (45% at the Pan Arab Games). Former WADA head Dick Pound said: "You can do hundreds of thousands of tests and then catch nobody if you don't want to catch anybody." WADA's own investigation found "no general appetite to undertake the effort and expense of a successful effort to deliver doping-free sport" [CBC News](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=X2aeV3fIUDo).

**3.5 The "Natural Lottery" Critique**

The review article by Urban Wiesing, published in Sports Medicine (February 2011), examines whether performance-enhancing drugs should be legalized in sport under medical supervision. Writing from a perspective that ultimately concludes against legalization, Wiesing nevertheless acknowledges a key argument: "The 'natural lottery' of athletic talents would be compensated for only partially by use of performance-enhancing agents. It would also be complemented by another 'natural lottery' of variable responses to doping measures, combined with the inventiveness of doping doctors." The article also notes that "there would be no gain in 'justice' (i.e. fairer results that reflected efforts made) for athletes as a result of legalizing doping" [PubMed Sports Medicine](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21244107).

**3.6 Performance Enhancement Attitude Scale (PEAS) Evidence**

The Brazilian study published in *Behavioral Sciences* (2024) validated the Performance Enhancement Attitude Scale (PEAS) for Brazilian athletes. The research involved 994 Brazilian athletes (mean age 22.79, 56% male, 44% female) across individual and team sports. The PEAS measures doping attitudes, considered a key predictor of doping behavior. The study found that most athletes disagreed with doping-favorable statements, though item 7 (health problems from training being as bad as doping) divided the sample. Items 5 (guilt about rule-breaking) and 15 (doping is cheating) were most disagreed with, suggesting moral factors protect against doping [PMC PEAS](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11200879). The Doping Attitudes and Behaviour Scale (DABS) report (submitted to WADA in May 2008) surveyed 375 high-performance athletes (64% male, mean age 23.8 years) from 27 sports. Key findings: 62.6% had received information on banned substances; 51.2% of those who received information did not feel confident in their knowledge; 9.4% admitted inadvertent use of prohibited substances; 11% admitted knowing use (80.5% of these were recreational drugs); only 2.2% (8 of 375) had used performance-enhancing substances specifically; and 42.2% claimed to personally know athletes who had used or were using prohibited substances. The reported prevalence of doping varied widely across studies, from a high of 14.5% among Turkish athletes to a low of 0% among Finnish government-funded athletes [WADA DABS](https://www.wada-ama.org/sites/default/files/resources/files/moran_final_report.pdf).

**Analysis:** The fairness argument against prohibition is perhaps the most pragmatic of the pro-legalization positions. The evidence is overwhelming that doping is far more prevalent than official statistics suggest, and that the system punishes primarily those who are caught rather than those who actually dope. This creates a situation where the most sophisticated cheaters — those with access to the best doctors, the most advanced undetectable substances, and the most effective masking agents — are the least likely to be caught. The Armstrong defense, whatever one thinks of its moral validity, accurately describes the reality of professional cycling during his era. The Mitchell Report demonstrates that baseball faced a similar dynamic. If the goal of anti-doping is to ensure fair competition, a system that catches only a small fraction of violators and punishes them disproportionately while the majority escape detection is arguably making competition less fair, not more. Legalization would not eliminate the advantage of superior resources, but it would at least create transparency and allow for the monitoring of all athletes' physiological parameters, rather than pretending that the vast majority are "clean" when they are not.

### Argument 4: Enhancement Is the Spirit of Sport — The Ethical Case for Human Progress

**Thesis Statement:** The very purpose of elite sport is human enhancement, improvement, and the pursuit of excellence, and the use of performance-enhancing technologies — both pharmacological and technological — is fundamentally consistent with, rather than contrary to, the "spirit of sport," as it represents the same human drive for progress, innovation, and self-improvement that sport celebrates.

**Supporting Points and Evidence:**

**4.1 Julian Savulescu's Foundational Argument**

Oxford ethics professor Julian Savulescu, in his Aeon essay "Why we should legalise most doping in sport," makes a foundational ethical argument for legalization. He argues that current anti-doping rules in sport are incoherent and should be reformed. Savulescu makes a distinction between two types of doping: (1) Physiological doping (steroids, growth hormone, EPO, blood doping): Enhances natural processes, allows athletes to train harder, and the human element remains central. The author argues these should be legalized as they "express the spirit of sport" — the human drive to be better. (2) Non-physiological/mental doping (analgesics, painkillers, cocaine, beta-blockers, stimulants like modafinil): Alters willpower, perception of pain, and mental attributes. The author argues these undermine courage, determination, and toughness, and should remain banned. Key quotes from Savulescu: "Sport originally evolved as a way of showing off our genetic fitness. Displaying great speed, strength, intelligence, ingenuity and co-ordination in public demonstrated to potential mates your capability to survive and reproduce." "I believe the virtues we want sport to test are not compromised by the kinds of doping Ben and Lance wish to engage in. Like all athletes, they will dedicate their lives to their sport." "Central to human progress (in life, as in sport) has been our ability to understand the world and ourselves, and modify these for the better. If this is admirable in life, why is doping against the spirit of sport? Doping expresses the spirit of sport. To be human is to be better." "Mental doping undermines courage, determination and 'toughness' — our very nature as human agents — and should be banned." Savulescu also notes that legal enhancers already exist: creatine (5-15% power increase), caffeine (10% increase in time-to-exhaustion), beetroot nitrates (15-25% increase). He critiques the "performance enhancement" criterion as bizarre since elite sport is explicitly about enhancement [Aeon Savulescu](https://aeon.co/essays/why-we-should-legalise-most-doping-in-sport).

**4.2 Andy Miah's Argument on Gene Doping and Being Human**

Ethicist Andy Miah argues that simply being human matters in sport and that genetic modification does not have to challenge this capacity. His work on "Genetically Modified Athletes: Biomedical Ethics, Gene Doping and Sport" (2004, cited by 478 subsequent works) explores the extremely important issue of modifying athletes genetically in order to develop elite sportsmen. Miah's work on "From anti-doping to a 'performance policy' sport technology, being human, and doing ethics" (2005, cited by 72 subsequent works) discusses three questions related to the ethics of performance enhancement in sport [PhilPapers Miah](https://philpapers.org/rec/MIAGMA) [Taylor & Francis Miah](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/17461390500077285).

**4.3 The Historical Context of Enhancement in Sport**

The Britannica article traces PED use back to ancient Olympic athletes who consumed animal hearts, hallucinogenic mushrooms, and other substances. The article notes that "the ancient Olympic champions were professionals who competed for huge cash prizes… most forms of what we would call cheating were perfectly acceptable to them, save for game-fixing" (Sally Jenkins). The article also discusses "gene doping" (inserting genes to enhance strength, endurance, etc.), which WADA banned in 2003, and "techno-doping" (specialized equipment like Oscar Pistorius's carbon-fiber blades and AI-powered smart glasses). Sally Jenkins is quoted: "Gene doping… the next step… athletes will be able to inject genes that build muscle. At which point steroids will seem as crude as sugar cubes soaked in ether" [Britannica ProCon.org](https://www.britannica.com/procon/sports-and-drugs-debate).

**4.4 The Inconsistency of Banning Some Enhancements While Allowing Others**

The Enhanced Games, launched on May 24, 2026, is framed by its founder Aron D'Souza as the "safest sporting event in history," claiming that organizers will pre-screen athletes for potential medical hazards and monitor athletes' health status while they are competing. The Stanford Law analysis notes that the boundary between acceptable and impermissible enhancement is already blurred — citing examples of equipment regulation (swimsuit bans, shoe restrictions), testing uncertainty (the Shelby Houlihan pork burrito case), and discriminatory testing practices. The article notes that bodybuilding already separates natural and enhanced categories [Stanford Law School](https://law.stanford.edu/2026/05/22/analyzing-doping-enhanced-sport-and-enterprise-responsibility). The legal analysis on Verfassungsblog notes: "The idea that athletes should compete substance-free is a 20th-century invention, not a timeless moral principle" (from the Enhanced Games vision statement) [Verfassungsblog](https://verfassungsblog.de/the-enhanced-games-from-a-human-rights-perspective).

**4.5 The Blair/WADA Spirit of Sport Critique**

WADA's own definition states: "Anti-doping programs seek to preserve what is intrinsically valuable about sport… the spirit of sport is the celebration of the human spirit, body and mind." However, critics argue that the human drive to transcend limitations through technology and pharmacology is itself a celebration of the human spirit. Savulescu argues: "Central to human progress (in life, as in sport) has been our ability to understand the world and ourselves, and modify these for the better. If this is admirable in life, why is doping against the spirit of sport? Doping expresses the spirit of sport. To be human is to be better" [Aeon Savulescu](https://aeon.co/essays/why-we-should-legalise-most-doping-in-sport).

**4.6 The Pistorius CAS Ruling as a Precedent for Technological Enhancement**

The Court of Arbitration for Sport (CAS) ruled in favor of Oscar Pistorius on May 16, 2008, in the case Pistorius v. The International Association of Athletics Federations (CAS 2008/A/1480). The ruling allowed Pistorius to compete against able-bodied athletes, establishing that his carbon-fiber blades did not provide a net metabolic advantage over human legs. The scientific evidence showed that his energy loss was greater than that of able-bodied runners, and that he was not gaining an unfair advantage. This ruling established a precedent that technological enhancement that compensates for a disability is not necessarily an unfair advantage — it can be an equalizer [CAS](https://www.tas-cas.org). The article from WBUR notes: "Who's going to pay for the testing of all the new developments that come up? When does a prosthetic limb that's designed to just be an equalizer for competition become performance enhancement?" (Dr. Robert Gailey) [WBUR](https://www.wbur.org/onlyagame/2012/08/04/pistorius).

**Analysis:** The enhancement-as-progress argument challenges the fundamental premise of the anti-doping movement. It posits that the human drive to improve through technology is not contrary to the spirit of sport but rather its fullest expression. This argument is particularly powerful when considering the historical trajectory of sport: athletes today run faster, jump higher, and throw farther than their predecessors not only because of better training and nutrition but also because of technological advances in equipment, clothing, and track surfaces. The distinction between "natural" enhancement (training, nutrition, altitude training) and "unnatural" enhancement (pharmacology, gene doping) is philosophically unstable — both are forms of human intervention designed to improve performance. Savulescu's distinction between physiological and mental doping offers a principled way to draw lines, but it would require a fundamental restructuring of the current system. The Pistorius case demonstrates that even technological enhancement can be ruled permissible when the evidence shows it does not create an unfair advantage, suggesting that the question is not whether enhancement occurs but whether it creates an unacceptable competitive imbalance.

### Argument 5: The Incoherence and Inconsistency of Current Bans — Technological and Environmental Enhancements

**Thesis Statement:** The current anti-doping regime is fundamentally incoherent because it arbitrarily bans certain forms of enhancement (pharmacological) while permitting — and even celebrating — other forms that provide equal or greater competitive advantages (technological, environmental, nutritional, and legal supplements), making the distinction between "natural" and "enhanced" performance essentially meaningless.

**Supporting Points and Evidence:**

**5.1 Legal Technological Enhancements That Are Not Banned**

A 2020 study found WBC produced superior timing-sequence recovery compared to traditional cold water immersion (Xia et al., 2020). WBC works through four mechanisms: reducing DOMS, anti-inflammatory cascade, norepinephrine release, and vasoconstriction-vasodilation cycle. WBC has been adopted by 15+ NFL teams, Olympic training programs, and the UFC Performance Institute. Used by LeBron James, Cristiano Ronaldo, and the 2011 Dallas Mavericks championship squad [Advanced Recovery Cryotherapy](https://advancedrecoverycryo.com/cryotherapy-for-athletes-sports-recovery-guide). The NPR article (September 16, 2006) notes: "The World Anti-Doping Agency will decide whether the tents should be on the banned list of performance enhancers, just like anabolic steroids" [NPR](https://www.npr.org/2006/09/16/6087755/anti-doping-agency-reviews-low-oxygen-tents).

**5.3 Legal Supplements That Provide Significant Performance Gains**

Savulescu's Aeon essay notes that legal enhancers already provide significant performance gains: creatine (5-15% power increase), caffeine (10% increase in time-to-exhaustion), beetroot nitrates (15-25% increase) [Aeon Savulescu](https://aeon.co/essays/why-we-should-legalise-most-doping-in-sport). The page notes that IV infusions for rehydration or recovery after exercise are not clinically indicated, as oral rehydration is the preferred method. **5.5 The Inconsistency of Equipment Regulation**

The Stanford Law analysis notes that the boundary between acceptable and impermissible enhancement is already blurred — citing examples of equipment regulation (swimsuit bans, shoe restrictions). The article notes that bodybuilding already separates natural and enhanced categories, and some scholars argue medically supervised doping may be ethically preferable to hidden use [Stanford Law School](https://law.stanford.edu/2026/05/22/analyzing-doping-enhanced-sport-and-enterprise-responsibility). The WBUR article on Pistorius notes: "We may not see another Oscar for a long, long time" (Dr. Robert Gailey). [WBUR](https://www.wbur.org/onlyagame/2012/08/04/pistorius).

**5.6 The Spectacle of the Enhanced Games**

The Stanford Law article reports on the Enhanced Games, which took place on May 24, 2026, backed by Peter Thiel, Donald Trump Jr., and Christian Angermayer, offering athletes $250,000 per event won and $1 million bonuses. The event openly allows performance-enhancing drugs and gene editing in defiance of the World Anti-Doping Code and UNESCO International Convention Against Doping in Sport. The Enhanced Games challenges the moral consensus by demonstrating that an alternative model is possible. The Verfassungsblog analysis notes: "WADA has strongly condemned the Enhanced Games, describing them as 'a dangerous and irresponsible concept'" [Verfassungsblog](https://verfassungsblog.de/the-enhanced-games-from-a-human-rights-perspective).

**Analysis:** The incoherence argument exposes the arbitrary nature of current anti-doping regulations. Athletes are permitted to sleep in hypoxic tents that simulate altitude training, use cryotherapy chambers that accelerate recovery, and consume legal supplements that provide measurable performance benefits — all of which are forms of technological and pharmacological enhancement. Yet they are prohibited from using anabolic steroids or EPO, even though the evidence for the performance-enhancing effects of the latter is at least as strong as for the former. The distinction between "natural" altitude training (traveling to a high-altitude location) and "artificial" altitude training (using a hypoxic tent) is particularly revealing: both produce the same physiological effect, but one is permitted because it requires travel and the other is viewed with suspicion. The Enhanced Games represents a real-world challenge to this incoherence, offering an alternative model where all enhancements are permitted under medical supervision. Critics argue that this approach would lead to a "wild west" of doping, but proponents counter that the current system is already a "wild west" — just one where the rules are enforced arbitrarily and the most sophisticated cheaters are rarely caught.

### Argument 6: The Lessons of Prohibition Failure — The East German Program and the Case for Regulation

**Thesis Statement:** The history of the East German state-sponsored doping program — which operated in secret, without consent, and with devastating health consequences — demonstrates both the catastrophic failure of prohibition and the dangers of unregulated doping, and the strongest lesson from this history is the need for a transparent, regulated, medically supervised framework that prioritizes athlete health and consent over punishment and secrecy.

**Supporting Points and Evidence:**

**6.1 The East German Program: The Dangers of Unregulated, Secret Doping**

The Wikipedia article on doping in East Germany details the systematic state-sponsored doping program conducted by the government of East Germany (GDR) from the 1960s until the 1990s. The program involved the coercive administration of performance-enhancing drugs, initially testosterone esters and later Oral Turinabol, to elite athletes. The goal was to bolster East Germany's international prestige by winning Olympic medals. The program was highly formalized, secretive, and pervasive, with athletes often deceived about the drugs they were given (told they were "vitamins"). Particular attention was paid to doping women and adolescent girls, as they gained the greatest performance boost. The program allowed East Germany to consistently score near the top of the Olympic medal standings, placing second overall in both 1976 and 1980, despite being a country of only 17-18 million people. An estimated 10,000 athletes were affected, with up to 1,000 suffering serious long-term physical and psychological damage. The motto at the pool was: "You eat the pills, or you die" [Wikipedia Doping in East Germany](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doping_in_East_Germany). The PBS "Secrets of the Dead" article notes that following the 1974 "state plan theme 14-25," the East German government, through its elite sports federation led by Manfred Ewald and monitored by the Stasi, systematically administered anabolic steroids (primarily Oral Turinabol) to athletes, including girls as young as 12, without their knowledge or consent. At the 1976 Montreal Olympics, East Germany won 40 gold medals, with the women's swim team winning 11 of 13 swim events. The doping was produced by the state-run company Jenapharm and monitored by over 3,000 Stasi informants. "Doping in the GDR was different from the doping in the West of the world but it was also different from the doping in other parts of the East. It was German, it was orderly, it was bureaucratic, it was written up" [PBS](https://www.pbs.org/wnet/secrets/the-state-sponsored-doping-program/52). The Gymnastics History article (2025) details the systematic doping program in East German gymnastics, centered on the Stasi archives and State Research Plan 14.25. From the mid-1970s onward, roughly 10,000 young athletes (some as young as 10) were enrolled in a centrally financed, medically engineered program that treated pharmacology as part of training. Drugs including anabolic steroids (Oral-Turinabol, STS 646), psychotropic substances, and neuropeptides (LVP, oxytocin, Substance P, ACTH 4-10) were administered without consent or explanation to athletes. Scientists developed new compounds specifically to evade detection, conducted clearance studies to time dosing for negative tests, and experimented with brain-altering substances to sharpen focus and coordination. Female gymnasts as young as 10-12 were given steroids via the "Kaiser-Schema" (originally a medical therapy for bone disorders) which stunted growth and caused permanent joint damage. By 1988, even insiders like Dr. Berndt Pansold warned that the combination of extreme training and drugs had "reached the limit of stress capacity" for athletes' connective and supporting tissues, causing permanent damage [Gymnastics History](https://www.gymnastics-history.com/2025/11/you-dont-treat-children-like-that-the-pharmaceutical-manipulation-of-east-german-gymnasts). Dagmar Kersten (silver medalist at Seoul 1988) said: "I would never have thought that something like that existed among us—it was outrageous. You don't treat children like that; it's the very last thing anyone in a position of trust should exploit" [Gymnastics History](https://www.gymnastics-history.com/2025/11/you-dont-treat-children-like-that-the-pharmaceutical-manipulation-of-east-german-gymnasts).

**6.2 The Lesson: Regulation and Consent, Not Prohibition**

The key lesson from the East German program is that the secrecy and coercion inherent in a prohibition system created the conditions for the worst abuses. The athletes were not informed, could not consent, and had no medical oversight focused on their well-being. The drugs were administered by coaches and state officials who had no accountability to the athletes. As the Ethics Unwrapped case study notes: "The training motto at the pool was, 'You eat the pills, or you die.' It was forbidden to refuse" [Ethics Unwrapped](https://ethicsunwrapped.utexas.edu/case-study/east-germanys-doping-machine). One doctor involved in the program said: "I can only repeat my profound regret. I was far too obedient. We were pressured into producing for the political leadership. We had to create international champions for the glory of the communist sporting machine." And: "I have friends who tell me that if I had not injected these teenage swimmers… someone else would have stepped in and replaced me. Of course, that was the same excuse used during the Third Reich that if we didn't respond to Hitler, another doctor would have fulfilled the function" [Ethics Unwrapped](https://ethicsunwrapped.utexas.edu/case-study/east-germanys-doping-machine). The DW article (July 8, 2025) profiles Andreas Wornowski, a former East German boxer who was a victim of the state-run doping program. Around 15,000 athletes were affected by the GDR doping program. Wornowski describes the ongoing struggle: "It's not just the lack of evidence, the health problems, the financial difficulties — it's disgusting and almost unbearable." Some of his former training buddies are already dead. "And at every funeral, everyone has the same thought: who knows what they gave us back then?" [DW](https://www.dw.com/en/east-german-doping-victim-fights-for-the-truth/a-73197063).

**6.3 The Russia State-Sponsored Doping Program**

The Stanford Law article draws on the GDR's state-sponsored doping program and Russia's four-year ban to argue that punishing only athletes misidentifies the source of harm. The article proposes an enterprise liability-shifting framework: maintaining WADA's four-year ban for serious intentional doping while adding enterprise duties of disclosure, independent testing, medical monitoring, insurance, and compensation [Stanford Law School](https://law.stanford.edu/2026/05/22/analyzing-doping-enhanced-sport-and-enterprise-responsibility).

**6.4 The Failure of Anti-Doping Enforcement**

The UK Government review (October 2017) found no compelling case to criminalise the act of doping in sport, concluding that "Criminalising the act of doping in sport in the UK would be disproportionate in the battle to keep sport clean." The review found that the strongest deterrent for athletes is being banned from sport, not criminal sanctions [UK Government Review](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/5a82b2ece5274a2e8ab58e27/Review_of_Criminalisation_of_Doping_in_Sport.doc). The Global Triathlon Network video (August 2024) explains: "The biggest battle that anti-doping authorities face is the battle for public perception because there is a perception that the dopers are always ahead of the authorities. You can watch 100 races without any positive tests and that is only seen as evidence that the anti-doping authorities are not doing their job but one positive test out of all of those 100 races and that's seen as evidence that everyone is doping" [Global Triathlon Network](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ZnULjLThWLA).

**6.5 The Ben Johnson Case: Due Process Failures**

The Globe and Mail article documents that Johnson was denied due process, with multiple irregularities in his drug testing, conflicts of interest, and the IOC officials acting as testers, prosecutors, and judges. This pattern of selective enforcement undermines the legitimacy of the entire anti-doping regime [The Globe and Mail](https://www.theglobeandmail.com/sports/article-trouble-in-the-lab-the-ben-johnson-doping-case-was-anything-but-open).

**6.6 The Lance Armstrong Case: The Cost of the "War on Doping"**

The USADA reasoned decision against Armstrong (October 10, 2012) was a 200-page report with testimonies from 11 former teammates and 15 other witnesses, portraying Armstrong as the ringleader of "the most sophisticated, professionalized and successful doping program that sport has ever seen" [USADA Reasoned Decision](https://www.usada.org/wp-content/uploads/ReasonedDecision.pdf). The Harvard Business School case study by Professor Clayton S. Rose, "Following Lance Armstrong: Excellence Corrupted," highlights how the central ethical question revolves around followers like Tyler Hamilton, who initially doped and aided Armstrong before later testifying against him. Rose emphasizes that the case is about how "good people" can find themselves participating in corrupt systems. "There was one Armstrong, but there were lots of Hamiltons" [Harvard Business School](https://www.library.hbs.edu/working-knowledge/lessons-from-the-lance-armstrong-cheating-scandal). The Ethics Unwrapped case study on Armstrong notes: "It was not enough that his teammates give maximum effort on the bike, he also required that they adhere to the doping program outlined for them or be replaced. He was not just a part of the doping culture on his team, he enforced and re-enforced it" [Ethics Unwrapped Armstrong](https://ethicsunwrapped.utexas.edu/video/armstrongs-doping-downfall).

**6.7 The Mitchell Report and the MLB Experience**

The Mitchell Report's key recommendations included: independent testing administration, improved player education on health dangers, and modernized standards for drug program negotiations. After the report, MLB significantly increased testing and penalties, with first positive tests now resulting in 80-game suspensions, second in 162 games, and third in lifetime bans. George Mitchell said in 2018: "I think it's abandonment of the responsibility as a voter if you just look at the statistics because the Hall of Fame has clear standards other than statistics" [ESPN Mitchell](https://www.espn.com/mlb/story/_/id/22182872/george-mitchell-legacy-mitchell-report-peds-hall-fame).

**6.8 The Overbye Study: Athlete Preferences Under a Regulated System**

The Overbye study (2018) of 775 Danish elite athletes found that 58% of athletes reported interest in one or more of 13 proposed substances/methods under medical supervision. Male speed/power sports athletes of increasing age had the highest likelihood of AAS interest (41% at age 36). This suggests that a regulated system could meet the needs of athletes who are currently doping in secret, while providing medical oversight that protects their health [ScienceDirect Overbye](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S095539591730381X).

**6.9 The Stanford Enterprise Liability Proposal**

The Stanford Law article proposes maintaining WADA's four-year ban for serious intentional doping while adding enterprise duties of disclosure, independent testing, medical monitoring, insurance, and compensation. The U.S. 9th Circuit's decision in *Hunt v. Zuffa* is cited as pointing toward this path by allowing legal theories tied to the promoter's failure to disclose an opponent's doping status [Stanford Law School](https://law.stanford.edu/2026/05/22/analyzing-doping-enhanced-sport-and-enterprise-responsibility).

**Analysis:** The East German program is often cited by opponents of doping as evidence of its dangers, but it can equally be read as evidence of the dangers of unregulated, secret doping conducted without athlete consent or medical oversight focused on athlete welfare. The program's horrors — the coercion, the deception, the lack of informed consent, the devastating health consequences — were made possible by the secrecy and lack of accountability that the prohibition model creates. In a regulated, transparent system, athletes would have access to medical supervision, would be informed about what they are taking, and would have the legal protections that come with any medical treatment. The Russian state-sponsored doping program represents a similar dynamic: athletes were caught up in a system that prioritized medal counts over their health and well-being. The Stanford enterprise liability proposal offers a concrete alternative that would shift responsibility from individual athletes to the organizations that profit from their performances, creating incentives for safer practices. The key insight is that the East German program's worst features — coercion, secrecy, lack of consent — are not inherent to doping but to the specific conditions under which it occurred. A regulated system could eliminate these features while preserving the performance benefits.

## Part II: Arguments Against Allowing Performance-Enhancing Drugs and Technologies

### Argument 1: Health Risks and Medical Consequences of Performance-Enhancing Drugs

**Thesis Statement:** Performance-enhancing drugs pose severe and well-documented health risks to athletes, including cardiovascular disease, psychiatric disorders, hormonal disruption, organ damage, and death, and legalizing these substances would expose athletes to unacceptable medical harms that are fundamentally incompatible with the duty of care that sport organizations owe to participants.

**Supporting Points and Evidence:**

**1.1 Anabolic-Androgenic Steroids (AAS) — Documented Health Consequences**

The Endocrine Society Scientific Statement (PMC4026349) reports that at least 3 million PED users exist in the U.S. alone, making PED use more prevalent than type 1 diabetes or HIV infection, yet public health resources allocated to this issue are negligible. The statement emphasizes that the vast majority of PED users are not competitive athletes, but rather nonathlete weightlifters (recreational bodybuilders) focused on personal appearance. There is a widespread misperception that PED use is safe or that adverse effects are manageable. The great majority of AAS use begins after the teenage years (median age of onset 22-24 years). About 32.5% of AAS users develop AAS dependence, meaning approximately 1 million American men have experienced AAS dependence. PED users frequently combine multiple drugs (stacking), including classical drugs of abuse like opiates. Adverse health effects include: cardiovascular disease (dyslipidemia, cardiomyopathy, hypertension), psychiatric disorders (mania, depression, aggression, AAS dependence), endocrine effects (HPT suppression, hypogonadism, gynecomastia), hepatic toxicity (especially from oral 17α-alkylated androgens), musculoskeletal injuries (tendon rupture), renal failure, and infectious disease risk from needle sharing. Researchers cannot ethically conduct controlled studies of the long-term adverse effects of PEDs in normal volunteers, especially when using supraphysiologic doses [PMC Endocrine Society](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4026349). The AMA Journal of Ethics (Dr. Carlos R. Hamilton) states: "The cardiac toxicities of AAS, stimulants, and other performance-enhancing drugs are well known and include sudden death, thromboembolic phenomena, cardiomyopathy, and arrhythmias." AAS have unique adverse outcomes in adolescents because they accelerate bone epiphysis maturation, leading, ultimately, to reduced matured height. The prescribing of potentially harmful drugs for nontherapeutic uses not only violates federal and state drug laws but is contrary to all standards of medical practice. It is well known that the desired effects of AAS on muscle growth and strength are not sustained beyond the time of use and may actually result in decreased long-term performance [AMA Journal of Ethics](https://journalofethics.ama-assn.org/article/medical-ethics-and-performance-enhancing-drugs/2005-11). The Annals of Sports Medicine and Research article (PMC5215887) notes: "Administration of ergogenic substances causes serious health risks for the sports people. For example the toxicities of anabolic-androgenic steroids (AAS) which are among the well-known drugs used to enhance the performance of athletes include sudden death, thromboembolic phenomena, cardiomyopathy, arrhythmias, stroke, seizures, and psychiatric conditions such as anxiety, and mood changes" [PMC Annals](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5215887). The USADA webpage on effects of PEDs reports that side effects of anabolic agents (testosterone) include: acne, male pattern baldness, liver damage, stunted growth, increased aggressiveness ("Roid Rage"), depression, and suicide. Male-specific: breast tissue, testicular shrinkage, impotence. Female-specific: voice deepening, facial hair, enlarged clitoris, abnormal menstrual cycles. "Withdrawal from anabolic steroid use can be associated with depression, and in some cases, suicide" [USADA Effects](https://www.usada.org/substances/effects-of-performance-enhancing-drugs). The Physiopedia article on PEDs notes that approximately 50% of the general population have taken some form of dietary supplements, while 76-100% of athletes in some sports report using them. Clinical consequences of PEDs include increased risk of arrhythmias, heart failure, and sudden cardiac death. Direct effects include hypertension, dyslipidemia, myocyte hypertrophy, and interstitial fibrosis [Physiopedia](https://www.physio-pedia.com/Effects_of_Performance_Enhancing_Drugs). The Mayo Clinic article on PEDs reports that anabolic steroids (synthetic testosterone) can cause severe side effects including infertility, breast development, liver damage, and heart disease in both men and women. Androstenedione (andro) may damage the heart and increase risk of heart attack and stroke. Human growth hormone may cause swelling, joint pain, nerve damage, and increased cancer risk. Diuretics alter fluid balance and can cause dehydration, muscle cramps, dizziness, and even death. Creatine may cause weight gain, cramps, and dehydration. Stimulants (including caffeine, amphetamines, and energy drinks) can cause anxiety, jitters, insomnia, dehydration, and heart problems [Mayo Clinic](https://www.mayoclinic.org/healthy-lifestyle/fitness/in-depth/performance-enhancing-drugs/art-20046134). A case report (PubMed, 1998, Hausmann et al., PMID 9728754) documents the sudden cardiac death of a 23-year-old bodybuilder who used anabolic steroids combined with other performance-enhancing drugs [PubMed Hausmann](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9728754).

**1.2 Erythropoietin (EPO) and Blood Doping — Health Consequences and Deaths**

The Austin Publishing Group review article on blood doping (2016) reports that EPO is a naturally occurring peptide hormone that stimulates red blood cell production. The synthetic form, rHu EPO, is abused in sports like cycling, endurance running, and cross-country skiing. Studies show rHu EPO increases hemoglobin (7-11%), hematocrit (up to 50%), and VO2 max (7-12%), improving time to exhaustion. However, EPO use elevates hematocrit, increasing blood viscosity and peripheral resistance, raising blood pressure and cardiac workload. It can cause hypertension, cerebral sinus thrombosis, thromboembolic events, and sudden death. Between 1987-1990, 18 Dutch and Belgian cyclists died with very high hematocrit levels, attributed to blood doping. EPO also reduces plasma volume, worsening dehydration. The article notes: "The fatalities of 18 Dutch and Belgian cyclists with very high haematocrit between 1987 and 1990 have never been fully justified. It is mostly believed that blood boosting or EPO use might have triggered hyperviscosity." In a case report of a 26-year-old cyclist who presented with chronic headaches and photophobia for two months, he admitted the use of 200 IU of rHu EPO and growth hormones. His brain MRI showed cerebral sinus thrombosis and obstruction of the superior and transverse sagittal sinus. There is a shortage of information on the mechanism by which rHu EPO causes serious complications such as MI, stroke, seizure, hypertension, thromboembolism, congestive heart failure and even death [Austin Publishing Group](https://austinpublishinggroup.com/emergency-medicine/fulltext/aem-v2-id1015.php). The Mayo Clinic article confirms: "In the 1990s, it was common for pro cyclists to use erythropoietin. But the drug may have played a role in at least 18 deaths" [Mayo Clinic](https://www.mayoclinic.org/healthy-lifestyle/fitness/in-depth/performance-enhancing-drugs/art-20046134). The USADA webpage states: "Blood doping... increases the number of red blood cells in the body. This increase thickens the blood, making it difficult for the heart to pump. There have been multiple deaths attributed to blood doping" [USADA Effects](https://www.usada.org/substances/effects-of-performance-enhancing-drugs).

**1.3 Other Pharmacological Agents — Health Risks**

The USADA webpage reports that peptide hormones (EPO, hGH, HCG, insulin) carry risks including hypertension, blood cancers, strokes, heart attacks, pulmonary embolism, and thyroid problems. Beta-2 Agonists cause palpitations, headaches, nausea, muscle cramps, nervousness. Diuretics cause dehydration, muscle cramps, dizziness, drop in blood pressure, potential death. Stimulants cause insomnia, anxiety, addiction, increased heart rate, stroke, heart attack, cardiac arrhythmia. "Abuse of some stimulants has been shown to age the cardiovascular system more aggressively than smoking." Cannabinoids increase heart rate, impair memory, slow coordination, and cause respiratory diseases. Narcotics cause respiratory depression, false sense of invincibility, addiction, and potential death. Beta blockers cause lowered blood pressure, slow heart rate, and sleep disorders [USADA Effects](https://www.usada.org/substances/effects-of-performance-enhancing-drugs). The Britannica ProCon.org article on sports and drugs states that despite injury recovery benefits, PEDs are dangerous and can cause addiction, heart damage, stroke, psychosis, and death. Bodybuilding has seen 14 deaths in 10 years [Britannica ProCon.org](https://www.britannica.com/procon/sports-and-drugs-debate).

**1.4 The East German State-Sponsored Doping Program — Devastating Health Consequences**

The Wikipedia article on doping in East Germany reports that the government of the German Democratic Republic (GDR) conducted a decades-long program of coercive administration and distribution of performance-enhancing drugs, initially testosterone esters and later Oral Turinabol, to elite athletes. The program was highly formalized, secretive, and pervasive, with athletes often deceived about the drugs they were given (told they were "vitamins"). Particular attention was paid to doping women and adolescent girls because they gained the greatest performance boost. The program allowed East Germany to consistently score near the top of Olympic medal rankings, placing second overall in both 1976 and 1980, despite being a country of only 17-18 million people. However, it caused devastating health consequences for athletes, including virilization in women, cardiovascular disease, liver problems, cancer, and psychological damage. An estimated 10,000 athletes were affected, with up to 1,000 suffering serious long-term physical and psychological damage. After German reunification in the 1990s, secret Stasi records revealed the scale of the program. Key figures like Sports Minister Manfred Ewald and top sports doctor Manfred Höppner were convicted of causing bodily harm to athletes. Former athletes have since sought and received financial compensation from the German government, including a 13.65 million euro fund established in 2016 (Second Doping Victims Assistance Act, passed June 28, 2016). Notable cases include Andreas Krieger (formerly Heidi), who underwent sex reassignment surgery partly attributed to steroid use; Renate Neufeld, a sprinter who defected; and Jörg Sievers, a teenage swimmer who died from an enlarged heart linked to steroid use [Wikipedia Doping in East Germany](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doping_in_East_Germany). The Ethics Unwrapped case study (UT Austin) reports that the GDR systematically doped approximately 9,000 athletes, primarily with the anabolic steroid Oral-Turinabol ("little blue and pink pills, handed out like candy"). The training motto at the pool was: "'You eat the pills, or you die.' It was forbidden to refuse." Severe side effects included: men's penises shrinking, women's voices deepening, excessive hair growth, liver damage, heart disease, cancers, infertility, and birth defects. Doctors and coaches knew the risks but continued due to political pressure and obedience to authority [Ethics Unwrapped](https://ethicsunwrapped.utexas.edu/case-study/east-germanys-doping-machine). The PMC article "Doping in sport—a warning from history" (Macauley, 2007) notes that East German athletes who were doped to win gold medals in 1976 Olympics now struggle with chronic health problems [PMC Macauley](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1988980).

**1.5 Tyler Hamilton's First-Hand Account of Doping Dangers**

The USADA webpage features Tyler Hamilton's first-person account of his doping experience: "I knew it was wrong from the moment I put it in my mouth... I thought about suicide, I drank to just check out at some points." He described starting with a testosterone pill offered by a team doctor, progressing to EPO injections and blood transfusions, and his eventual testimony before a federal grand jury [USADA Effects](https://www.usada.org/substances/effects-of-performance-enhancing-drugs).

**Analysis:** The health evidence against PEDs is overwhelming and should be taken seriously. The documented deaths of 18 Dutch and Belgian cyclists in the late 1980s, the sudden cardiac death of a 23-year-old bodybuilder, the devastating health consequences for East German athletes, and the widespread medical complications documented in the Endocrine Society's scientific statement all demonstrate that PEDs can cause catastrophic harm. The 32.5% dependence rate among AAS users is particularly concerning, as it suggests that many users may not be able to stop even when they want to. The argument that medical supervision would eliminate these risks is questionable: even under medical supervision, supraphysiological doses of these substances carry inherent risks that cannot be fully mitigated. The East German program, despite its medical supervision, caused devastating health consequences because the goal was performance enhancement, not athlete welfare. The harm reduction argument acknowledges that prohibition has not eliminated doping but assumes that regulation would make it safer — yet the evidence from medical contexts where these drugs are used therapeutically (e.g., testosterone replacement therapy, EPO for anemia) suggests that the doses used for performance enhancement, which are typically much higher than therapeutic doses, would still carry significant risks even under medical supervision.

### Argument 2: Cheating, Integrity, and the Spirit of Sport

**Thesis Statement:** Performance-enhancing drugs fundamentally undermine the integrity of sport by violating the principle of fair competition, distorting the relationship between effort and achievement, and compromising the "spirit of sport" that values natural talent, dedication, and human excellence attained through legitimate means.

**Supporting Points and Evidence:**

**2.1 WADA's "Spirit of Sport" Framework**

The World Anti-Doping Code states that "there is an intrinsic value about sports that is the celebration of the human spirit, body and mind, and is reflected in values other than winning or being the first in any sports game." The WADA Code defines the "spirit of sport" as encompassing values including ethics, fair play, honesty, health, excellence, character, teamwork, and respect. The Britannica ProCon.org article quotes WADA: "Anti-doping programs seek to preserve what is intrinsically valuable about sport. This intrinsic value is often referred to as 'the spirit of sport.' It is the essence of Olympism, the pursuit of human excellence through the dedicated perfection of each person's talents" [Britannica ProCon.org](https://www.britannica.com/procon/sports-and-drugs-debate). The Annals of Sports Medicine and Research article (PMC5215887) notes that the ultimate goal of the Code is to justify that doping is fundamentally contrary to sport ethics that constitutes the "spirit of sport." However, the article also notes that the Code and anti-doping strategies have been subject to criticism by the ethics community with the arguments that there is no substantial definition of the term "spirit of sports," and that the fight against doping is based on questionable ethical grounds consisting of dubious claims about fairness in sports and terms such as "level playing field" [PMC Annals](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5215887).

**2.2 The Hastings Center's Ethical Framework**

The Hastings Center for Bioethics briefing by Thomas H. Murray, PhD, presents three arguments for prohibiting PEDs: (1) The Significance of Rules in Sport: Rules define which characteristics determine winners and losers. Equipment innovations (like fiberglass poles vs. banned super-slippery swimsuits) are evaluated based on continuity of skill requirements and equal opportunity. (2) Natural Talents and Their Perfection: The point of sport is to see how natural talents can be developed through intensive training, dedication, and smart tactics—not pharmacological shortcuts. (3) The "Arms Race" in Sport: PEDs create pressure on all athletes to use them, similar to an arms race, potentially leading to a public health catastrophe from high-dose, bizarre drug combinations. The Hastings Center states: "The athletes I know all crave a level playing field. If my competition is gaining an edge by using a drug that tilts the field in their favor, then their choice pressures me to do the same. Otherwise I may end up losing to someone who is less talented or less dedicated." And: "The drug race in sport has the potential to create a slow-motion public health catastrophe." And: "What do we value in sport? Emerging technologies—from hypoxic chambers and carbon fiber prostheses to genetic manipulation—will force us consider what, after all, is the point of sport?" [Hastings Center](https://www.thehastingscenter.org/briefingbook/sports-enhancement).

**2.3 The Britannica ProCon.org Ethical Arguments**

The Britannica article presents three Con arguments (against PEDs): (1) PEDs and smart technology violate the spirit and integrity of sport, altering the nature of competition by elevating certain physical attributes over skill, strategy, and mental resilience. (2) Despite injury recovery benefits, PEDs are dangerous and can cause addiction, heart damage, stroke, psychosis, and death. (3) Allowing PEDs will increase youth drug use (average first use at age 14) and encourage other dangerous behaviors among young fans who view athletes as role models. The article quotes Adam Nelson: "The thing that makes sports fantastic is watching competition on a level playing field. Throw PEDs in the mix, and it becomes not about how good you are, but how good your doctor is" [Britannica ProCon.org](https://www.britannica.com/procon/sports-and-drugs-debate).

**2.4 The Coercion Argument (Philosophical)**

The PhilPapers entry on "The Coercion Argument Against Performance-Enhancing Drugs" (Veblen) states: "According to this argument, lifting the ban on PEDs would undermine the autonomy of athletes by creating a situation where everyone must either use PEDs or not" [PhilPapers Coercion](https://philpapers.org/rec/VEBTCA). The MDPI Religions article (Blazer, 2020) argues that rituals of confession preserve an underlying ideology that sport is morally valuable. When these rituals fail, they reveal less noble structural motivations that lead to doping in the first place like monetary reward, intense pressure to perform, and the entertainment demands of elite sport. The article quotes Swiss rider Alex Zülle: "As a rider, you feel tied into the system. It's like being on the highway. The law says there's a speed limit of 65, but everyone is driving 70 or faster. Why should I be the one who obeys the speed limit?" The article also notes the history of "Muscular Christianity," a 19th-century Protestant movement that defined manliness as a combination of Christian morality and athleticism, which shaped the belief that sport builds character. Sports sociologist Jay Coakley is quoted: "In large part, organized sports became important because they could be used to train loyal and hardworking people dedicated to achievement and production for the glory of God and country" [MDPI Religions](https://www.mdpi.com/2077-1444/11/11/605).

**2.5 BBC Ethics — Arguments Against Legalization**

The BBC Ethics Guide page presents arguments against legalizing performance-enhancing drugs in sport, noting that "some of the arguments against allowing drugs are tied in with the arguments against cheating in general" [BBC Ethics](https://www.bbc.co.uk/ethics/sport/debate/against.shtml).

**Analysis:** The integrity argument against PEDs is rooted in the fundamental purpose of sport: to provide a venue for testing and displaying human excellence achieved through natural talent, dedication, and hard work. When athletes use drugs to enhance performance, the argument goes, the competition becomes a test of who has the best pharmacology rather than who has the best combination of natural ability and training. The Hastings Center's framework is particularly useful in distinguishing between permissible enhancements (like better equipment) and impermissible ones (like drugs) based on whether they preserve the continuity of skill requirements and equal opportunity. The coercion argument adds a crucial dimension: even if some athletes would prefer not to use PEDs, the knowledge that their competitors are using them creates pressure to follow suit. The current ban, whatever its flaws, at least provides a formal framework for resisting this pressure. Critics of the integrity argument note that sport has always involved technological enhancement (from better shoes to lighter bicycles), and that the line between natural and unnatural is difficult to draw. However, the fact that the line is difficult to draw does not mean that no line exists — and the ethical consensus, reflected in WADA's Code and the laws of most nations, is that pharmacological enhancement for performance purposes crosses an important boundary.

### Argument 3: The Arms Race, Coercion, and Unequal Access

**Thesis Statement:** Legalizing performance-enhancing drugs would create an uncontrolled "arms race" in which athletes are coerced into using dangerous substances to remain competitive, while those with the greatest financial resources — rather than the greatest natural talent or dedication — would gain the most significant advantages, fundamentally undermining the fairness that sport is supposed to embody.

**Supporting Points and Evidence:**

**3.1 The Arms Race and Coercion to Dope**

The Hastings Center briefing states: "The drug race in sport has the potential to create a slow-motion public health catastrophe." PEDs create pressure on all athletes to use them, similar to an arms race, potentially leading to a public health catastrophe from high-dose, bizarre drug combinations. The Hastings Center notes: "The athletes I know all crave a level playing field. If my competition is gaining an edge by using a drug that tilts the field in their favor, then their choice pressures me to do the same. Otherwise I may end up losing to someone who is less talented or less dedicated" [Hastings Center](https://www.thehastingscenter.org/briefingbook/sports-enhancement). The Argufight.com debate notes: "PED bans are not. The biological arms race evolves faster than testing technology, which guarantees perpetual cat-and-mouse policing rather than fairness" [Argufight](https://www.argufight.com/debate/09a3e17f-2ae9-429d-9bd5-07747dacd268/should-performance-enhancing-drugs-be-allowed-in-sports).

**3.2 Unequal Access to Technology**

The Britannica ProCon article notes that Oscar Pistorius's "Cheetah blades" (carbon-fiber prosthetics) cost $15-18,000 USD per pair. The article addresses the debate over whether such technology creates an unfair advantage and the broader issue of techno-doping (specialized equipment or technological augmentation) [Britannica ProCon.org](https://www.britannica.com/procon/sports-and-drugs-debate). The Hastings Center briefing notes that emerging technologies—from hypoxic chambers and carbon fiber prostheses to genetic manipulation—will force us to consider what is the point of sport [Hastings Center](https://www.thehastingscenter.org/briefingbook/sports-enhancement).

**3.3 Hypoxic Chambers and Altitude Tents — Unequal Access and Ethical Debate**

The "Feel the Byrn" article (Dr. John Hellemans) on altitude simulation methods discusses three main approaches: altitude houses, breath-holding (apnoea training), and altitude tents. In 2003, altitude simulation methods were banned by the Governments of Norway and Italy. They based their decision on a paper published by Baker and Hopkins (1998), which suggested that hypoxic training gave athletes an unfair technological advantage and was dangerous if the training was not planned and monitored by an expert in hypoxic training. The article notes that altitude simulation existed in Eastern Europe and Russia before WWII [Substack Hellemans](https://feelthebyrn.substack.com/p/altitude-simulation). The NPR article (September 2006) reports that WADA's panel ruled that the altitude tent violated the spirit of sport because it required the athlete to do something unnatural [NPR](https://www.npr.org/2006/09/16/6087755/anti-doping-agency-reviews-low-oxygen-tents). The Canadian Science Publishing article (PMID 17486171, Malloy et al., 2007) notes that currently, altitude training is considered moral, but other means of improving aerobic performance are not; for example, blood doping. The article discusses the logic and morality of hypoxic tents [CDNSciencePub](https://cdnsciencepub.com/doi/10.1139/H07-088). The PubMed article (Malloy, 2007) on "The spirit of sport, morality, and hypoxic tents" examines the logic behind the ethical distinction between altitude training and other performance-enhancing methods. There is actual debate on a recent position of WADA, which has cautiously refrained from banning hypoxic tents and intends to regulate them [PubMed Malloy](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17486171).

**3.4 Oscar Pistorius's Running Blades — The Technological Advantage Debate**

The Wikipedia article on the mechanics of Oscar Pistorius's running blades reports that the Flex-Foot Cheetah prosthetics are made from layers of carbon-fiber-reinforced polymer (30-90 sheets depending on athlete weight), autoclaved to fuse into a solid plate, and bolted to custom carbon-fibre sockets. Each limb costs $15-18,000 USD. The blades are designed to store kinetic energy like a spring, allowing the wearer to jump and run effectively. The IAAF initially banned the blades in 2008 after the Brüggemann study found that Pistorius used ~25% less energy expenditure than non-disabled athletes once he achieved a given speed, that the blades returned nearly 3x more energy than a human ankle, and that energy loss in the blade was 9.3% vs. 42.4% in a normal ankle — showing a difference of more than 30%. However, the Court of Arbitration for Sport (CAS) reinstated him after the Weyand et al. study (2008) found his energy usage was 3.8% lower than elite distance runners and 17% lower than non-disabled 400m sprinters, concluding that running on blades is "physiologically similar but mechanically different" from running with biological legs. The study also found that Pistorius re-positioned his legs 15.7% faster than most world record sprinters, allowing for a 15-30% increase in sprint speed. A follow-up Grabowski study (2009) found that single running blades reduced ground force production by 9%, limiting top speed. The 2012 Paralympics saw controversy when Brazilian runner Alan Oliveira and USA runner Blake Leeper switched to longer running blades, leading to marked improvement. Oliveira's stride length was 2.2m vs. Pistorius's 2.0m (9% longer), though Oliveira took more strides (99 vs. 92). Research limitations include that studies were conducted on level treadmills and did not measure performance from starting blocks, on curved tracks, or account for physiological differences between amputees and non-amputees [Wikipedia Mechanics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mechanics_of_Oscar_Pistorius%27s_running_blades). The Guardian article (2012) argues that the blades do give an advantage over able-bodied runners [The Guardian](https://www.theguardian.com/sport/2012/sep/04/pistorius-blades-advantage-in-olympics). The Scientific American article (2012) notes that scientists debate whether prosthetic legs give Pistorius an unfair advantage in the 400-meter race [Scientific American](https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/scientists-debate-oscar-pistorius-prosthetic-legs-disqualify-him-olympics).

**Analysis:** The arms race argument against legalization is powerful because it addresses both ethical and practical concerns. If PEDs were legalized, athletes who do not wish to use them would face an impossible choice: either use dangerous substances or accept a competitive disadvantage. This is not genuine choice but coercion. Furthermore, the unequal access argument highlights that not all athletes would benefit equally from legalization. Wealthy athletes from developed countries would have access to the best doctors, the most advanced drugs, and the most sophisticated monitoring technology, while athletes from developing countries would be left behind. The Pistorius case illustrates this dynamic: his carbon-fiber blades cost $15-18,000 per pair, putting them beyond the reach of many amputee athletes. If technological enhancement is permitted, those with the greatest financial resources gain the greatest advantage. The same would apply to pharmacological enhancement: the best drugs, the best medical supervision, and the best monitoring would be available to those who can afford it. This would transform sport from a competition of talent and dedication into a competition of financial resources, undermining the meritocratic ideal that sport is supposed to represent.

### Argument 4: The Legal Framework — Anti-Doping Rules, Sanctions, and Deterrence

**Thesis Statement:** The current anti-doping legal framework, despite its imperfections, provides a necessary deterrent effect that protects the integrity of sport, and the evidence shows that testing and sanctions, when properly implemented, can reduce doping prevalence and maintain public confidence in the fairness of competition.

**Supporting Points and Evidence:**

**4.1 WADA's Anti-Doping Rule Violations (ADRVs) — Statistics and Sanctions**

The WADA 2023 Anti-Doping Rule Violations (ADRVs) Report (published December 2025) contains a total of 2,005 ADRVs recorded in 2023, a significant increase from 1,652 in 2022. These violations involve individuals from 124 nations and across 94 sports/disciplines. In 2023, 271,775 samples were collected by Anti-Doping Organizations (ADOs), compared to 241,143 in 2022. Of the ADRVs, 1,654 came from Adverse Analytical Findings (positive tests), while 351 were non-analytical findings derived from investigations and intelligence (involving 325 athletes and 24 Athlete Support Personnel). The non-analytical findings represent a 19.38% increase from 2022's 294 figure and the highest total since 2020. WADA Director General Olivier Niggli noted: "The year-on-year increase of Anti-Doping Rule Violations, even allowing for the fact that more samples were collected in 2023, is a positive sign that the system is catching more violators." He cited improvements in testing, analytical techniques, non-analytical methods like intelligence and investigations, and education programs [WADA 2023 ADRV Report](https://www.wada-ama.org/en/news/wada-publishes-2023-anti-doping-rule-violations-report). The Global Sports Advocates webpage details sanctions under the World Anti-Doping Code (WADC). The period of ineligibility ranges from no ineligibility (if No Fault or Negligence is established) up to six years (with aggravating circumstances). Key violation categories: Positive Test/Use (Articles 2.1/2.2): 2 years for specified substances, 4 years for non-specified substances. Reductions possible based on establishing source and degree of fault. Possession (Article 2.6): Same sanction range as Articles 2.1/2.2. Failing to Submit/Tampering (Articles 2.3/2.5): 4 years standard, reducible to 2 years if unintentional or exceptional circumstances. Whereabouts Failures (Article 2.4): 2 years, reducible to 1 year. Trafficking/Administration (Articles 2.7/2.8): Minimum 4 years up to lifetime ineligibility. Complicity (Article 2.9): Minimum 2 years up to lifetime ban. Prohibited Association (Article 2.10): 2 years, reducible to 1 year. Discouraging/Retaliating Against Reporting (Article 2.11): Minimum 2 years up to lifetime ineligibility [Global Sports Advocates](https://www.globalsportsadvocates.com/library/world-anti-doping-code-sanctions-rule-violations.cfm).

**4.2 Olympic Doping Statistics**

The Britannica ProCon.org article reports that drug testing at the Olympics began in 1968. As of the 2020 Summer Games in Tokyo, there had been 442 positive doping tests, resulting in the loss of 173 medals (57 gold, 66 silver, and 50 bronze) over 28 Games in 52 years. Russian athletes have most often tested positive, with 133 positive doping results, and have had the most medals stripped at 63 total (17 gold, 34 silver, and 12 bronze). The most positive tests are found in athletics (track and field), with 172 positive tests and 59 lost medals. Turinabol (an androgenic anabolic steroid) is the drug most associated with positive doping tests, showing up in 65 tests by itself and in 9 drug cocktails for another 35 positive tests [Britannica ProCon.org](https://www.britannica.com/procon/sports-and-drugs-debate/Doping-Cases-at-the-Olympics).

**4.3 The Dubin Inquiry (Canada, 1989)**

Following the 1988 Ben Johnson positive drug test at the Seoul Olympics, the Canadian government established the Commission of Inquiry led by Chief Justice Charles Dubin (January 1989, 91 days, 122 witnesses, 46 athletes admitted steroid use, cost $5 million). Johnson testified on June 12, 1989: "I lied. I lied and I was ashamed for my family, my friends, other Canadian athletes. I was just in a mess." The Dubin Inquiry investigated widespread drug use in sports and found that steroid use was rampant among top athletes. The Canadian Olympic Committee website notes that of the top five competitors in the 100m final at Seoul 1988, only former world record holder and eventual bronze medalist Calvin Smith never failed a drug test during his career. Smith later said: "I should have been the gold medalist." Time would prove that five of the other seven runners in the 100m final at Seoul 1988 were eventually caught cheating during their career [Canadian Olympic Committee](https://olympic.ca/team-canada/ben-johnson). The Wikipedia article on Ben Johnson notes that the 1988 100m final has been "often dubbed 'the dirtiest race in history,' the 1988 100 m final had only two out of a total of eight runners who remained clean throughout their careers (Calvin Smith and Robson da Silva)." In testimony before the Dubin inquiry, coach Charlie Francis charged that Johnson was only one of many cheaters, and he just happened to get caught [Wikipedia Ben Johnson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ben_Johnson_(Canadian_sprinter)).

**4.4 The Mitchell Report on Major League Baseball (2007)**

The Mitchell Report (formally "Report to the Commissioner of Baseball of an Independent Investigation into the Illegal Use of Steroids and Other Performance Enhancing Substances by Players in Major League Baseball") is a 409-page document released on December 13, 2007, resulting from former Democratic U.S. Senator George J. Mitchell's 20-month investigation. The report names 89 MLB players alleged to have used performance-enhancing drugs. It covers the history of illegal substance use in baseball, the effectiveness of MLB's drug prevention program, and recommendations for future prevention. The MLB Players Association was "largely uncooperative," advising players not to participate. Key witnesses included Kirk Radomski (a former Mets batboy who provided most names) and Brian McNamee (a personal trainer who alleged he injected Roger Clemens with steroids from 1998-2001). The report found that 5-7% of players tested positive for steroids in 2003 random testing, and after mandatory testing began in 2004, HGH became the substance of choice as it was undetectable. At least one player from each of the 30 MLB teams was implicated. The congressional hearing (January 15, 2008) on the Mitchell Report featured Chairman Henry Waxman stating: "The illegal use of steroids and performance-enhancing drugs was pervasive for more than a decade. Major League Baseball was slow and ineffective in responding to the scandal, and the use of Human Growth Hormone has been rising." The report documented widespread illegal steroid use, detailed backroom drug deals, players injecting each other in locker rooms, and efforts at obfuscation. The report found that MLB's medical director gave a 1998 presentation on the benefits of testosterone, and the league's security office failed to properly investigate steroid cases. The committee emphasized the connection between professional athletes' steroid use and the epidemic of steroid use among high school and college athletes, citing tragic cases like Efrain Marrero, a 19-year-old college athlete who committed suicide after using steroids. Frank and Brenda Marrero (his parents) stated: "Simple, honest accountability is all we're asking for... no family should have to endure the anguish we've suffered, but tens of thousands of youngsters are at risk" [Congress.gov](https://www.congress.gov/event/110th-congress/house-event/LC7395/text). After the Mitchell Report, MLB significantly increased testing and punishments, including testing for 74 performance-enhancing drugs (including HGH), 56 stimulants, and increasing suspensions to 80 games for a first offense, 162 games for a second, and lifetime ban for a third [Wikipedia Mitchell Report](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mitchell_Report).

**4.5 The BALCO Scandal**

The EBSCO article on steroids in baseball reports that in September 2003, federal investigators raided the Bay Area Laboratory Co-Operative (BALCO) in California, suspecting it supplied undetectable steroids to athletes. This led to a grand jury investigation involving Barry Bonds and Jason Giambi. The Hastings Center briefing notes that clandestine drug developers created undetectable substances (e.g., THG/"the clear" from the Balco lab) [Hastings Center](https://www.thehastingscenter.org/briefingbook/sports-enhancement). The HISTORY.com article notes that the BALCO scandal (2003) involved a U.S. lab that supplied undetectable designer steroid THG to athletes including Marion Jones (who surrendered five Olympic medals and served prison time) and Barry Bonds [HISTORY.com](https://www.history.com/articles/doping-scandals-through-history-list).

**Analysis:** The legal framework against doping, while imperfect, has had measurable effects. The WADA system has developed increasingly sophisticated detection methods, including the Athlete Biological Passport, longitudinal profiling, and intelligence-based investigations. The increase in ADRVs from 1,652 in 2022 to 2,005 in 2023 suggests that the system is becoming more effective at catching violators. The sanctions imposed — including loss of medals, public exposure, and bans from competition — have significant deterrent effects. The UK Government review found that the strongest deterrent for athletes is being banned from sport, not criminal sanctions [UK Government Review](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/5a82b2ece5274a2e8ab58e27/Review_of_Criminalisation_of_Doping_in_Sport.doc). The Mitchell Report and subsequent MLB reforms demonstrate that a robust anti-doping framework can change behavior: after the introduction of testing and meaningful penalties, the prevalence of steroid use in baseball declined significantly, and while HGH use increased as a substitute, the overall pattern shows that enforcement can shift behavior. The BALCO scandal demonstrates that the anti-doping system can uncover and prosecute sophisticated doping networks. Critics argue that the system only catches "the dopey dopers," but this underestimates the deterrent effect of even imperfect enforcement. The knowledge that one might be caught — and the severe consequences of being caught — likely deters many athletes who would otherwise consider doping. The alternative — legalization — would remove this deterrent entirely and potentially normalize the use of dangerous substances.

### Argument 5: Case Studies of Systemic Harm — The Real-World Consequences of Doping

**Thesis Statement:** The history of doping in elite sports, as documented through major scandals and investigations, demonstrates that doping causes systemic harm to athletes, teams, institutions, and the public trust, and that legalization would not eliminate these harms but would instead legitimize and expand them.

**Supporting Points and Evidence:**

**5.1 Lance Armstrong Doping Case**

The Wikipedia article on the Lance Armstrong doping case reports that the investigation by USADA led to Armstrong being stripped of his seven consecutive Tour de France titles (1999-2005) and one Olympic bronze medal, and his eventual admission of using performance-enhancing drugs. Key chronology: 2010-2012: U.S. federal prosecutors pursued doping allegations via a grand jury. June 2012: USADA accused Armstrong of doping and drug trafficking based on blood samples and witness testimonies. August 24, 2012: USADA announced Armstrong would be stripped of his titles and banned for life. October 10, 2012: USADA published a 200-page "Reasoned Decision" with testimonies from 11 former teammates and 15 other witnesses, portraying Armstrong as the ringleader of "the most sophisticated, professionalized and successful doping program that sport has ever seen." January 2013: Armstrong confessed to Oprah Winfrey, stating his "mythic, perfect story" was "one big lie." He later admitted he began doping in late spring of 1995 [Wikipedia Armstrong](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lance_Armstrong_doping_case). The USADA Reasoned Decision details an overwhelming doping scheme described as "more extensive than any previously revealed in professional sports history." Armstrong committed anti-doping rule violations including use/possession/trafficking/administration of EPO, testosterone, blood transfusions, corticosteroids, and masking agents from 1998-2010. The evidence includes sworn statements from 24+ witnesses (15 cyclists, 12 team staff), banking records showing $1M+ in payments to Dr. Michele Ferrari, and scientific blood test evidence. Key findings: Armstrong enforced a team-wide doping program, requiring teammates to dope or be replaced. He used Dr. Ferrari as a doping consultant, used blood transfusions in hotel rooms during the Tour de France, and engaged in witness intimidation and perjury. 20 of 21 Tour de France podium finishers from 1999-2005 were tied to likely doping. The court found that "the USADA arbitration rules, which largely follow those of the American Arbitration Association, are sufficiently robust to satisfy the requirements of due process" [USADA Reasoned Decision](https://www.usada.org/wp-content/uploads/ReasonedDecision.pdf). The Guardian article (2012) reports that Dr. Michele Ferrari prescribed a blend of testosterone and olive oil, managed blood-doping programs, and charged riders $15,000 per season. Armstrong is described as an "enforcer for Dr. Ferrari's doping plan," reprimanding riders who didn't follow the program closely [The Guardian](https://www.theguardian.com/sport/blog/2012/oct/11/lance-armstrong-denials-usada-detail).

**5.2 Ben Johnson's Disqualification**

The Wikipedia article on Ben Johnson reports that at the 1988 Seoul Olympics, Johnson won the 100m final in a world record of 9.79 seconds, but three days later tested positive for the anabolic steroid stanozolol and was stripped of his gold medal and world record. He later admitted to using steroids since 1981. His 1987 world record (9.83 seconds) was also rescinded. The scandal led to the Dubin Inquiry in Canada (1989), which investigated widespread drug use in sports. Coach Charlie Francis alleged that steroid use was rampant among top athletes, and that the 1988 100m final was "the dirtiest race in history" — only 2 of 8 runners remained clean throughout their careers (Calvin Smith and Robson da Silva). After a suspension, Johnson returned in 1991 but failed to qualify for the 1992 Olympics final. In 1993, he tested positive for excess testosterone and received a lifetime ban from the IAAF [Wikipedia Ben Johnson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ben_Johnson_(Canadian_sprinter)).

**5.3 Maria Sharapova's Suspension**

The ESPN article on Maria Sharapova (June 8, 2016) reports that Sharapova was suspended for two years by the International Tennis Federation (ITF) for testing positive for the banned substance meldonium at the 2016 Australian Open. She announced she would appeal to the Court of Arbitration for Sport. The ITF tribunal ruled that her violation was not intentional, rejecting the ITF's request for a four-year suspension, but found she bore "sole responsibility" and "very significant fault." She had taken the drug mildronate (meldonium) since 2006 for medical reasons, but it was added to the WADA banned list effective January 1, 2016. She also failed an out-of-competition test in Moscow on February 2, 2016. The suspension was retroactive to the Australian Open, meaning she would be eligible to return at the 2018 Australian Open [ESPN Sharapova](https://www.espn.com/tennis/story/_/id/16044538/maria-sharapova-suspended-two-years-international-tennis-federation-positive-drug-test-meldonium). The Guardian article on meldonium explains that the drug is used to treat ischaemia (lack of blood flow), particularly for angina or heart failure. It is manufactured in Latvia and distributed only in Baltic countries and Russia. It is not FDA-approved in the US or authorized in Europe. The drug increases blood flow and improves exercise capacity in athletes by carrying more oxygen to muscle tissue. WADA found "evidence of its use by athletes with the intention of enhancing performance." A 2015 study found that 17% of Russian athletes tested (724 of 4,316) had meldonium in their system, while a global study found 2.2% of athletes had it. Sharapova's lawyer argued the suspension was unfairly harsh and intended to make an example of her due to her fame: "I am disappointed that the ITF tribunal gave Maria an unfairly harsh suspension because she is such a famous athlete and they wanted to make an example out of her" [The Guardian Sharapova](https://www.theguardian.com/sport/2016/mar/08/meldonium-maria-sharapova-failed-drugs-test).

**5.4 East German Doping Program (Detailed in Section 1.4)**

**5.5 Russian State-Sponsored Doping**

The HISTORY.com article (2021) reports that the 2021-2022 Russia's Olympic ban was due to a state-sponsored doping scheme at the 2014 Sochi Olympics, leading to a two-year ban from the Court of Arbitration for Sport. Russian athletes competed but not under their flag or anthem. The World Anti-Doping Agency imposed a four-year ban on Russia for the Olympic Games and world championship sporting events (reduced to two years on appeal) [HISTORY.com](https://www.history.com/articles/doping-scandals-through-history-list). The Hastings Center briefing notes that Russia's organized doping and coverup at the 2014 Sochi Winter Olympics was documented, leading to sanctions against many Russian athletes [Hastings Center](https://www.thehastingscenter.org/briefingbook/sports-enhancement). The Wikipedia article on doping in athletics notes that following allegations of state-sponsored doping in Russia, the IAAF suspended the country's athletes from competition, including the 2016 Summer Olympics [Wikipedia List of doping cases](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_doping_cases_in_athletics).

**5.6 Festina Cycling Team Scandal (1998)**

The HISTORY.com article reports that in 1998, the Festina cycling team's masseur was caught with doping substances at the Tour de France. The scandal contributed to the creation of the World Anti-Doping Agency (WADA) in 1999 [HISTORY.com](https://www.history.com/articles/doping-scandals-through-history-list). The MDPI Religions article (Blazer, 2020) notes that Willy Voet (soigneur for the Swiss Festina team) was stopped with a variety of drugs, revealing widespread doping. Less than half the riders finished the race [MDPI Religions](https://www.mdpi.com/2077-1444/11/11/605).

**5.7 The Biogenesis Scandal (MLB, 2013)**

The HISTORY.com article reports that in 2013, a Florida clinic supplied MLB players with HGH and PEDs. Over a dozen players were suspended, including Alex Rodriguez (162 games) and Ryan Braun (65 games) [HISTORY.com](https://www.history.com/articles/doping-scandals-through-history-list).

**Analysis:** The case studies demonstrate that doping is not a victimless crime. The Armstrong case shows how a single athlete can corrupt an entire team, coercing teammates into doping and using intimidation to maintain silence. The East German program shows how a state can systematically exploit and harm thousands of young athletes. The Ben Johnson case shows how the pursuit of glory can lead to lifelong disgrace. The Sharapova case illustrates the complexities of unintentional doping and the difficulties of establishing intent. The Russian state-sponsored doping program shows how a nation can corrupt the Olympic movement and undermine the integrity of international competition. The Festina and Biogenesis scandals show how doping networks can operate across teams and sports. Each of these cases resulted in significant harm: to the athletes themselves (health consequences, loss of reputation, financial penalties), to the sport (loss of public trust, devaluation of achievements), and to clean athletes (who were denied opportunities and recognition). The argument that legalization would eliminate these harms is not supported by the evidence. The most egregious cases — East Germany, Russia — involved state-sponsored programs that would likely have continued regardless of legal status. The Armstrong case involved sophisticated doping that evaded detection for years. Legalization would not eliminate the incentives to dope or the potential for abuse; it would simply remove the legal consequences.

### Argument 6: Youth Impact, Role Modeling, and Cultural Values

**Thesis Statement:** Allowing performance-enhancing drugs in elite sports would send a dangerous message to young people about the acceptability of drug use, increase the already alarming rates of PED use among adolescents, and undermine the cultural value of sport as a vehicle for character development and healthy lifestyle promotion.

**Supporting Points and Evidence:**

**6.1 Youth Drug Use and the Role Model Effect**

The Britannica ProCon.org article argues that allowing PEDs will increase youth drug use — the average first use of PEDs is at age 14 — and encourage other dangerous behaviors among young fans who view athletes as role models [Britannica ProCon.org](https://www.britannica.com/procon/sports-and-drugs-debate). The Hastings Center briefing notes high rates of adolescent non-therapeutic drug use (especially for cosmetic effects like looking "buff") [Hastings Center](https://www.thehastingscenter.org/briefingbook/sports-enhancement). The AMA Journal of Ethics article notes that AAS have unique adverse outcomes in adolescents because they accelerate bone epiphysis maturation, leading to reduced matured height. The article also notes that PED use is growing rapidly among recreational users, bodybuilders, and school children [AMA Journal of Ethics](https://journalofethics.ama-assn.org/article/medical-ethics-and-performance-enhancing-drugs/2005-11). The Annals of Sports Medicine and Research article (PMC5215887) notes that ergogenic substance use is growing among the general population and amateur athletes (e.g., 6.6% of male high school students used steroids in a 1988 study), creating public health problems like needle-sharing and communicable disease transmission [PMC Annals](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5215887). The Endocrine Society Scientific Statement reports that at least 3 million PED users exist in the U.S. alone, and the vast majority are not competitive athletes but rather recreational bodybuilders. However, media attention has focused almost entirely on PED use by elite athletes [PMC Endocrine Society](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4026349).

**6.2 The Congressional Connection: Professional Athletes Influencing Youth**

The Mitchell Report congressional hearing (January 15, 2008) emphasized the connection between professional athletes' steroid use and the epidemic of steroid use among high school and college athletes. The hearing cited the tragic case of Efrain Marrero, a 19-year-old college athlete who committed suicide after using steroids. His parents, Frank and Brenda Marrero, stated: "Simple, honest accountability is all we're asking for... no family should have to endure the anguish we've suffered, but tens of thousands of youngsters are at risk." Ranking Member Tom Davis stated: "We want steroids and other dangerous drugs out of sports, period" [Congress.gov](https://www.congress.gov/event/110th-congress/house-event/LC7395/text).

**6.3 Philosophical Perspectives on Young Athletes and Doping**

The Frontiers in Sports and Active Living article (Teetzel, 2022) examines philosophical perspectives on doping sanctions for young athletes. The paper argues that age should matter more than it currently does in anti-doping rule violations, as young athletes lack the developed capacity for rational, independent decision-making that adults possess. Key cases discussed: Two 17-year-old wrestlers at the 2010 Youth Olympic Games who received 2-year bans and public disclosure despite being minors; 16-year-old gymnast Andreea Răducan losing her Olympic gold medal at Sydney 2000 for taking cold medicine given by her team physician; 15-year-old figure skater Kamila Valieva's positive test at the 2022 Beijing Olympics for trimetazidine, highlighting the "protected persons" category in WADA's Code; 12-year-old Igor Walilko who received an 18-month ban after a banned substance was traced to an energy bar. The paper argues that young athletes cannot exercise the "second-order capacities" needed for truly autonomous decision-making, especially when coercion from coaches, parents, and sport systems is present. Doping control procedures (urinalysis, blood testing, whereabouts programs) violate privacy, and it's unclear if minors can meaningfully consent to these violations. The author argues that "the moral responsibility ought to be on adults to protect children rather than on children to be precocious in the ways of the world before they are truly ready to meet those challenges" [PMC Philosophical Perspectives](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8971751).

**6.4 The "Spirit of Sport" and Cultural Values**

The Hastings Center briefing states: "We need a revolution in the governance of sport that demands honesty and accountability from all actors." The briefing notes that public reaction to doping is split: some categorically condemn it as cheating, while others see entertainment value (e.g., more home runs) [Hastings Center](https://www.thehastingscenter.org/briefingbook/sports-enhancement). The WADA Code states that doping is fundamentally contrary to the "spirit of sport," which encompasses values including ethics, fair play, honesty, health, excellence, character, teamwork, and respect. The MDPI Religions article (Blazer, 2020) argues that American culture tends to preserve the idea that sport is morally valuable ("character-building") and therefore hold athletes to a high moral standard. However, athletes tend to see doping as a systemic problem, rather than an individual problem, and therefore they are disinclined to individual confession. When rituals of confession fail, they reveal less noble structural motivations like monetary reward, intense pressure to perform, and the entertainment demands of elite sport [MDPI Religions](https://www.mdpi.com/2077-1444/11/11/605).

**Analysis:** The youth impact argument may be the most socially consequential of the six arguments against PEDs. Elite athletes are powerful role models, and their behavior — both on and off the field — shapes the attitudes and behaviors of young people. The evidence that the average age of first PED use is 14 is deeply concerning, and the connection between professional athletes' PED use and youth use was a central concern of the Mitchell Report's congressional hearing. The tragic case of Efrain Marrero, who committed suicide after using steroids, illustrates the stakes involved. The argument that legalization would reduce youth use by removing the "forbidden fruit" factor is speculative and contradicted by evidence from other areas of drug policy. In jurisdictions that have legalized cannabis, for example, youth use has generally increased or remained stable, not decreased. Furthermore, the cultural value of sport as a vehicle for character development — teaching discipline, teamwork, perseverance, and fair play — would be undermined if the message to young people is that anything goes in the pursuit of victory. The Teetzel article's argument that young athletes cannot meaningfully consent to doping is particularly important, as it highlights the vulnerability of minors in high-performance sport systems where the pressure to dope can be intense.

## Conclusion

The debate over whether performance-enhancing drugs and technologies should be allowed in elite sports is not a simple binary choice between prohibition and permissiveness, but rather a complex question that requires careful consideration of competing values. The six pro arguments — athlete autonomy, harm reduction, leveling the playing field, enhancement as the spirit of sport, the incoherence of current bans, and the lessons of prohibition failure — each raise important points that challenge the legitimacy and effectiveness of the current anti-doping regime. The evidence that doping is far more prevalent than official statistics suggest, that the current system punishes the caught rather than the guilty, and that prohibition drives doping underground where it becomes more dangerous, are serious concerns that cannot be dismissed.

However, the six con arguments — health risks, cheating and integrity, the arms race and unequal access, the legal framework, case studies of systemic harm, and youth impact — present equally compelling reasons for maintaining the prohibition on PEDs. The documented deaths from doping, the devastating health consequences for athletes, the coercion of clean athletes into a pharmaceutical arms race, and the powerful influence of elite athletes on youth behavior are all weighty considerations.

The most defensible position may be a middle ground that acknowledges the failures of the current system while recognizing the dangers of full legalization. The Stanford enterprise liability proposal offers a promising framework for reforming the system without abandoning it entirely, shifting responsibility from individual athletes to the organizations that profit from their performances. The Enhanced Games, whatever one thinks of its ethics, has opened a space for discussing alternative models. The distinction between physiological and mental doping proposed by Savulescu offers a principled basis for drawing lines. The recognition that young athletes require special protection is essential.

Ultimately, the question of whether PEDs should be allowed in sports is not just a question about drugs and technology — it is a question about what kind of sport we want to have, and what values we want sport to represent. The answer will depend on whether we value sport primarily as a spectacle of human excellence achieved through natural talent and dedication, or as a venue for exploring the limits of human performance through any available means. These are fundamentally different visions of sport, and the debate between them will continue to shape the future of athletic competition.
