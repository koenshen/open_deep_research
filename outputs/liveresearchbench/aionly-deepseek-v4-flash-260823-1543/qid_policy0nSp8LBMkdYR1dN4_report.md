# Cross-Sector Analysis of Cybersecurity Incident Disclosure Frameworks (2024–2026): CISA CIRCIA, SEC Rules, and NERC CIP

## Executive Summary

This report provides a comprehensive cross-sector analysis comparing three major regulatory frameworks governing cybersecurity incident disclosure in the United States: the Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency's (CISA) Cyber Incident Reporting for Critical Infrastructure Act (CIRCIA) regulations, the Securities and Exchange Commission's (SEC) cybersecurity disclosure rules, and the North American Electric Reliability Corporation's (NERC) Critical Infrastructure Protection (CIP) standards. The analysis covers the most current compliance challenges from 2024 through 2026, focusing on reporting timelines, covered-entity definitions, enforcement mechanisms, and penalty structures across energy, finance, healthcare, and other critical infrastructure sectors.

The landscape reveals a rapidly evolving and increasingly fragmented regulatory environment. CIRCIA's final rule remains pending (now expected September 2026) after multiple delays, while the SEC's rules face mounting political pressure for repeal or substantial revision. Meanwhile, NERC CIP continues to undergo significant modernization, with FERC approving multiple new and revised standards addressing virtualization, internal network security monitoring, and supply chain risk management. Organizations operating across multiple sectors face overlapping, sometimes conflicting, reporting obligations that require sophisticated coordination and compliance infrastructure.

## 1. Regulatory Framework Descriptions

### 1.1 CISA CIRCIA Regulations

The Cyber Incident Reporting for Critical Infrastructure Act of 2022 (CIRCIA) represents the most significant expansion of mandatory federal cyber incident reporting in U.S. history. Enacted in response to attacks like the Colonial Pipeline ransomware incident, CIRCIA requires the Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency (CISA) to develop regulations requiring covered entities to report covered cyber incidents and ransomware payments. [CISA Official Page](https://www.cisa.gov/topics/cyber-threats-and-advisories/information-sharing/cyber-incident-reporting-critical-infrastructure-act-2022-circia)

CISA published its Notice of Proposed Rulemaking (NPRM) on April 4, 2024 (89 FR 23644), proposing regulations codified at 6 CFR Part 226. [Federal Register NPRM](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2024/04/04/2024-06526/cyber-incident-reporting-for-critical-infrastructure-act-circia-reporting-requirements) The statutory deadline for the final rule was October 4, 2025, but CISA missed this deadline. The Spring 2025 Unified Agenda scheduled publication for May 2026, and the most recent update now expects the final rule in September 2026. [Hunton Andrews Kurth](https://www.hunton.com/privacy-and-cybersecurity-law-blog/cisa-plans-to-finalize-cyber-incident-reporting-regulations-in-september-2026)

CISA held virtual town hall meetings from June 15-18, 2026, with more than 1,200 stakeholders participating across four days of sessions. [Hunton Andrews Kurth](https://www.hunton.com/privacy-and-cybersecurity-law-blog/cisa-plans-to-finalize-cyber-incident-reporting-regulations-in-september-2026) As of August 2026, reporting is not yet mandatory and will only take effect after the final rule is published.

### 1.2 SEC Cybersecurity Disclosure Rules

On July 26, 2023, the SEC voted 3-2 to adopt final rules requiring registrants to provide enhanced and standardized disclosures regarding cybersecurity risk management, strategy, governance, and incidents. The rules were published in Release No. 33-11216 (Cybersecurity Risk Management, Strategy, Governance, and Incident Disclosure) and became effective on September 5, 2023. [SEC Final Rule](https://www.sec.gov/resources-small-businesses/small-business-compliance-guides/cybersecurity-risk-management-strategy-governance-incident-disclosure)

The rules have two main components: (1) current reporting on Form 8-K (Item 1.05) for material cybersecurity incidents, required within four business days of materiality determination, and (2) periodic disclosure in annual reports (Regulation S-K Item 106) covering cybersecurity risk management, strategy, governance, and board oversight. [Deloitte Heads Up](https://dart.deloitte.com/USDART/home/publications/deloitte/heads-up/2023/sec-rule-cyber-disclosures)

The SEC has issued multiple interpretive guidance documents, including a May 21, 2024 statement by Director Erik Gerding clarifying the appropriate use of Item 1.05 versus Item 8.01 for voluntary disclosures, [WilmerHale](https://www.wilmerhale.com/en/insights/blogs/keeping-current-disclosure-and-governance-developments/20240522-sec-staff-clarifies-form-8-k-item-105-is-for-cybersecurity-incidents-that-are-determined-to-be-material) and five new Compliance and Disclosure Interpretations (C&DIs) on June 24, 2024 addressing ransomware payments and materiality. [Akin Gump](https://www.akingump.com/en/insights/blogs/ag-deal-diary/sec-publishes-five-canddis-covering-cybersecurity-incident-disclosures-pursuant-to-item-105-of-form-8-k)

The rules face significant political headwinds. The House Financial Services Committee urged the SEC to repeal the rules on March 31, 2025, [Free Writings & Perspectives](https://www.freewritings.law/2025/04/house-financial-services-committee-urges-sec-withdrawal-of-14-proposed-and-final-rules) and a group of banking associations petitioned the SEC to rescind Item 1.05 on May 22, 2025. [DLA Piper](https://marketedge.dlapiper.com/2025/06/the-future-of-the-secs-cybersecurity-disclosure-rules) In January 2026, the SEC requested comments on Regulation S-K, and industry groups have called for repeal of Item 1.05.

### 1.3 NERC CIP Standards

The North American Electric Reliability Corporation (NERC) Critical Infrastructure Protection (CIP) standards are a set of mandatory, FERC-enforceable cybersecurity and physical security standards for the Bulk Electric System (BES) in North America. [ReliaMag](https://reliamag.com/guides/nerc-cip-compliance) NERC was designated as the Electric Reliability Organization (ERO) following the Energy Policy Act of 2005, and the first CIP standard was approved by FERC in 2008.

The current standards framework includes CIP-002 (BES Cyber System Categorization) through CIP-015 (Internal Network Security Monitoring). [American Power Engineers](https://americanpowerengineers.com/blog/nerc-cip-standards-overview) Major recent developments include:

- **CIP-015-1** (Internal Network Security Monitoring), effective September 2, 2025, with phased compliance through September 2030 [Fortinet](https://www.fortinet.com/resources/cyberglossary/nerc-cip)
- **FERC Order No. 919** (March 24, 2026), approving 11 modified CIP standards enabling secure application of virtualization technologies, with full compliance by July 1, 2028 [Federal Register](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2026/03/24/2026-05716/order-no-919-virtualization-reliability-standards)
- **CIP-003-11** (Security Management Controls for low-impact BES Cyber Systems), approved by FERC Order No. 918 on March 19, 2026, with effective date July 1, 2029 [VComply](https://www.v-comply.com/blog/nerc-cip-requirements)
- **CIP-002-8**, approved March 19, 2026, improving risk identification of Transmission Owner Control Centers [Fortress Information Security](https://www.fortressinfosec.com/resources/reports/april-2026-compliance-report)
- **FERC Order No. 912** (September 18, 2025), directing NERC to submit new/modified standards within 18 months addressing supply chain risk management gaps [Davis Wright Tremaine](https://www.dwt.com/blogs/energy--environmental-law-blog/2025/09/ferc-orders-reshape-bulk-power-system-reliability)

## 3. Real-World Compliance Challenges per Framework

### 3.1 CISA CIRCIA Compliance Challenges

**A. Broad Scope and Industry Criticism**

CISA's proposed rule drew significant criticism for defining "covered entities" too broadly. The NPRM estimated over 300,000 entities across 16 critical infrastructure sectors would be subject to requirements, many of which have not traditionally considered themselves critical infrastructure. [Paul, Weiss](https://www.paulweiss.com/insights/client-memos/cisa-issues-highly-anticipated-far-reaching-rules-for-cyber-incident-reporting)

During the June 2026 town halls, stakeholders were highly critical of the proposed definition of "covered entities," raising concerns about: (1) the broad scope (potentially 316,000+ entities), (2) duplication with existing federal and state reporting requirements, and (3) misalignment of proposed definitions with real-world incident response. [Davis Wright Tremaine](https://www.dwt.com/blogs/privacy--security-law-blog/2026/07/trust-issues-july-2026)

Commenters expressed significant concerns about the number and scope of companies covered, a definition of reportable cyber incident that would flood CISA with low-impact reporting, the lack of substantial steps to harmonize CIRCIA requirements with other cybersecurity regulatory requirements, and expansive proposed reporting requirements that would gather sensitive information from victim companies. [REN-ISAC](https://www.ren-isac.net/services/publications/alerts/CIRCIA_Report_Requirements_Alert.html)

**B. Regulatory Overlap and Conflicts**

A report conducted under CIRCIA identified 52 in-effect or proposed incident reporting requirements at the federal level alone, in addition to data breach reporting laws enacted by every U.S. state and multiple territories. [Davis Wright Tremaine](https://www.dwt.com/blogs/privacy--security-law-blog/2026/02/cisa-seeks-input-cybersecurity-reporting-rules)

The proposed rules would require faster and more detailed reporting than many existing incident reporting regimes. The bar for reporting incidents to CISA under the NPRM will be significantly lower than the SEC rule, requiring covered entities to report many more incidents. [Paul, Weiss](https://www.paulweiss.com/insights/client-memos/cisa-issues-highly-anticipated-far-reaching-rules-for-cyber-incident-reporting)

CIRCIA does not replace existing obligations (HIPAA, SEC, state breach laws) but adds to them. Key differences include:
- CIRCIA's 72-hour reporting deadline vs. HIPAA's 60-day breach notification window
- CIRCIA requires ransomware payment reporting within 24 hours, which HIPAA does not
- CIRCIA reports go to CISA, while HIPAA notifications go to HHS OCR plus affected individuals
- For electric utilities, CIRCIA adds a new layer of reporting on top of existing NERC CIP requirements (CIP-008 with different timelines and processes) [Elisity](https://www.elisity.com/blog/circia-healthcare-compliance-guide-new-regulations-critical-controls-for-2026)

**C. Compliance Preparation Burdens**

Organizations face significant preparation challenges, including: determining coverage status, assigning incident response ownership, auditing response plans, investing in detection/monitoring, mapping supply chain exposure, building data retention practices (2-year preservation requirement), coordinating reporting obligations across multiple regulatory frameworks, updating incident response playbooks with CIRCIA procedures, and setting internal escalation timelines (within 24 hours). [Fisher Phillips](https://www.fisherphillips.com/en/insights/insights/new-federal-cybersecurity-reporting-rules-are-on-their-way)

**D. Final Rule Delays Create Uncertainty**

The statutory deadline for the final rule was October 4, 2025, but CISA first delayed publication to May 2026, then further to September 2026. [MIT Urban Cyber Defense Blog](https://urbancyberdefense.mit.edu/blog/cisa-moves-circia-deadline-to-may-2026) This extended period of regulatory uncertainty makes it difficult for organizations to allocate resources and build compliance programs.

### 3.2 SEC Cybersecurity Disclosure Rules Compliance Challenges

**A. Materiality Determination Challenges**

The most significant compliance challenge is determining whether a cybersecurity incident is "material" within the required timeframe. The SEC's May 21, 2024 statement emphasized that registrants should consider qualitative factors alongside quantitative factors, including harm to reputation, customer or vendor relationships, competitiveness, and the possibility of litigation or regulatory investigations. [WilmerHale](https://www.wilmerhale.com/en/insights/blogs/keeping-current-disclosure-and-governance-developments/20240522-sec-staff-clarifies-form-8-k-item-105-is-for-cybersecurity-incidents-that-are-determined-to-be-material)

First-year filing statistics (December 2023 – December 2024) revealed significant variation in practice: only 14% of filings stated the incident was material, 33% stated it was immaterial, 28% undetermined, and 25% a mixture. [NYU Compliance and Enforcement](https://wp.nyu.edu/compliance_enforcement/2025/03/25/lessons-learned-one-year-of-form-8-k-material-cybersecurity-incident-reporting)

**B. The SEC's "Sweep" Review and Enforcement Actions**

The SEC conducted a "sweep" review issuing 14 comment letters focusing on proper use of Item 1.05 and expanding disclosure beyond financial impacts. [Cleary Gottlieb](https://www.clearygottlieb.com/news-and-insights/publication-listing/cybersecurity-disclosure-and-enforcement-developments-and-predictions)

Major enforcement actions demonstrate the SEC's aggressive posture:

- **SolarWinds Corp. and Timothy Brown (CISO):** The SEC's first litigated action against a public company and its CISO concerning cybersecurity disclosures. On July 18, 2024, the SDNY court dismissed bulk of the claims, holding that (1) cybersecurity controls are not internal accounting controls under Section 13(b)(2)(B), (2) generic cybersecurity puffery is not actionable, and (3) the SEC's statutory authority does not extend to regulating corporate cybersecurity. [Cooley](https://investigations.cooley.com/2024/07/25/federal-court-dismisses-bulk-of-secs-complaint-against-solarwinds-in-cyberattack-case) The SEC dismissed the remainder of the case with prejudice on November 20, 2025. [Hunton Andrews Kurth](https://www.hunton.com/privacy-and-cybersecurity-law-blog/sec-dismisses-remainder-of-solarwinds-case)

- **R.R. Donnelley & Sons Co. (June 2024):** $2.125 million penalty for cybersecurity-related controls violations. The SEC treated IT systems as an "asset" under internal accounting controls provision, prompting two commissioners to dissent. [SEC Press Release](https://www.sec.gov/newsroom/press-releases/2024-75)

- **Four SolarWinds Downstream Victims (October 2024):** Unisys ($4M), Avaya ($1M), Check Point ($990K), and Mimecast ($990K) penalized for misleading cybersecurity disclosures. The SEC alleged they used generic, hypothetical risk language after actual breaches or omitted material information. [Davis Polk](https://www.davispolk.com/insights/client-update/sec-charges-public-companies-inadequate-disclosures-aftermath-solarwinds)

- **Flagstar Financial (December 2024):** $3.55 million penalty for misleading statements about its 2021 "Citrix Breach."

**C. The Change Healthcare Incident (February 2024)**

The cyberattack on UnitedHealth Group's Change Healthcare subsidiary was the largest healthcare data breach in U.S. history, affecting 192.7 million people. The attack occurred on February 21, 2024, perpetrated by the ALPHV/BlackCat ransomware group. Attackers gained access via a Citrix portal lacking multi-factor authentication, spending 9 days inside the network before deploying ransomware. UHG reportedly paid a $22 million Bitcoin ransom. [Senate HELP Committee](https://www.help.senate.gov/2024-05-14-letter-from-bc-to-uhg-andrew-witty-re-change-cyberattack)

Total cost to UHG: approximately $3.1 billion in direct breach response costs through 2024. The incident caused massive disruptions: pharmacies faced challenges dispensing medications, electronic prescribing and claim processing were halted, and providers went weeks without pay. [American Hospital Association](https://www.aha.org/change-healthcare-cyberattack-underscores-urgent-need-strengthen-cyber-preparedness-individual-health-care-organizations-and)

**D. Timing Conflicts and the AT&T Precedent**

AT&T became the only company to use the national security/public safety delay provision, filing its 8-K 84 days after detection. The national-security delays were invoked twice, both by AT&T in 2024. The FBI provided guidance on requesting delays, noting early outreach is critical and that the FBI will not process requests unless received immediately upon a company's determination that a cybersecurity incident is material. [Cleary Gottlieb](https://www.clearygottlieb.com/news-and-insights/publication-listing/cybersecurity-disclosure-and-enforcement-developments-and-predictions)

**E. Political Pressure and Potential Repeal**

The rules face mounting challenges. Between January and July 2025, there were only seven Item 1.05 filings, compared to 19 during the same period in 2024. There have been no publicly disclosed enforcement actions initiated by the SEC under the cyber rules in 2025. [DLA Piper](https://marketedge.dlapiper.com/2025/06/the-future-of-the-secs-cybersecurity-disclosure-rules)

### 3.3 NERC CIP Compliance Challenges

**A. Supply Chain Risk Management (CIP-013)**

FERC Order No. 912 (September 18, 2025) directed NERC to submit new/modified standards within 18 months addressing gaps in CIP standards related to supply chain risk management. [Davis Wright Tremaine](https://www.dwt.com/blogs/energy--environmental-law-blog/2025/09/ferc-orders-reshape-bulk-power-system-reliability)

Common CIP-013 compliance challenges include: siloed procurement and cybersecurity processes, inconsistent vendor risk assessments, weak contract language, limited visibility into vendor access, poor tracking of vendor incidents, manual evidence collection, and difficulty proving implementation during audits. [VComply](https://www.v-comply.com/blog/nerc-cip-013-cyber-security-requirements)

Project 2025-06 (Supply Chain Risk Management) responds to FERC Order No. 912 by modifying CIP-005, CIP-010, and CIP-013 to address gaps in identifying, assessing, and responding to supply chain risks. The initial ballot was conducted July 8-17, 2026. [NERC Project 2025-06](https://www.nerc.com/standards/reliability-standards-under-development/2025-06-supply-chain-risk-management)

**B. Transitioning to New Standards Versions**

The compliance environment changed significantly in early 2026. FERC approved five new reliability standards for inverter-based resources, updated the CIP control center definition, finalized rules on virtualization, and introduced mandatory cybersecurity controls for low-impact BES Cyber Systems, all within weeks of one another. [Fortress Information Security](https://www.fortressinfosec.com/resources/reports/april-2026-compliance-report)

Key transitions include:
- **CIP-003-9** enforcement began April 1, 2026, expanding governance requirements for low-impact BES Cyber Systems. Vendor remote access at smaller substations and remote facilities is identified as the most likely area of non-compliance. [Fortress Information Security](https://www.fortressinfosec.com/resources/reports/april-2026-compliance-report)
- **CIP-015-1** (Internal Network Security Monitoring) effective September 2, 2025, with phased compliance: September 2, 2028 for high-impact and control center medium-impact systems; September 2, 2030 for other medium-impact systems. [Fortinet](https://www.fortinet.com/resources/cyberglossary/nerc-cip)
- **Virtualization Standards (Order No. 919):** Entities must comply with 11 updated CIP standards (CIP-002-7 through CIP-013-3) by July 1, 2028. [Federal Register](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2026/03/24/2026-05716/order-no-919-virtualization-reliability-standards)

A NERC white paper (December 2024) identified major barriers to adopting new technologies while maintaining CIP compliance, including: workforce shortages, financing limitations, rigid compliance approaches, and a standards development process that lags behind technology innovation. [NERC White Paper](https://www.nerc.com/globalassets/our-work/white-papers/sites_whitepaper_newtechfieldtesting.pdf)

**C. Internal Network Security Monitoring (CIP-015)**

CIP-015-1 was specifically adopted to address the lack of internal visibility in existing CIP standards. Firewalls protect network edges, but CIP-015-1 ensures visibility and helps detect lateral movement and internal threats that traditional perimeter defenses may overlook. [Fortinet](https://www.fortinet.com/resources/cyberglossary/nerc-cip)

**D. Transient Electronic Devices (TEDs) and Operational Conflicts**

The management of Transient Electronic Devices (laptops, USB drives, test equipment, contractor devices) that connect to BES Cyber Systems remains a significant compliance challenge, particularly for remote substations and field operations. [VComply](https://www.v-comply.com/blog/nerc-cip-requirements)

Conflicts between real-time operational needs and cybersecurity controls create inherent tensions:
- 15-30+ year lifecycles for equipment
- Limited patching windows (can't take critical systems offline during operation)
- Proprietary protocols (Modbus, DNP3, IEC 61850) that complicate security monitoring
- Operational consequences from security tool deployment
- Patches must be evaluated at least once every 35 calendar days under CIP-007 [ReliaMag](https://reliamag.com/guides/nerc-cip-compliance)

**E. Idaho National Laboratory Report on Gaps Beyond NERC CIP Perimeters**

A December 2025 Idaho National Laboratory report (INL/RPT-25-89588) identified a critical cybersecurity gap: while NERC CIP standards secure equipment within facility perimeters, all communications infrastructure beyond Electronic Access Points (EAPs) remains largely unregulated. This includes control center-to-substation communications, dispatchable distribution-level generation signals, inter-substation SCADA traffic, and third-party telecom provider networks. [Idaho National Laboratory](https://csdet.inl.gov/content/uploads/45/2026/03/Addressing-Gaps-Beyond-NERC-CIP-Facility-Perimeters.pdf)

**F. Most-Reported Violations**

NERC's CIP standards were the most-reported violations, with five standards accounting for more than 800 noncompliances. The most violated standards include: CIP-003 (Security Management Controls), CIP-004 (Personnel & Training), CIP-007 (System Security Management), CIP-010 (Configuration Change Management), and CIP-014 (Physical Security). [TRC Companies](https://www.trccompanies.com/insights/seven-most-violated-nerc-standards-and-how-to-maintain-compliance)

## 4. Strategic Implications for U.S.-Based and Global Enterprises

### 4.1 The Compliance Overlap Challenge

Organizations operating across multiple critical infrastructure sectors face a daunting compliance landscape. A single cybersecurity incident could trigger reporting obligations under CIRCIA (72 hours), SEC rules (4 business days after materiality determination), NERC CIP (1 hour for reportable incidents), HIPAA (60 days), and numerous state breach notification laws (varying from 30-60 days). This creates significant operational complexity, as incident response teams must simultaneously triage the incident, assess materiality under multiple standards, and prepare parallel filings with different timelines, content requirements, and receiving agencies.

The harmonization challenge is particularly acute. CIRCIA includes a "substantially similar" reporting exception, but CISA cannot assess which reporting programs may be eligible until the final rule is published. [REN-ISAC](https://www.ren-isac.net/services/publications/alerts/CIRCIA_Report_Requirements_Alert.html) Organizations should expect to maintain separate compliance workstreams for each framework for the foreseeable future.

### 4.2 Strategic Recommendations for Multi-Sector Enterprises

**A. Develop a Unified Incident Response and Disclosure Framework**

Organizations should build a single incident response framework that maps all applicable reporting obligations across federal, state, and sector-specific requirements. This framework should include:
- Pre-documented materiality determination criteria and escalation protocols
- Templates for each required filing (CIRCIA, SEC 8-K, NERC CIP-008, HIPAA, state breach notices)
- Internal timelines that are more aggressive than the shortest regulatory deadline (e.g., 1-hour internal notification to enable NERC CIP compliance)
- Designated cross-functional teams (legal, compliance, IT security, public relations, investor relations)
- Regular tabletop exercises testing coordinated disclosure under multiple frameworks simultaneously

**B. Invest in Compliance Technology and Automation**

The volume of reporting obligations requires automated compliance management systems. Key capabilities include:
- Centralized incident tracking with automated timeline calculations for each regulatory deadline
- Document management for evidence preservation (CIRCIA requires 2-year records retention)
- Workflow automation for internal escalation and approval processes
- Integration with security monitoring tools for real-time incident detection and classification
- Vendor risk management platforms for supply chain compliance (CIP-013, CIRCIA third-party provisions)

**C. Address the Governance Gap**

The SEC's rules require clear disclosure of board roles and responsibilities in cybersecurity, while NERC CIP requires documented security management controls. The SEC staff has noted that mere statements that a process exists are insufficient; disclosure must clearly describe the process and individual expertise within management groups. [Deloitte Heads Up](https://dart.deloitte.com/USDART/home/publications/deloitte/heads-up/2023/sec-rule-cyber-disclosures)

Organizations should ensure:
- Board-level cybersecurity expertise and oversight
- Clear management accountability structures
- Documented incident response governance
- Regular reporting to board on cybersecurity risk posture
- Integration of cybersecurity into enterprise risk management frameworks

### 4.3 Sector-Specific Strategic Implications

**For Energy Sector (Dual NERC CIP + CIRCIA + SEC Coverage)**

Energy sector entities face the most complex compliance burden, potentially subject to all three frameworks simultaneously. An electric utility that is a public company and operates in multiple states must:
- Report incidents to E-ISAC within 1 hour (CIP-008-8)
- File with SEC within 4 business days of materiality determination
- Report to CISA within 72 hours (once CIRCIA final rule takes effect)
- Comply with state breach notification laws
- Manage TSA pipeline directives if applicable

The NERC CIP framework is increasingly serving as a foundational model for emerging OT cybersecurity regulations globally. [ReliaMag](https://reliamag.com/guides/nerc-cip-compliance) Entities should invest in robust OT security programs that exceed minimum compliance requirements, as these will likely serve as the baseline for future regulatory expectations.

**For Financial Sector (SEC + CIRCIA Dominance)**

Financial institutions face the highest exposure from SEC enforcement, given the SEC's demonstrated willingness to pursue enforcement actions against companies for inadequate cybersecurity disclosures. The banking association petition to rescind Item 1.05 (May 2025) reflects industry concern about conflicts with confidential reporting, interference with law enforcement, market confusion, and extortion by ransomware criminals. [DLA Piper](https://marketedge.dlapiper.com/2025/06/the-future-of-the-secs-cybersecurity-disclosure-rules)

Financial institutions should:
- Maintain robust documentation of materiality determinations
- Ensure disclosure controls and procedures specifically address cybersecurity incidents
- Develop clear policies for when to use Item 1.05 versus Item 8.01
- Document all qualitative factors considered in materiality assessments
- Prepare for CIRCIA compliance, which will apply to financial services entities meeting size-based criteria

**For Healthcare Sector (HIPAA + CIRCIA + SEC)**

Healthcare entities face a unique challenge: HIPAA's 60-day breach notification window is significantly more generous than CIRCIA's 72-hour requirement, but HIPAA requires notification to affected individuals, which CIRCIA does not. [Elisity](https://www.elisity.com/blog/circia-healthcare-compliance-guide-new-regulations-critical-controls-for-2026)

The Change Healthcare incident demonstrated the catastrophic consequences of cybersecurity failures in healthcare. Key lessons for the sector include:
- MFA implementation is critical; the breach occurred via a Citrix portal lacking MFA despite FBI/CISA/HHS joint advisory
- Third-party risk management is essential; the breach cascaded through the healthcare ecosystem
- Business continuity planning must account for extended outages
- Incident response coordination with federal agencies (FBI, CISA, HHS) is critical

### 4.4 The Evolving Regulatory Landscape

**A. Uncertainty Around CIRCIA Final Rule**

The delay of the CIRCIA final rule from October 2025 to September 2026 creates significant compliance uncertainty. Organizations should not wait for the final rule to prepare, but should:
- Conduct a gap analysis against the NPRM to determine likely coverage status
- Begin building incident response capabilities that meet the 72-hour deadline
- Establish relationships with CISA (voluntary reporting is encouraged)
- Prepare for the 2-year records preservation requirement
- Develop processes for coordinating with other federal agencies on substantially similar reporting

**B. Political Threats to SEC Rules**

The SEC's cybersecurity disclosure rules face significant political headwinds. The House Financial Services Committee has urged repeal, and the banking industry has petitioned for rescission of Item 1.05. [Free Writings & Perspectives](https://www.freewritings.law/2025/04/house-financial-services-committee-urges-sec-withdrawal-of-14-proposed-and-final-rules) However, as of August 2026, the rules remain in effect. Organizations should continue to comply while monitoring developments, but should also consider the possibility that the rules could be modified or rescinded.

**C. NERC CIP Modernization**

NERC CIP continues to evolve rapidly, with FERC approving multiple new standards and revisions. The emphasis on supply chain risk management (FERC Order No. 912), internal network security monitoring (CIP-015), and virtualization (Order No. 919) indicates that the regulatory framework is becoming more comprehensive and aligned with modern cybersecurity practices. [Fortress Information Security](https://www.fortressinfosec.com/resources/reports/april-2026-compliance-report)

### 4.5 Global Implications for Enterprises Operating Under U.S. Jurisdiction

Foreign private issuers (FPIs) face parallel SEC disclosure requirements via amendments to Forms 20-F and 6-K. [SEC Small Entity Compliance Guide](https://www.sec.gov/resources-small-businesses/small-business-compliance-guides/cybersecurity-risk-management-strategy-governance-incident-disclosure) CIRCIA's broad scope would also apply to foreign entities that operate in U.S. critical infrastructure sectors and meet size-based criteria.

Global enterprises should:
- Map all U.S. regulatory obligations alongside home-country requirements
- Prepare for potential conflicts between U.S. reporting timelines and foreign data protection laws (e.g., GDPR)
- Develop a global incident response framework that accommodates multiple jurisdictional requirements
- Consider the extraterritorial reach of U.S. regulations, particularly CIRCIA, which applies to entities "in" critical infrastructure sectors
- Monitor for harmonization efforts between U.S. and international cybersecurity standards

---

## 5. Conclusion

The period from 2024 to 2026 represents a watershed moment for cybersecurity incident disclosure regulation in the United States. Three major frameworks—CIRCIA, SEC rules, and NERC CIP—are converging to create a complex, multi-layered compliance environment that demands sophisticated organizational responses.

The key challenges include: (1) overlapping and sometimes conflicting reporting timelines that require coordinated incident response and disclosure processes; (2) broad and sometimes ambiguous covered-entity definitions that create uncertainty about which organizations are subject to which requirements; (3) aggressive enforcement postures, particularly from the SEC, that create significant legal and financial risk; and (4) a rapidly evolving regulatory landscape where rules are being modified, delayed, or potentially repealed.

Organizations that invest in unified compliance frameworks, automated incident response capabilities, and robust governance structures will be best positioned to navigate this challenging environment. The strategic imperative is clear: cybersecurity incident disclosure is no longer just a technical or legal compliance issue—it is a fundamental business risk that requires board-level attention, cross-functional coordination, and significant resource investment.

The next 12-18 months will be critical. The CIRCIA final rule is expected in September 2026, NERC CIP compliance deadlines for new standards are approaching, and the SEC's rules face potential modification or repeal. Organizations should maintain flexibility in their compliance programs while continuing to invest in the core capabilities that will be required regardless of the specific regulatory framework: robust incident detection and response, clear materiality determination processes, and effective coordination across legal, security, and business functions.
