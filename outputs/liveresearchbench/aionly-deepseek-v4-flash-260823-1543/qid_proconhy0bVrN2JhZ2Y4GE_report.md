# Social Media and European Teenagers: A Comprehensive Analysis of Societal Impact

## Executive Summary

This report examines whether social media is "good for society" through the specific lens of European teenagers aged 13-18. Drawing on evidence from the past five years (2021-2026), with emphasis on European studies, government reports, and regulatory developments, the analysis evaluates social media's impact across four dimensions: mental health and well-being, educational and developmental outcomes, social and civic engagement, and safety and risk factors. The report presents six pro-social media arguments and six con-social media arguments, each supported by multiple lines of credible evidence.

The evidence reveals a deeply nuanced picture. Social media offers demonstrable benefits for social connection, identity formation (particularly for marginalized youth), civic engagement, and educational access. However, it simultaneously poses significant risks to mental health, sleep, academic performance, and personal safety, with problematic use rising sharply across Europe. The regulatory landscape is evolving rapidly, with the EU Digital Services Act, UK Online Safety Act, and Nordic national initiatives creating new frameworks for protection, though implementation remains uneven. The overall assessment depends critically on usage patterns, individual vulnerabilities, and the effectiveness of regulatory safeguards.

# Part One: Pro-Social Media Arguments

## PRO Argument 1: Social Media Reduces Loneliness and Provides Social Support for European Teenagers

### Thesis Statement

Moderate, active social media use enhances social connectedness, reduces loneliness, and provides emotional support networks for European teenagers, particularly when used to maintain existing friendships and access peer communities.

### Supporting Evidence

**WHO/HBSC Study (2024) — Heavy but Non-Problematic Users Report Stronger Peer Support**

The WHO Regional Office for Europe's Health Behaviour in School-aged Children (HBSC) study, surveying nearly 280,000 adolescents aged 11, 13, and 15 across 44 countries in Europe, Central Asia, and Canada, found that heavy but non-problematic social media users reported stronger peer support and social connections [10][17][20]. Dr Hans Henri P. Kluge, WHO Regional Director for Europe, stated: "It's clear that social media can have both positive and negative consequences on the health and well-being of adolescents" [10]. Dr Natasha Azzopardi-Muscat, Director for Country Health Policies and Systems at WHO/Europe, added: "This study reveals both the promise and the pitfalls of digital engagement for our young people" [17].

**Norwegian Qualitative Study (BMC Psychology, 2021) — Social Media Facilitates Connectedness**

A qualitative study published in BMC Psychology investigated how Norwegian adolescents experience the role of social media in relation to mental health and well-being. Using focus group interviews with 27 adolescents (mean age 16.8) from two senior high schools in Norway, three main themes emerged: interpersonal consequences (social media facilitated social connectedness and made it easier to communicate and maintain friendships), personal consequences (social media provided a sense of belonging and social support), and motivations (adolescents felt torn between the necessity of using social media to stay connected socially and the negative consequences they experienced) [48]. The participants stressed that "there was no split between social media and real life" and that if they missed out on things happening on social media, they had missed "half the conversation" [48].

**NIH-Funded ECHO Study (Journal of Adolescent Health) — Friendship Quality Matters Most**

A study funded by the NIH's Environmental Influences on Child Health Outcomes (ECHO) Program, published in the Journal of Adolescent Health, found that the quality of friendships has a much stronger impact on teen mental health than social media use. The study included 963 teens aged 13-18 across the United States [45]. Lead researcher Courtney K. Blackwell, PhD of Northwestern University stated: "Social media use does not occur in a vacuum—it's one piece of a much larger picture. Our study found that peer relationships seem to matter a lot more than social media when it comes to teen mental health outcomes." The study found that "a teen with strong, supportive friendships is likely to experience positive mental health, regardless of how much time they spend on social media" [45].

**Current Pediatrics Reports (2025) — Active Engagement Reduces Loneliness**

A 2025 article in Current Pediatrics Reports by Nagata et al. from UC San Francisco, Harvard, and UW-Madison reviewing the health benefits of social media use in adolescents and young adults found that "active participation on social media, such as engaging with posts, is associated with reduced loneliness, while passive use, such as doomscrolling, does not show the same effect" [44]. The review also found that "social media can help adolescents maintain and build new social connections with peers" [44].

**Pew Research Center (2022) — 80% of Teens Feel More Connected**

A 2022 Pew Research Center survey of 1,316 U.S. teens (ages 13-17) found that 80% of teens feel more connected to friends' lives through social media, and 32% of teens say social media has had a mostly positive effect on themselves personally [50].

### In-Depth Analysis

The evidence consistently distinguishes between active and passive social media use. Active engagement—direct messaging, commenting on friends' posts, sharing personal content—is consistently associated with positive social outcomes, while passive scrolling correlates with negative ones. This distinction is crucial for policy design: blanket restrictions on social media use may inadvertently prevent teenagers from accessing the social support networks that active use provides.

The Norwegian qualitative study is particularly revealing because it captures teenagers' own perspectives. The finding that adolescents see "no split between social media and real life" challenges the notion that online and offline relationships are separate domains. For European teenagers, social media is deeply integrated into their social fabric, and disconnection can mean social exclusion.

The WHO/HBSC data's distinction between "problematic" and "heavy but non-problematic" users is also critical. It suggests that time spent on social media is less important than the quality of engagement and the presence of addiction-like symptoms. This nuance is often lost in public debates that focus solely on screen time.

## PRO Argument 2: Social Media is a Lifeline for LGBTQ+ European Teenagers, Providing Community, Identity Support, and Mental Health Benefits

### Thesis Statement

For LGBTQ+ teenagers across Europe, social media provides essential safe spaces for community connection, identity exploration, and peer support that are often unavailable in their offline environments, with active social media use being protective for mental health.

### Supporting Evidence

**Systematic Review (JMIR, 2022) — Social Media Supports LGBTQ+ Youth Mental Health**

A systematic review published in the Journal of Medical Internet Research (2022) examined how social media use affects the mental health and well-being of LGBTQ+ youth aged 10-24 years. The review followed PRISMA guidelines and searched five databases, with 26 studies meeting inclusion criteria (14,112 total participants). Studies were from the UK (12%), Canada (15%), US (65%), Australia (8%), China (4%), and Ukraine (4%) [11]. Key findings included: "Social media was a popular tool used by LGBTQ youths to connect with LGBTQ communities"; in qualitative data, "LGBTQ youths negotiated and explored identity and obtained support from peers on social media"; and quantitative studies (3/8, 38%) "showed that social media was associated with reduced mental health concerns and increased well-being among LGBTQ youths" [11]. The review concluded that "social media may support the mental health and well-being of LGBTQ youths through peer connection, identity management, and social support" [11].

**Current Pediatrics Reports (2025) — Active Use is Protective for Transgender/Nonbinary Youth**

A review article published in Current Pediatrics Reports (Volume 13, Article 3, January 2025) examining the relationship between social media use and LGBTQ+ youth found that "while some research suggests that social media use and overuse is associated with negative outcomes such as depression and anxiety, many recent studies have found that, for LGBTQ+ youth in particular, social media may be a necessary safe space they have for representation and community building, especially when absent in their physical world" [12]. Critically, the review found that "active social media use served a protective effect and that deliberate 'social media breaks' were associated with increased depression, suggesting that regulating screen time might not be beneficial for the mental health of LGBTQ+ youth" [12]. The authors warned that "simply suggesting a one-size-fits-all solution such as minimizing social media time is harmful, especially for some LGBTQ+ youth who intentionally create safe spaces online that might not exist offline" [12].

**The Trevor Project (2024) — LGBTQ+ Youth Need Online Safe Spaces**

The Trevor Project's research brief "Online Experiences and Mental Health of LGBTQ+ Young People" (2024 U.S. National Survey, 18,663 participants, 65% TGNB) found that 7% of LGBTQ+ young people reported not feeling safe and understood anywhere on the internet. These individuals had worse mental health outcomes and greater likelihood of past-year suicide consideration (47% vs. 38%) and attempts (14% vs. 11%) compared to those who felt safe somewhere online [15]. The Trevor Project warns: "Using these results to justify limiting access to online platforms could have harmful consequences, possibly increasing isolation and reducing support for LGBTQ+ youth, thereby exacerbating mental health challenges and suicide risk" [15].

**Computers in Human Behavior Study (2024) — Social Media Offers Connection and Refuge**

A research article published in Computers in Human Behavior (Volume 156, July 2024) titled "Social media: A double-edged sword for LGBTQ+ youth" by Celia B. Fisher, Xiangyu Tao, and Madeline Ford used a mixed-methods online approach with 406 LGBTQ+ youth aged 14-18. Three thematic narrative groups emerged: social media as a source of connection, refuge, or rejection. The authors call for practices that "acknowledge the dual-faceted nature of social media and promote connections within LGBTQ+ communities to increase LGBTQ+ youth's mental health resilience against online discrimination" [14].

### In-Depth Analysis

The evidence for social media's benefits to LGBTQ+ youth is among the strongest in the entire literature. Multiple studies converge on the finding that for this population, social media serves functions that offline environments cannot reliably provide: identity exploration, peer support from similar others, access to representation, and a space where they can be their authentic selves.

The policy implications are profound. Proposals for blanket social media bans or age restrictions, which are gaining traction across Europe, may disproportionately harm LGBTQ+ youth. The Trevor Project's finding that LGBTQ+ youth who lack safe online spaces have significantly higher suicide risk is a stark warning against one-size-fits-all approaches. The Current Pediatrics Reports review's finding that deliberate social media breaks increased depression among LGBTQ+ youth directly challenges the assumption that reducing screen time is universally beneficial.

The European dimension is particularly relevant. While the systematic review included UK data, the broader European context includes countries where LGBTQ+ rights are more or less advanced. In countries where offline acceptance is lower, the online lifeline becomes even more critical. The EU's Charter of Fundamental Rights guarantees equality and non-discrimination, which should inform how child protection measures are designed.

## PRO Argument 3: Social Media Enhances Educational Outcomes and Develops Digital Literacy for European Teenagers

### Thesis Statement

Social media platforms serve as valuable tools for informal learning, digital literacy development, and access to educational content for European teenagers, with the European Union actively investing in digital education strategies that leverage these benefits.

### Supporting Evidence

**EU Digital Education Action Plan (2021-2027) — Strategic Vision for Digital Learning**

The European Commission's Digital Education Action Plan (2021-2027) lays out a strategic vision to foster high-quality, inclusive, and accessible digital education across Europe. Adopted in 2020 in response to the COVID-19 pandemic, the plan aims to help national education and training systems adapt to the digital age [16][21][23]. Key figures highlighting the need for action include: fewer than 40% of EU educators felt ready to use digital technologies in teaching (OECD, 2018); over 40% of 13-14-year-olds in the EU lack basic digital skills, falling short of the EU's 2030 goal of reducing this below 15% (ICILS, 2023); and 95% of respondents in the public consultation said the COVID-19 pandemic marked a turning point in educational technology use (2020) [16][23]. The European Digital Education Hub, launched in June 2022, had grown to over 5,500 members with more than 100 publications and 200 activities by January 2025 [39].

**European Commission Guidelines on Digital Education (March 2026) — Teachers Must Support Digital Literacy**

The European Commission released four new sets of guidelines on March 5, 2026, to help primary and secondary school teachers navigate Europe's digital transformation. The guidelines address tackling disinformation, navigating artificial intelligence (AI), teaching informatics, and selecting digital education content [41]. According to a Eurobarometer survey, 81% of citizens believe teachers should have the skills to use and understand AI, and nearly 90% say that all teachers should be able to help students recognize disinformation online [41].

**UK Study on Health-Related Learning Through Social Media (Teaching and Teacher Education, 2021)**

This academic paper examined young people's health-related learning through social media. Based on UK data from 170 participants (young people aged 13-18, teachers, community workers, and researchers), the study found that social media is a "user-centric and interest-driven technology" that serves as a valuable health-related learning tool for physical activity, diet/nutrition, and body image [18]. The study concluded that "teachers need to be empathetic rather than antagonistic toward young people's uses of social media" and that "the tendency for schools to ban mobile phones should be re-considered given educational and health-related benefits" [18].

**TEDx Talk — #BookTok and Creative Expression**

A TEDx talk by Zoe Ruth Diego Jacob (May 2025) highlights that "#BookTok on TikTok has garnered over 239 billion views online creating a vibrant world around reading" and that "about 71% of teens use social media platforms like TikTok and Instagram to help express their creative expressions" [52]. The talk notes that social media fosters collaborative learning, broadens understanding of the world, and teaches digital literacy, helping youth navigate online platforms and identify credible sources [52].

**Current Pediatrics Reports (2025) — Adolescents Use TikTok for Health Information**

The 2025 review by Nagata et al. found that "adolescents use platforms like TikTok for mental health coping strategies and health information, though content accuracy varies" [44]. The authors note that "although teens viewed TikTok as an important tool for mental health help, most of them did not view the platform as a replacement for professional mental health help" [44].

### In-Depth Analysis

The educational potential of social media is often overshadowed by concerns about distraction and harm. However, the evidence suggests that social media platforms are increasingly serving as informal learning environments, particularly for topics that teenagers find relevant to their lives but may not be adequately covered in formal education.

The EU's Digital Education Action Plan represents a strategic recognition that digital literacy is not optional—it is a fundamental skill for citizenship and employability in the 21st century. The fact that over 40% of 13-14-year-olds in the EU lack basic digital skills highlights the gap that social media can help fill, even as it may also contribute to the problem through distraction and misinformation.

The UK study's finding that teachers should be "empathetic rather than antagonistic" toward young people's social media use is particularly important. It suggests that the most effective educational approach is to integrate social media literacy into the curriculum rather than simply banning devices. The European Commission's 2026 guidelines on tackling disinformation and teaching AI literacy represent a step in this direction.

The #BookTok phenomenon, with 239 billion views, demonstrates that social media can drive engagement with traditional literacy. This challenges the narrative that social media is purely a distraction from reading and learning.

## PRO Argument 4: Social Media Facilitates Civic Engagement, Political Awareness, and Community Participation for European Youth

### Thesis Statement

Social media platforms are increasingly important tools for European teenagers' civic and political engagement, with young Europeans more likely than the general population to participate in civic issues online, and these platforms helping build friendships and diverse perspectives.

### Supporting Evidence

**Eurostat (2025) — 24.3% of Young Europeans Engage in Civic/Political Issues Online**

According to Eurostat data from 2025, 24.3% of young Europeans aged 16 to 29 engaged in civic or political issues online (expressing opinions, online consultations, or voting), compared to 20.2% of the overall population [20]. The highest youth participation was in Slovenia (49.4%), Latvia (33.3%), and the Netherlands (31.3%). In 23 of 27 EU countries, young people were more likely to use the internet for civic or political participation than the overall population [20].

**European Youth and Digital Engagement Study (Springer, 2025) — Digital Skills Linked to Civic Participation**

A study published in the Journal of Applied Youth Studies (Volume 8, pages 219–247, 2025) by Eva Tsouparopoulou, Maria Symeonaki, Dimitris Parsanoglou, and Aggeliki Kazani investigated how young Europeans (aged 15–35) perceive digital communication, focusing on digital skills, digital citizenship, and civic engagement. Using data from the European Social Survey (ESS) Round 10 (2020) across over 30 European countries, the study found that "internet use encourages political participation but also exposes youth to misinformation and fake news" and that "social media provides platforms for civic engagement and affects political behavior both online and offline" [21].

**CIRCLE (Tufts University) — Media-Making Builds Civic Confidence**

CIRCLE's post-2020 election survey of U.S. teens (ages 14-17, n=1,847) found that media-making about social and political issues builds confidence and civic engagement. Nearly half (45%) of teens had engaged in some form of political media creation in the past 30 days. "More than four in five teens (82%) who created some form of media in the last month said they felt more informed about politics as a result, and a similar proportion (80%) said they felt like their voice was more powerful" [22]. Students who had learned media literacy skills in school were about twice as likely to have created media [22].

**TEDx Talk — Civic Engagement and Activism**

The TEDx talk highlighted that "platforms like Twitter enable youth to advocate for issues (e.g., Greta Thunberg's climate activism, Black Lives Matter)" and that "social media empowers young people to stand up for their beliefs and create global awareness" [52].

### In-Depth Analysis

The Eurostat data provides a powerful European-specific finding: across 23 of 27 EU countries, young people are more likely to use the internet for civic and political participation than the general population. This contradicts the narrative that young people are apathetic and disengaged. Instead, they are engaging in politics through different channels than previous generations.

The CIRCLE finding that media-making builds civic confidence is particularly important. It suggests that when young people create content about political issues—whether through videos, memes, or posts—they develop a sense of political efficacy that translates into offline engagement. This has implications for civic education: rather than just teaching students to consume news critically, educators should also teach them to produce media.

The example of Greta Thunberg's climate activism, which originated on social media and grew into a global movement, illustrates the potential of these platforms to amplify youth voices on issues they care about. The Fridays for Future movement, which began with a single teenager's school strike posted on social media, mobilized millions of young people across Europe.

However, the Springer study's acknowledgment that "internet use encourages political participation but also exposes youth to misinformation and fake news" captures the dual-edged nature of this phenomenon. The same platforms that enable civic engagement also facilitate the spread of disinformation.

## PRO Argument 5: Social Media Develops Creativity, Identity Formation, and Self-Expression for European Teenagers

### Thesis Statement

Social media platforms provide European teenagers with valuable opportunities for creative expression, identity exploration, and self-confidence building, with a majority of teens using platforms to express creativity and develop their sense of self.

### Supporting Evidence

**Pew Research Center (2022) — Creativity and Connection**

The Pew Research Center survey found that 71% of teens feel more creative through social media content, and 80% feel more connected to friends' lives [50].

**Mayo Clinic — Online Identity Creation**

The Mayo Clinic notes that social media can help teens "create online identities, build social networks, and find support from peers with shared hobbies or experiences," noting this is "particularly helpful for teens from marginalized groups, those with niche interests, or those prone to depression" [46].

**Current Pediatrics Reports (2025) — Identity Formation for Marginalized Youth**

The 2025 review by Nagata et al. found that "for marginalized adolescents, social media offers a way to connect with other peers who share similar identities" [44]. The authors note that social media provides important benefits including social connection, identity formation, mental health support, and health education [44].

**Norwegian Qualitative Study (BMC Psychology, 2021) — Social Media as Peer Interaction Arena**

The Norwegian study found that social media is "an important part of the participants' everyday life, with both a positive and negative impact on their mental health and well-being" and emphasized "the importance of social media as an arena for peer interaction" [48].

**TEDx Talk — Creative Expression**

The TEDx talk highlights that "about 71% of teens use social media platforms like TikTok and Instagram to help express their creative expressions (arts, music, writing). Social media serves as a space for identity exploration, particularly for marginalized groups, and provides feedback that motivates young people to pursue passions" [52].

### In-Depth Analysis

Identity formation is a central developmental task of adolescence. Social media provides a space for teenagers to experiment with different aspects of their identity, receive feedback, and refine their self-presentation. For teens with niche interests—whether it's a particular music genre, artistic style, or academic subject—social media can connect them with communities that share those interests, providing validation and motivation.

The finding that 71% of teens feel more creative through social media is significant. Platforms like TikTok, Instagram, and YouTube have lowered the barriers to creative production. Teenagers can create videos, edit photos, write, and share their work with audiences that would have been inaccessible in previous generations. This creative engagement can build skills that are valuable in education and employment.

The Mayo Clinic's observation that identity creation is "particularly helpful for teens from marginalized groups" underscores the importance of social media for those who may not find acceptance in their offline environments. For LGBTQ+ youth, ethnic minority youth, or teens with disabilities, social media can provide validation and representation that is absent in their physical communities.

## PRO Argument 6: EU and UK Safety Regulations Are Creating a Safer Social Media Environment for European Teenagers

### Thesis Statement

The European Union's Digital Services Act (DSA) and the United Kingdom's Online Safety Act (OSA) are establishing robust regulatory frameworks with age verification, platform moderation, and enforcement mechanisms that are demonstrably improving safety for European teenagers online.

### Supporting Evidence

**EU Digital Services Act (DSA) — Age Verification and Enforcement**

The European Commission has developed a harmonised approach to age verification, focusing on user-friendly, privacy-preserving solutions. A blueprint for the age verification solution was released on July 14, 2025, and became feature-ready on April 15, 2026 [5]. The solution, also called the 'mini wallet,' is built on the same technical specifications as the European Digital Identity Wallets that will be rolled out by the end of 2026 in all EU Member States [5]. The Commission adopted a recommendation on April 29, 2026, laying down actions for Member States to roll out age verification by the end of 2026, including making use of the EU's age verification blueprint, drawing up implementation plans, and ensuring compliance with cybersecurity and privacy standards [5].

On July 23, 2026, the European Commission sent TikTok its preliminary findings indicating that the platform fails to protect minors' privacy under the DSA. The Commission determined that TikTok violated Article 28(1) of the DSA by maintaining default settings that expose minors to cyberbullying, unwanted contact, and predatory behavior. If confirmed, TikTok faces fines of up to 6% of its global annual turnover [2].

**UK Online Safety Act 2023 — Implementation and Enforcement**

The Online Safety Act 2023 received Royal Assent on October 26, 2023, and is being implemented by Ofcom in three phases. Key provisions include: "The strongest protections in the Act have been designed for children. Platforms will be required to prevent children from accessing harmful and age-inappropriate content and provide parents and children with clear and accessible ways to report problems online" [32]. Companies can be fined up to £18 million or 10 percent of their qualifying worldwide revenue, whichever is greater [32].

On July 25, 2025, the first Protection of Children Codes of Practice came into force, completing Phase 2 of Ofcom's three-phase implementation roadmap [9]. Ofcom published guidance on January 16, 2025, on "highly effective age assurance" methods, confirming that self-declaration of age is not sufficient [10]. As of October 2025, Ofcom had launched 5 enforcement programmes and opened 21 investigations [11].

**Nordic Collaboration — NOCTA and National Initiatives**

The Nordic network NOCTA (Nordic Child Tech Policy Alliance), consisting of seven organizations from Sweden, Norway, Finland, and Denmark, aims to influence tech companies and decision-makers to prioritize children's rights in digital environments. NOCTA has contributed to EU-level policy processes including the DSA Guidelines under Article 28 [12].

### In-Depth Analysis

The regulatory landscape for protecting minors online has transformed dramatically in the past three years. The EU and UK have moved from a largely self-regulatory approach to one with legal obligations, significant fines, and active enforcement. This represents a fundamental shift in the balance of power between tech companies and public authorities.

The DSA's enforcement actions against TikTok, Meta, and Snapchat demonstrate that the new regulatory framework is not merely symbolic. The finding that TikTok's default settings expose minors to harm, and the requirement to change those settings, represents a concrete improvement in the safety of the platform for millions of European teenagers.

The UK's Online Safety Act, with its requirement for "highly effective age assurance" and its rejection of self-declaration as sufficient, addresses a long-standing loophole in online child protection. The fact that Ofcom has already issued fines demonstrates that enforcement is real.

However, significant challenges remain. The EU Kids Online 2026 report found that 47% of children disagree with restrictions on social media, fearing it would make it difficult to participate in online discussions or stay in touch with friends [16]. This highlights the tension between protection and participation that regulators must navigate. The DSA guidelines themselves emphasize a proportionate, risk-based approach, noting that "radical, disproportionate measures such as outright bans are simplistic solutions incompatible with emphatic declarations regarding the comprehensive protection of minors" [10].

# Part Two: Con-Social Media Arguments

## CON Argument 1: Social Media Increases Anxiety, Depression, Body Image Issues, and Reduces Self-Esteem Among European Teenagers

### Thesis Statement

The preponderance of European and multinational evidence demonstrates that social media use, particularly heavy and passive consumption, is systematically associated with elevated rates of depression, anxiety, body dissatisfaction, and diminished self-esteem among adolescents aged 13-18, with problematic use rising sharply across Europe.

### Supporting Evidence

**WHO/HBSC Report (2024) — Problematic Social Media Use Rising Sharply**

The WHO Regional Office for Europe, drawing on the HBSC study surveying nearly 280,000 adolescents across 44 countries, found that problematic social media use rose from 7% in 2018 to 11% in 2022 [10]. Girls are disproportionately affected (13%) compared with boys (9%) [11]. Problematic use is defined by addiction-like symptoms: inability to control usage, withdrawal symptoms, neglecting other activities, and negative consequences in daily life. The report notes that such use "has been shown to lead to depression, bullying, anxiety and poor academic performance" [10]. Dr Hans Henri P. Kluge stated: "It's clear we need immediate and sustained action to help adolescents turn the tide on potentially damaging social media use" [10].

**World Happiness Report 2026 — Seven Lines of Evidence Converging on Population-Level Harm**

Jonathan Haidt and Zachary Rausch (NYU) contributed a chapter to the World Happiness Report 2026 arguing that social media is harming adolescents "at a scale large enough to affect population-level mental health trends" [9]. They present seven lines of evidence including:

- **Victim testimony**: 34% of US teens wish Instagram did not exist; 47% regret TikTok; 50% regret X. Internal Meta research found that 1 in 3 teen girls said Instagram worsened body image issues, and 37% of teen girls reported seeing posts that made them feel worse about their bodies [9].
- **Company documents**: Internal TikTok documents acknowledge "compulsive usage correlates with a slew of negative mental health effects." Snapchat internally acknowledged approximately 10,000 monthly sextortion reports. Meta's founding president Sean Parker admitted: "We understood this consciously. And we did it anyway" [9].
- **Cross-sectional studies**: Heavy social media users (5+ hours/day) are about 2 times more likely to meet criteria for depression. A 2022 meta-analysis found that depression odds increase by 13% for every additional hour of daily social media use. The risk elevation from heavy use is comparable to sleep deprivation and greater than poverty [9].

Haidt and Rausch conclude that "the cost of the opposite error – incorrectly concluding that social media is safe for adolescents – is catastrophically high," and inaction condemns millions to "higher levels of mental illness, self-harm, and online victimisation" [9].

**Scoping Review of Reviews (2025) — Majority of Reviews Link Social Media to Depression and Anxiety**

A scoping review of reviews by Agyapong-Opoku et al. (University of Alberta, published in Behavioral Sciences, 2025) analyzed 43 review articles published between July 2020 and July 2024 [1]. The majority of studies linked social media use to depression (13 studies) and anxiety (7 studies), as well as suicidal ideation/self-harm (6 studies), body dissatisfaction/eating disorders (5 studies), and ADHD. A dose-response relationship was noted: "For each hour of increase in social media use among adolescents, there was a 13% increased risk of depression, an association which was more robust for adolescent females than males" [1].

**HBSC Finland Study (2024) — Social Media Threats Systematically Associated with Poor Health**

A study using nationally representative HBSC data from Finland (2,288 respondents aged 11, 13, and 15), published in Child and Adolescent Psychiatry and Mental Health (May 2024), found that daily and weekly exposure to social media threats was systematically associated with poor self-rated health, frequent depressive feelings, and anxiety symptoms [8]. The most prevalent daily threat was misinformation (12.9%); the most prevalent weekly threat was misinformation (44.2%). Other prevalent weekly threats included harmful social media challenges (22.3%) and content causing appearance pressures [8].

**Pew Research Center (2025) — Teens Themselves Increasingly See the Harm**

Pew's survey of 1,391 U.S. teens (ages 13-17) found that 48% of teens believe social media has a mostly negative effect on people their age, up from 32% in 2022 [4]. However, only 14% say it negatively affects them personally. 45% of teens say they spend too much time on social media (up from 27% in 2023), and 44% have tried to cut back. Teen girls are more likely than boys to report negative impacts on mental health (25% vs. 14%) [4].

### In-Depth Analysis

The convergence of evidence across multiple study designs—cross-sectional, longitudinal, experimental, and internal industry documents—strengthens the case that social media use causes harm to adolescent mental health, not merely correlates with it. The WHO/HBSC data showing a rise in problematic use from 7% to 11% between 2018 and 2022, based on nearly 280,000 adolescents across 44 countries, provides the strongest European-wide epidemiological picture.

The gender difference is striking and consistent: girls are disproportionately affected across all measures. This is likely due to a combination of factors: social media's emphasis on visual appearance and social comparison, which affects girls more; the nature of content girls are exposed to; and the way girls use social media (more passively and for social comparison, while boys use it more for gaming and entertainment).

The HBSC Finland study adds an important dimension by showing that it's not just time spent but the specific threats encountered (misinformation, appearance pressures, harmful challenges) that drive negative health outcomes. This suggests that interventions should go beyond time limits to address content moderation and algorithmic design.

The World Happiness Report chapter's inclusion of internal industry documents is particularly damning. Meta's own research finding that 1 in 3 teen girls said Instagram worsened body image issues, and the admission by Meta's founding president that they "understood this consciously and did it anyway," suggests that the harm is not accidental but a feature of platform design.

## CON Argument 2: Social Media Displaces Sleep, a Critical Driver of Adolescent Mental Health

### Thesis Statement

Social media use is a powerful competitor for adolescent sleep, displacing rest, delaying bedtimes, and disrupting sleep quality. Sleep deprivation in turn mediates the negative impact of social media on mental health, creating a vicious cycle particularly pronounced among girls and heavy users.

### Supporting Evidence

**UK Study (University of Glasgow, BMJ Open) — Heavy Social Media Use Linked to Poor Sleep**

A UK study involving 12,000 teenagers aged 13-15 found that very high social media users (5+ hours/day) were 70% more likely than average users (1-3 hours) to fall asleep late on school nights and after midnight on other days [53]. One in three teens use social media for more than three hours a day, and one in five spend five hours or more. Girls were twice as likely as boys to spend more than five hours on social media and also had more disrupted sleep. Dr Holly Scott (University of Glasgow) stated: "Teenagers may be lying awake because they are not ready to fall asleep and then struggling to disengage from social media because they don't want to miss out" [53].

**Scoping Review on Social Media, Sleep, and Mental Health (Current Psychiatry Reports, 2024)**

A scoping review by Yu et al. synthesized 33 studies (2020-2023) examining youth aged 15-24 [54][55]. Key findings: 16 of 17 cross-sectional studies found significant associations between social media use dimensions (internet addiction, duration, nighttime use) and poor sleep outcomes (delayed bedtime, longer sleep-onset latency, shorter duration, poorer quality). Two of three cohort studies found higher social media use predicted later bedtime and shorter sleep after 1-2 years. Critically, "sleep was found to mediate the negative impacts of social media on mental health and emotional symptoms in adolescents" [54][55].

**Canadian HBSC Study (2024) — Problematic Social Media Use Linked to Severe Sleep Disruption**

Using HBSC data from 12,557 Canadian adolescents aged 11-17, the study found that problematic social media use was associated with the highest odds of poor sleep health (adjusted odds ratios from 1.67 to 3.24) [56]. Intense social media use was also associated with worse sleep (aORs from 1.09 to 2.24). Associations were stronger for girls than boys: problematic social media use among girls vs. boys was associated with late bedtime on school days (aOR=3.74 vs. 1.84) and non-school days (aOR=4.13 vs. 2.18) [56].

**WHO/HBSC Report — Problematic Users Report Less Sleep**

The WHO Regional Office for Europe report noted that problematic social media users report less sleep and later bedtimes [10]. Across Europe, 34% of adolescents play digital games daily, with 22% playing for at least 4 hours on gaming days, further displacing sleep [10].

**UK Parliament Education Committee Report (2024) — Late Bedtimes Predict Poorer Outcomes**

The House of Commons Education Committee report found that late bedtimes (linked to social media use) predict poorer academic and emotional outcomes [26]. The report notes that children with >2 hours daily recreational screen time had worse working memory, processing speed, attention, language skills, and executive function [26].

### In-Depth Analysis

The sleep-social media nexus is one of the most consistent findings in the literature, with multiple European and international studies showing a clear dose-response relationship. The mechanisms are well-understood: blue light from screens suppresses melatonin production; the interactive and engaging nature of social media creates psychophysiological arousal that makes it difficult to fall asleep; the "never-ending" feed and fear of missing out (FoMO) keep users scrolling past their intended bedtime; and constant notifications disrupt sleep throughout the night.

The mediation analyses from the Yu et al. scoping review are particularly important. They establish that sleep is not just another outcome that social media affects—it is a pathway through which social media harms mental health. This means that interventions that improve sleep (such as device-free bedrooms or screen time cutoffs before bedtime) could mitigate some of the mental health harms of social media, even if overall usage doesn't change.

The gender differences in the Canadian HBSC study are stark. Girls' odds of late bedtimes on school days with problematic social media use are more than double those of boys (aOR=3.74 vs. 1.84). This suggests that the sleep-displacement effect is a significant contributor to the gender gap in mental health outcomes.

The UK Parliament report's linkage of sleep disruption to academic outcomes shows the cascading effects: social media disrupts sleep, sleep deprivation impairs cognitive function, and this in turn affects academic performance. This creates a reinforcing cycle where poor academic performance may lead to more social media use as a coping mechanism, further disrupting sleep.

## CON Argument 3: Social Media Harms Attention Spans, Reduces Academic Performance, and Displaces Beneficial Activities

### Thesis Statement

Excessive social media use among European teenagers is associated with diminished attention capacity, lower academic achievement, and displacement of sleep, reading, and physical activity. OECD data show a clear pattern where moderate use (under 3 hours/day) is beneficial but heavy use steadily undermines performance, and mobile phone bans in schools improve test scores, particularly for disadvantaged students.

### Supporting Evidence

**OECD Growing Up in the Social Media Age (2026) — 35 Hours/Week on Social Media**

The OECD Digital Economy Paper (No. 385, 2026) using PISA 2022 data found that on average across OECD countries, 15-year-olds spend almost 35 hours per week on social media, exceeding time spent on homework and school lessons [19]. Girls spend 37.2 hours/week vs 31.8 hours for boys. Usage varies from under 26 hours in Japan to over 45 hours in Chile [19].

Regarding academic performance, a "Goldilocks hypothesis" is observed: moderate use (under 3 hours/day) is associated with higher test scores, while performance declines steadily beyond 3 hours/day. Mathematics performance declines steadily as time spent increases beyond three hours per day. Disadvantaged students spend more time on social media, but the performance decline associated with heavy use is more pronounced for advantaged students (37-point drop at 35 hours/week vs. 19-point drop for disadvantaged) [19].

**Education and Training Monitor 2025 (European Commission) — Digital Distractions Harm Basic Skills**

The European Commission's Education and Training Monitor 2025 reports that 29.5% of EU students underachieve in mathematics, with three key drivers identified: (1) digital distractions in classrooms (32.1% of students report frequent distractions), (2) worsening teacher shortages, and (3) declining parental involvement [20]. Moderate use of digital tools (up to 1 hour/day) is associated with better performance, but excessive use is detrimental [20].

Regarding digital skills, the ICILS 2023 data shows that 42.5% of 8th-grade students (13-14 years old) score below minimum proficiency in computer and information literacy across EU countries, far short of the 2030 target of under 15% underachievement [20].

**UK Parliament Education Committee (2024) — Mobile Phone Bans Improve Test Scores by 6.41%**

The House of Commons Education Committee report "Screen time: impacts on education and wellbeing" (May 2024) found that schools report smartphones disrupt learning; it can take up to 20 minutes to refocus after non-academic activity [26]. Children with >2 hours daily recreational screen time had worse working memory, processing speed, attention, language skills, and executive function. Prolonged screen use is linked to diminished attention capacity and heightened distractibility. Mobile phone bans improve test scores by 6.41% (with wide compliance); low-achieving students improved by 14.23% [26].

**University of Delaware Study (2024) — Social Media Decreases Academic Achievement**

A study of 1,459 middle schoolers (ages 11-15) found that increased use of Facebook, Instagram, Snapchat, and Twitter (X) is associated with decreased academic achievement [25]. The researchers suggest social media may distract early adolescents from schoolwork or disrupt healthy family functioning and peer relationships [25].

**Common Sense Education Survey (2024) — Social Media is an Extensive Distraction**

A 2024 survey reported that 54% of ages 8-12 and 69% of ages 13-18 said social media is an extensive distraction from homework [27].

### In-Depth Analysis

The OECD data are particularly compelling: 35 hours per week on social media for 15-year-olds exceeds time spent on homework and school lessons combined. This represents a massive reallocation of time away from educational activities, sleep, physical activity, and face-to-face social interaction.

The "Goldilocks" pattern is important: moderate use (under 3 hours/day) is associated with better outcomes than no use at all, but heavy use is clearly detrimental. This suggests that the goal should not be to eliminate social media but to encourage moderate, purposeful use. The fact that the performance decline is more pronounced for advantaged students (37-point drop) than for disadvantaged students (19-point drop) may seem counterintuitive, but it likely reflects that advantaged students have more to lose—they have more educational resources and opportunities that are displaced by social media.

The UK Parliament report's finding that mobile phone bans improve test scores by 6.41%, with low-achieving students improving by 14.23%, is strong causal evidence of social media's educational harm. The fact that low-achieving students benefit most suggests that phone bans may reduce educational inequality. However, the OECD also notes that 29% of students in schools that ban mobile phones still report using their phone at school several times a day, indicating that bans alone are insufficient without enforcement.

The European Commission's Education and Training Monitor linking digital distractions to the EU's basic skills crisis places this issue in a broader policy context. With 29.5% of EU students underachieving in mathematics and 42.5% lacking basic digital skills, the impact of social media on education is not just an individual concern but a threat to Europe's human capital and economic competitiveness.

## CON Argument 4: Social Media Exposes European Teenagers to Misinformation, Echo Chambers, and Polarization

### Thesis Statement

European teenagers face widespread exposure to misinformation on social media, with algorithms creating echo chambers that amplify polarization and reduce exposure to diverse viewpoints. While social media can complement in-person relationships, heavy use is associated with loneliness and displacement of face-to-face interaction, and the EU has recognised these threats through the Digital Services Act and other legislation.

### Supporting Evidence

**Ofcom Online Nation 2024 — Misinformation is the Most Prevalent Online Harm**

Ofcom's Online Nation 2024 report (UK) found that misinformation was the most prevalent potential harm, with 39% of users aged 13+ encountering it in June 2024 [38]. Three in ten UK internet users aged 13+ encountered something upsetting online in the past four weeks. Hateful content reports increased from 23% (June 2023) to 26% (June 2024). TikTok (22%) and YouTube (13%) were the most common platforms for teen harm encounters [38].

**BBC Bitesize / Ofcom (2025) — Teens Increasingly Trust Social Media for News**

Ofcom's 2025 news consumption research found that 80% of 16-24-year-olds go online for news, with 75% using social media specifically [42]. For 12-15-year-olds, 57% consume news on social media, with TikTok being the most popular platform. Trust in social media news among 12-15s has risen to 52% (up from 45% in 2024). One in ten (11%) 12-15-year-olds chose TikTok as their most important news source in 2025, more than any other individual source [42]. The article notes that younger people tend not to seek news out but prefer it to come to them via algorithms, "which can create filter bubbles" [42].

**European University Institute Study (2024) — Echo Chambers Facilitate Misinformation Spread**

A study published in Nature Scientific Reports by EUI Professor Arnout van de Rijt and colleagues built an online social laboratory and demonstrated that in integrated networks (with no echo chambers), misinformation struggled to spread compared to segregated networks containing liberal and conservative echo chambers [30]. "Echo chambers provide environments where people are more comfortable misbehaving as they are surrounded by other misbehaving people," explained van de Rijt, who added that "misinformation is much like a virus raging in a community of unvaccinated individuals" [30].

**EU RENEW Blog (2024) — Echo Chambers Threaten Democratic Process**

The blog post by Sylvia Lissens (KU Leuven) defined echo chambers as "a closed system where other voices are excluded by omission, causing your beliefs to become amplified or reinforced" [29]. Algorithms on platforms like Facebook and Twitter reinforce users' preferences, limiting exposure to dissenting opinions. The post notes the EU's 2018 Code of Practice on Disinformation (updated 2022) and the European Commission opening an investigation into Meta for potential breaches of the Digital Services Act related to election disinformation [29].

**Ofcom Children and Parents Report (2024) — Critical Evaluation Skills Are Weak**

Ofcom's 2024 report found that 69% of 12-17s are confident in judging real vs. fake online, but 16-17s are less confident than in 2022 (75% vs. 82%) [39]. Only 54% of 8-17s correctly identified paid-for results in a Google search; 37% thought they were the most popular results [39].

### In-Depth Analysis

The exposure of European teenagers to misinformation is widespread and growing. Ofcom's finding that 39% of UK users aged 13+ encounter misinformation monthly, combined with the finding that 52% of 12-15-year-olds trust social media news, creates a worrying picture: young people are increasingly turning to social media for news, trusting it, but being exposed to false information at high rates.

The EUI experiment provides causal evidence that echo chambers are not just correlated with misinformation spread but are a mechanism that facilitates it. This has implications for platform design: algorithmic changes that reduce echo chambers could reduce misinformation spread, regardless of content moderation policies.

The declining critical evaluation skills among 16-17-year-olds (from 82% confidence in 2022 to 75% in 2025) is concerning. This may reflect a generation that has grown up with social media and is more trusting of algorithmic recommendations, or it may reflect the increasing sophistication of misinformation that makes it harder to detect.

The EU's legislative response through the DSA, Political Advertising Regulation, and European Media Freedom Act represents a significant attempt to address these issues. However, the effectiveness of these measures will depend on enforcement. The DSA's requirement for systemic risk assessments by Very Large Online Platforms includes the risk of disinformation, but implementation is still in its early stages.

The OECD's finding that lonely students use more social media introduces a bidirectional complexity: social media may not cause loneliness but is used more by lonely adolescents, potentially displacing real-world coping strategies and creating a reinforcing cycle.

## CON Argument 5: Cyberbullying Rates Are High and Rising Across Europe, with Severe Mental Health Consequences

### Thesis Statement

Cyberbullying is the most common online risk reported across European helplines, affecting one in six school-aged children and rising sharply since the pandemic. It is associated with increased anxiety, depression, self-harm, and suicidal behaviour, with girls disproportionately affected. The EU has recognised this crisis by announcing a Cyberbullying Action Plan for 2026.

### Supporting Evidence

**WHO/Europe HBSC Study (March 2024) — Cyberbullying Rising Significantly**

The second volume of the HBSC study, focusing on bullying and peer violence across 44 countries and regions, found that cyberbullying has increased significantly between 2018-2022 [35]. Cyberbullying perpetration rose from 11% to 14% for boys and 7% to 9% for girls. Reports of being cyberbullied increased from 12% to 15% for boys and 13% to 16% for girls [35]. Dr Joanna Inchley, HBSC International Coordinator, stated: "The digital world, while offering incredible opportunities for learning and connecting, also amplifies challenges like cyberbullying. This calls for comprehensive strategies to protect our young people's mental and emotional well-being" [35].

**EU Kids Online — Cyberbullying is Most Common Issue Reported to Helplines**

The European Commission's Joint Research Centre policy brief on cyberbullying (2025) reports that cyberbullying is the most common issue reported to Safer Internet helplines across Europe, with 14% of 54,000 calls in 2024 reporting cases [34]. The EU Kids Online survey across 19 European countries found that one in 20 children are bullied at least monthly [34].

**European Commission Action Plan (2026) — 1 in 6 Adolescents Experience Cyberbullying**

The European Commission's action plan against cyberbullying (last updated March 2026) states that 1 in 6 adolescents in the EU have experienced cyberbullying, and 1 in 8 admit to having participated in it [37]. The action plan focuses on three pillars: coordinated EU approach to protection, prevention and awareness, and reporting and support—including an online safety app across all Member States [37].

**ESMED Study (Post-COVID) — Cyberbullying Experienced by 53.5% of Participants**

The European Society of Medicine study (N=850, ages 10-24) found that 53.5% of participants had experienced cyberbullying at some point in their lives [5]. Cyberbullying was the strongest predictor of negative outcomes across four of five questionnaires (depression, anxiety, stress, and quality of life). The study's regression analysis showed cyberbullying as the most consistent predictor of higher depression (t=7.416), anxiety, and stress scores [5].

**Romanian Children Face Europe's Highest Cyberbullying Rates**

Romania recorded the highest rate of cyberbullying among teenagers in a comparison of seven European countries. More than 37% of adolescents reported being bullied online within the previous year, compared with 27% in Greece and only 13% in Spain [36]. Children spending more than 2 hours daily on social media faced an 83% greater chance of being bullied online [36].

**Education and Training Monitor 2025 — Cyberbullying Rising Across EU**

The European Commission reports that cyberbullying is "on the rise across EU countries, especially among boys," and that PIRLS 2021 data shows worrying levels even among primary school students (ages 9-11), with negative impact on academic performance and wellbeing [20].

### In-Depth Analysis

Cyberbullying data from WHO/HBSC (16% of girls, 15% of boys report being cyberbullied in 2022) and the European Commission (1 in 6) are consistent and alarming. The upward trend from 2018 to 2022 suggests that the pandemic may have accelerated this trend, as children spent more time online.

The ESMED study's finding that cyberbullying was the strongest predictor of negative outcomes across all mental health measures is striking. In regression analyses, cyberbullying was a more consistent predictor of depression, anxiety, stress, and lower quality of life than other factors. This suggests that for many teenagers, the experience of being cyberbullied is the most damaging aspect of their social media use.

The Romanian data (37% cyberbullied) show stark national variations that require targeted responses. The finding that children spending more than 2 hours daily on social media faced an 83% greater chance of being bullied online provides a clear dose-response relationship that can inform guidance.

The fact that Romania remains one of the few EU countries where cyberbullying is not defined as a general criminal offence highlights the gap between the problem and the policy response. The EU's action plan is welcome, but implementation remains uneven across Member States.

The finding that cyberbullying is rising even among primary school students (ages 9-11) is particularly concerning. This suggests that the problem is not limited to teenagers and that prevention efforts need to start earlier.

## CON Argument 6: Social Media Exposes European Teenagers to Online Grooming, Sexual Exploitation, and Privacy Violations at Alarming Rates

### Thesis Statement

Social media platforms have become the primary vector for online child sexual exploitation (OCSE) in Europe. Reports of child sexual abuse material (CSAM) have surged, with over 20.5 million reports in 2024, 70% traced to a European country. Grooming offences in the UK have risen 89% in six years, and law enforcement warns that encryption is creating safe havens for offenders.

### Supporting Evidence

**European Parliament Briefing (2026) — 20.5 Million CSAM Reports, AI-Generated CSAM Up 1,325%**

An EPRS briefing (January 2026) details that in 2024, more than 20.5 million reports of suspected online child sexual abuse materials were detected [43]. Reports related to AI-generated CSAM saw a 1,325% increase, rising from 4,700 in 2023 to 67,000 in 2024 [43]. 70% of all known CSAM in 2024 was traced to a European country, an increase of 4% from 2022 [43]. Self-generated content is rising, particularly among younger children (ages 3-13). The proposed EU regulation to prevent and combat CSAM would require providers to proactively detect CSAM and grooming, but faces proportionality concerns, particularly regarding end-to-end encryption [43].

**Europol — 1.1 Million CyberTips Processed; Combating CSAM is Top Priority**

Europol states that combating Child Sexual Abuse Material is its top priority [44]. Last year alone, Europol processed 1.1 million CyberTips [45]. Europol warns that removing the legal basis for voluntary detection by online platforms could reduce these referrals, impairing the ability to safeguard children [45].

**UK Parliament Report (2024) — Sexual Crimes Against Children Online Up 400% Since 2013**

The House of Commons Education Committee report found that sexual crimes committed against children online have risen by 400% since 2013 [26]. There was a 60% increase in self-generated pornographic images from 7-10 year olds. 81% of girls aged 7-21 experienced threatening or upsetting behaviour online [26].

**NSPCC — Online Grooming Crimes at Record Levels**

The NSPCC reports record levels of online grooming, with 7,062 Sexual Communication with a Child offences recorded in 2023/24—an 89% increase since 2017/18 [49]. Sir Peter Wanless, NSPCC Chief Executive, stated: "One year since the Online Safety Act became law and we are still waiting for tech companies to make their platforms safe for children" [49].

**Ofcom Children Report (2024) — 51% of Children Under 13 Use Social Media Despite Age Limits**

Ofcom found that 51% of children under 13 use social media despite the 13+ minimum age requirement [39]. 40% of 8-17s admit to giving a fake age to access a new site/app. 20% of 8-15-year-olds with social media profiles have a user age of at least 18 [38]. Only 32% of parents correctly know the minimum age requirement for most social media platforms is 13 [39].

**UNODC — Live Streaming Abuse Often Involves Western Teens**

The UNODC teaching module on online child sexual exploitation explains that the Internet Watch Foundation "has more commonly encountered live streaming child sexual abuse involving white girls from relatively affluent Western backgrounds who are physically alone in a home setting, often their own bedrooms" [47]. This counters the stereotype that this crime is limited to developing countries.

### In-Depth Analysis

The scale of online child sexual exploitation is staggering and demands urgent attention. The figure of 20.5 million CSAM reports in 2024, with 70% from Europe, represents a crisis of unprecedented proportions. The 1,325% surge in AI-generated CSAM represents a new and rapidly growing threat that existing detection systems may not be equipped to handle.

The UK data showing a 400% increase in online child sexual offences since 2013 and an 89% increase in grooming offences point to a worsening crisis that has not been adequately addressed by platform self-regulation. The NSPCC's call for Ofcom to strengthen rules requiring platforms to proactively address child sexual abuse, particularly in private messaging apps, highlights the tension between encryption and child protection.

The fact that 51% of under-13s are on social media despite age limits, and that 40% of 8-17s use fake ages, demonstrates the failure of current age verification systems. The EU's development of the 'mini wallet' age verification system and the UK's requirement for "highly effective" age assurance are responses to this failure, but their effectiveness will depend on adoption and enforcement.

The UNODC finding that live streaming abuse often involves "white girls from relatively affluent Western backgrounds" challenges the assumption that this is a problem confined to developing countries. European teenagers are directly affected by these crimes.

The tension between child protection and privacy rights, particularly regarding encryption, is a fundamental policy challenge. The EU's proposed CSAM regulation would require platforms to detect CSAM in encrypted messages, which raises significant privacy concerns. The European Parliament briefing notes that the proposal faces proportionality concerns, particularly regarding end-to-end encryption. This tension reflects a deeper question: how to balance competing fundamental rights—the right to privacy and the right to be protected from harm.

# Part Three: The European Regulatory Landscape

## The EU Digital Services Act (DSA) and Age Verification

The European Union's Digital Services Act (DSA) represents the most comprehensive regulatory framework for online platforms globally. Article 28(1) requires platforms to ensure a high level of privacy, safety, and security for minors. The European Commission has been actively enforcing these provisions, with preliminary findings against TikTok for failing to protect minors' privacy, and investigations into Meta and Snapchat [2].

The Commission has developed a harmonised approach to age verification, including the 'mini wallet' system built on the same technical specifications as the European Digital Identity Wallets [5]. The non-binding Recommendation of April 29, 2026, urges Member States to implement age verification solutions by December 31, 2026 [5]. The Special Panel on Child Safety Online, established in March 2026, recommended a harmonised EU-wide access restriction for children under 13, effective age-assurance systems, and limits on addictive platform features [47].

The EU Kids Online 2026 report, based on surveys of 29,169 children aged 9-16 from 19 European countries, found that 47% of children disagree with restrictions on social media, fearing it would make it difficult to participate in online discussions about news or politics, and 48% are concerned that restrictions would make it more difficult to stay in touch with friends [16].

## The UK Online Safety Act (OSA)

The UK's Online Safety Act 2023 imposes a duty of care on online platforms to take action against illegal content and content harmful to children, with fines up to £18 million or 10% of annual turnover [32]. The Act empowers Ofcom to block access to websites and requires platforms to scan for child pornography and terrorism content, though enforcement of the encryption provision is deferred until "technically feasible" [6].

The Protection of Children Codes of Practice came into force on July 25, 2025, requiring platforms to implement "highly effective" age assurance measures [9]. Ofcom has confirmed that self-declaration of age is not sufficient [10]. The Children's Wellbeing and Schools Act 2026 will ban under-16s from social media by spring 2027, with a proposed curfew for 16-17 year-olds [11].

However, the Act has drawn criticism from academics, human rights organizations, and tech firms who argue it threatens privacy and freedom of speech [6]. The Council of Europe Commissioner for Human Rights and UNICEF have opposed such laws on grounds that they may backfire and constitute violations of freedom of speech and privacy rights [11].

## Nordic Approaches

The Nordic countries have taken different but complementary approaches. Norway has announced plans to increase the minimum age for social media use from 13 to 15, with a bill expected to be introduced in 2026 [13]. Sweden's government-appointed commission recommended a minimum age of 15 for social media use in June 2026 [52]. Denmark has announced a plan to ban social media for children under 15, with an exception allowing parents to consent from age 13, backed by 160 million Danish kroner (€21.4 million) for online child protection initiatives [25]. Finland has focused on media literacy, with the National Audiovisual Institute (KAVI) having a statutory duty to promote media education, and a new law prohibiting mobile phone use during lessons [12][13].

The Nordic Media Literacy Survey 2025, the first of its kind, surveyed 12,744 respondents including 3,257 children and youth aged 9-15. Key findings include that social media is the greatest news source for youth, yet trust in it is low; daily use of age-restricted platforms is substantial among 9-12-year-olds; and only 20% find it easy to tell if information is genuine [42].

# Part Four: Synthesis and Conclusion

## Summary Across the Four Dimensions

**Mental Health and Well-Being:** The evidence is deeply divided. On one hand, social media reduces loneliness and provides social support, particularly for marginalized youth. On the other hand, problematic use—characterized by addiction-like symptoms—is rising sharply across Europe, from 7% to 11% between 2018 and 2022, and is associated with depression, anxiety, and body image issues. The distinction between active and passive use, and between problematic and non-problematic use, is critical. The evidence suggests that moderate, active use can be beneficial, while heavy, passive use is harmful.

**Educational and Developmental Impacts:** Social media offers significant opportunities for informal learning, digital literacy development, and creative expression. The EU's Digital Education Action Plan recognizes these opportunities. However, the evidence also shows that heavy use displaces sleep, reduces attention span, and harms academic performance. OECD data shows that 15-year-olds spend 35 hours per week on social media, exceeding time spent on homework and school lessons. The "Goldilocks" pattern—where moderate use is beneficial but heavy use is harmful—suggests that the goal should be healthy moderation rather than elimination.

**Social and Civic Outcomes:** Social media facilitates civic engagement, with 24.3% of young Europeans engaging in civic or political issues online, higher than the general population. It enables community building and connection with diverse perspectives. However, it also exposes teenagers to misinformation (39% of UK users encounter it monthly), echo chambers, and polarization. The rise of TikTok as a primary news source for 12-15-year-olds, combined with declining critical evaluation skills, is a significant concern.

**Safety and Risk Factors:** The evidence on safety risks is the most alarming. Cyberbullying affects 1 in 6 adolescents and is rising. Online child sexual exploitation has reached crisis levels, with 20.5 million CSAM reports in 2024, 70% from Europe, and a 1,325% increase in AI-generated CSAM. Grooming offences in the UK have risen 89% in six years. The EU and UK have responded with significant regulatory frameworks, but enforcement remains uneven and 51% of under-13s continue to use social media despite age limits.

## The Role of Regulation

The European regulatory landscape is transforming rapidly. The EU DSA, UK Online Safety Act, and Nordic national initiatives represent a shift from self-regulation to legal obligation. The enforcement actions against TikTok, Meta, and Snapchat demonstrate that the new frameworks are not merely symbolic. However, significant challenges remain: the tension between child protection and privacy rights (particularly regarding encryption), the difficulty of effective age verification, and the gap between legislation and enforcement.

The EU Kids Online 2026 report's finding that nearly half of children disagree with restrictions highlights the need to involve young people in the design of protective measures. The DSA's emphasis on a proportionate, risk-based approach, rather than outright bans, reflects this tension.

## Conclusion

The question of whether social media is "good for society" for European teenagers cannot be answered with a simple yes or no. The evidence reveals a complex, multidimensional picture where the same platform can be simultaneously beneficial and harmful, depending on usage patterns, individual vulnerabilities, and the effectiveness of safeguards.

Social media is clearly good for society in some respects: it reduces loneliness, provides essential support for LGBTQ+ youth, facilitates civic engagement, and offers educational and creative opportunities. It is clearly harmful in other respects: it contributes to mental health problems, displaces sleep and academic work, exposes teenagers to misinformation and cyberbullying, and has become the primary vector for online child sexual exploitation.

The key insight from the evidence is that the outcomes depend on *how* social media is used, *who* is using it, and *what protections* are in place. The goal of policy should not be to eliminate social media but to create conditions where its benefits can be realized and its harms minimized. This requires a multi-faceted approach: effective regulation of platform design and algorithms, robust age verification, digital literacy education, support for vulnerable users, and ongoing research to track the evolving impact.

The European regulatory frameworks—the DSA, the Online Safety Act, and Nordic initiatives—represent important steps in this direction. However, their effectiveness will depend on rigorous enforcement, international cooperation, and a willingness to adapt as technology and usage patterns evolve. The voices of young people themselves, as captured in the EU Kids Online research, must be central to this process.
