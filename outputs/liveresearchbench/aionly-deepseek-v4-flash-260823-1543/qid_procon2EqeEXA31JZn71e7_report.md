# Comprehensive Analysis: Should Zoos and Aquariums Exist?

## Introduction

The debate over whether zoos and aquariums should exist has intensified in recent years, driven by evolving scientific understanding of animal welfare, shifting ethical frameworks, and growing scrutiny of conservation and education claims. With over 700 million people visiting zoos and aquariums worldwide annually in normal years, and AZA-accredited institutions alone generating $35 billion in economic impact while supporting 254,000+ jobs [44], these institutions occupy a significant place in modern society. Yet, mounting evidence of animal suffering, philosophical challenges to captivity, and questions about the effectiveness of conservation and education programs have led to increasingly vocal opposition.

This report presents a balanced analysis of six core arguments—three in favor of zoos and aquariums and three against—drawing primarily on research from 2021–2026. Each argument includes a clear thesis statement, multiple supporting points substantiated with credible evidence, and in-depth reasoning and analysis.

## Part I: Pro Arguments for Zoos and Aquariums

### Argument 1: Conservation — Zoos and Aquariums Are Essential Contributors to Species Conservation and Recovery

**Thesis Statement:** Modern accredited zoos and aquariums play an indispensable role in preventing species extinction through captive breeding programs, reintroduction initiatives, and field conservation investments, with documented success stories demonstrating that many species would be extinct without their intervention.

#### Supporting Point 1: Species Survival Plans and Captive Breeding Programs

The Association of Zoos and Aquariums (AZA) Species Survival Plan (SSP) program, established in 1981, represents one of the most sophisticated ex situ conservation frameworks in existence. As of 2025, there are nearly 300 SSP programs covering 295 species, focusing on animals that are threatened, endangered, or otherwise in danger of extinction [1][2]. These programs use cooperative population management, conservation genetics, and research to maintain healthy, genetically diverse populations.

The World Association of Zoos and Aquariums (WAZA) coordinates Conservation Breeding Programmes globally, with regional associations managing these efforts: AZA in North America uses SSPs; the European Association of Zoos and Aquaria (EAZA) runs the EAZA Ex situ Programme (EEP); and the Zoo and Aquarium Association (ZAA) Australasia operates the Australasian Species Management Program (ASMP) [4]. Since 2003, WAZA has also adopted Global Species Management Plans (GSMPs) for inter-regional cooperation.

#### Supporting Point 2: The AZA SAFE Program and Field Conservation Investment

The AZA SAFE (Saving Animals From Extinction) program, established in 2014, has dramatically increased conservation engagement. According to AZA's 2025 year-end report, AZA members invested more than $300 million in efforts that directly benefit animals and habitats in the wild [5]. SAFE expanded to 56 species programs, adding new initiatives focused on African hornbill, American burying beetle, bonobo, eastern massasauga rattlesnake, pangolin, and peninsular pronghorn.

A peer-reviewed study published in *Frontiers in Environmental Science* (September 2021) evaluated SAFE's impact using data from 2010–2019. Key findings: AZA-accredited facilities collectively committed over $231 million to conservation in 2019, up from $150 million annually since 2011, benefiting 987 species in 127 countries. After SAFE's establishment, overall field conservation spending increased 1.4-fold, and member engagement increased 1.2-fold. For eight selected SAFE species programs, conservation spending increased 3-fold and member engagement increased 2-fold after program initiation. The study concludes: "Conservation should be the higher purpose of any modern zoological facility, and collaboration is key to conservation success" [6].

#### Supporting Point 3: Documented Species Recovery Case Studies

**California Condor:** The California Condor Recovery Program is arguably the most celebrated conservation success story in North American history. By 1982, only 22 condors survived worldwide. Through a captive breeding program involving multiple zoos, the species has been brought back from the brink of extinction. As of December 31, 2025, the total world population reached 607, with 392 in the wild and 215 in captivity across five distinct populations [7]. The 2025 Annual Population Status Report shows continued growth, with 46 captive-bred birds released to the wild and 16 wild-fledged chicks recorded. The 1996 Recovery Plan established goals for downlisting from Endangered to Threatened: two wild, geographically distinct, self-sustaining populations, each with at least 150 birds and 15 breeding pairs, plus a third captive population [8].

**Arabian Oryx:** The Arabian oryx was declared extinct in the wild in 1972 due to hunting. "Operation Oryx" (1962), launched by the Fauna Preservation Society, captured three individuals that were sent to the Phoenix Zoo. Combined with animals from private collections, nine individuals formed the "World Herd" breeding program. By 1979, the herd had grown to 137 individuals. Reintroduction began in Oman in 1982, followed by Saudi Arabia, Israel, Jordan, and the UAE. As of 2011, the IUCN Red List reclassified the Arabian oryx from Extinct in the Wild to Vulnerable—the first time a species has been reclassified after being listed as extinct in the wild. Current estimates indicate roughly 1,000–1,220 wild individuals and about 6,000–7,000 in captive breeding programs worldwide [9][10][11]. Fauna & Flora notes: "The rescue of the Arabian oryx is one of Fauna & Flora's most celebrated conservation success stories" [9].

**Black-footed Ferret:** The black-footed ferret, one of North America's most endangered mammals, was thought extinct until rediscovered in Wyoming in 1981. After 18 specimens were captured (1985–1987), a captive breeding program was established. The species faced a severe genetic bottleneck—before 2024, every ferret in the breeding program descended from just 7 founders. In a groundbreaking achievement, Revive & Restore partnered with the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service, San Diego Zoo Wildlife Alliance, and ViaGen Pets and Equine to clone Willa, a female ferret that died in 1988 with no descendants. In December 2020, Elizabeth Ann, the first cloned black-footed ferret (and first cloned U.S. endangered species), was born. In June 2024, Antonia (a clone) bred naturally and gave birth to two healthy kits—the first offspring born from a cloned endangered animal, establishing Willa as the 8th founder for the population. Ryan Phelan, Executive Director of Revive & Restore, stated: "This project represents a historic milestone in conservation history. For the first time, we can definitively say that cloning contributed meaningful genetic variation back into a breeding population" [14].

**Przewalski's Horse:** All living Przewalski's horses (approximately 1,800–3,000) descend from just 12 wild founders captured between 1898 and 1947. A scientific article published in *Animals* (Basel) in February 2025 reports the successful cloning of two Przewalski's horses from cells cryopreserved in 1980 at the San Diego Zoo Wildlife Alliance's Frozen Zoo®. The clones, named Kurt and Ollie, are genetic copies of a stallion that was underutilized in the species' genetic management program. This is the fifth endangered species ever successfully cloned and the first time multiple viable clones of an endangered species survived past the neonatal period. Dr. Oliver Ryder, Director of Conservation Genetics at San Diego Zoo Wildlife Alliance, stated: "It is an enormously hopeful, unprecedented step to have the capacity to restore genetic diversity" [18][19].

**Florida Reef Tract Rescue Project:** The AZA-Florida Reef Tract Rescue Project (FRTRP) addresses the stony coral tissue loss disease (SCTLD) crisis, which has caused up to 90% reduction in abundance of some Florida coral species since 2014, with mortality rates of 66–100%. Conservative estimates by the Florida Fish and Wildlife Conservation Commission indicate that more than 50 million corals were lost due to SCTLD. Since 2019, nearly 2,000 corals have been placed in 21 facilities managed by AZA-accredited institutions across 14 states. AZA member facilities have provided more than $18 million in resources and in-kind services, over 80% of the total investment in rescue to date. Fifteen species have been successfully spawned and propagated in captivity, and to date, no Rescue corals have developed SCTLD-like lesions during their time in holding [21][22][23].

#### Supporting Point 4: Broader Conservation Contributions

The Saving Wildcats project in Scotland (Barclay et al., 2026) produced 56 kittens (2022–2025), with 28 released achieving 89% 6-month survival, and 24 kittens born in the wild [26]. The New England cottontail program bred over 600 kits from 139 founders, releasing over 250 [26]. The Chiricahua leopard frog program increased occupied sites from 37 to 155 over 25+ years [26]. A study in *Science* (Smith et al., 2023) reviewing 95 species that went extinct in the wild since 1950 noted that 12 recovered wild status [26]. Bolam et al. (2020) found that conservation actions prevented 21–32 bird and 7–16 mammal extinctions since 1993, with zoos among organizations behind these actions [26].

#### In-Depth Reasoning and Analysis

The conservation argument for zoos and aquariums is strongest when considering species that have been saved from extinction through captive breeding and reintroduction. The California condor, Arabian oryx, black-footed ferret, and Przewalski's horse represent undeniable success stories—species that would almost certainly be extinct today without zoo-based intervention. The AZA SAFE program's documented 3-fold increase in conservation spending and member engagement for targeted species provides empirical evidence that the zoo community can effectively mobilize resources for conservation.

However, a 2018 study in *Conservation Biology* provides an important caveat. Analyzing the North American Conservation Translocations (NACT) database, researchers found that of 279 animal species translocated, captive breeding by zoos contributed to only 40 species (14% of all translocated species, 25% of those bred in captivity). Zoo contributions varied by taxon: highest for amphibians (42%), followed by mammals (19%), birds (17%), and reptiles (15%). While the proportion of zoo-bred animals in translocations increased over time (1974–2014), the overall contribution remains low [28]. The study recommends increased collaboration between zoos, governments, and academic institutions, and suggests zoos prioritize threatened species and expand captive breeding programs linked to foreseeable releases.

The Endangered Species Coalition notes that genetics in breeding programs is crucial given the 69% decline in vertebrate populations since 1970. Strategies include rotating animals between zoos, genetic testing, and introducing wild individuals to diversify gene pools. Beyond breeding, zoos serve educational roles, fundraise for conservation projects, and act as sanctuaries during natural disasters [29].

The conservation argument is not absolute—it depends on the quality and focus of specific institutions. The most compelling case exists for AZA-accredited institutions that participate in SSP and SAFE programs, rather than the thousands of non-accredited facilities worldwide. Nonetheless, the evidence demonstrates that zoos and aquariums, when properly managed, can make significant contributions to species conservation.

### Argument 2: Education — Zoos and Aquariums Provide Large-Scale, Effective Conservation Education

**Thesis Statement:** Zoos and aquariums reach hundreds of millions of people annually with conservation education, and recent meta-analytic evidence demonstrates that these experiences produce measurable positive effects on visitors' conservation knowledge, attitudes, and behavioral intentions.

#### Supporting Point 1: Unparalleled Scale of Educational Reach

AZA-accredited zoos and aquariums educate over 180 million visitors annually, including 51 million students. Over the past ten years, AZA-accredited institutions have also trained more than 400,000 teachers with award-winning science curricula [30]. Globally, more than 700 million people visit zoos and aquariums in a normal year. Dr. Martín Zordan, CEO of WAZA, wrote in January 2021: "In a normal year we estimate more than 700 million people visit zoos and aquariums worldwide. Inspiring conservation action in the wake of the pandemic is essential if we are to reform humanity's relationship with the natural world" [31].

In the United States, over 180 million people visit a zoo or aquarium annually—more visitors than every NFL, NBA, NHL, and MLB games combined [32]. This scale of public engagement is unparalleled in the conservation education sector.

#### Supporting Point 2: Meta-Analytic Evidence of Educational Impact

A landmark meta-analysis published in *Conservation Biology* (2025) by McNally et al. from the University of Sheffield and Chester Zoo examined the effect of visiting zoos and aquariums on visitors' conservation knowledge, beliefs, and behavior. The researchers systematically reviewed 56 primary studies, using the Theory of Planned Behavior as a theoretical lens and the Behavior Change Technique (BCT) taxonomy to describe interventions. A multilevel meta-analysis was performed to account for dependencies in effect sizes [33][34].

Key findings: The meta-analysis revealed a small to medium positive effect of engaging with zoo-led interventions on outcomes (d+ = 0.40, 95% CI = 0.28–0.51). Specifically, visitors were more knowledgeable about conservation issues, held more favorable attitudes toward conservation, and reported being more likely to act for the benefit of biodiversity. No evidence of publication bias was found. The most commonly measured outcomes were knowledge (37 studies) and attitudes (30 studies), with fewer measuring intentions (14), behavior (7), self-efficacy (4), and none measuring social norms. The most common types of interventions were general visits (12) and live animal interactions (12). Six specific BCTs were identified in interventions: commitment, information about social/environmental consequences, demonstration of behavior, behavioral practice/rehearsal, credible source, and adding objects to the environment.

The conclusion: "Taken together, these findings demonstrate how behavior change frameworks can be used to describe zoo-led interventions and supports the assertion that zoos and aquariums can promote changes in beliefs and behaviors that may help protect biodiversity" [33].

#### Supporting Point 3: Positive Effects on Children and Adolescents

A systematic literature review published in 2024 (PMC) focused specifically on children's and adolescents' learning in zoos and aquariums, noting that six prior reviews focused on adult learning. The author searched ERIC and Web of Science databases, yielding 858 peer-reviewed articles, of which 51 met inclusion criteria. Studies were published between 2003 and 2024, with most from the US, and most participants aged approximately 10 years on average [35].

Key findings: Knowledge was the most frequently studied dependent variable (55% of studies), followed by attitude, motivation, interest, behavior, learning behavior, and communication. 69% of studies investigated causal relationships. Positive effects were found for zoo visits/learning trips, lectures/guided tours, educational programs, enrichment construction, direct animal contact, theater plays, parent-child conversations, camps, and virtual devices. The review concludes: "The results indicate differences in research focus in different countries and that zoos and aquariums are productive learning environments for various types of visiting." Learning methods including touch tanks, keeper talks, and guided tours were found to positively influence knowledge, attitudes, and behavior [35].

#### Supporting Point 4: Evidence from Guided Tours and Specific Programs

A study published in the *Journal of Zoological and Botanical Gardens* (2022) examined whether guided zoo tours influence participants' connection to nature and attitudes toward species conservation. The study was conducted at Opel-Zoo in Kronberg, Germany, with 269 voluntary adult participants. Three types of tours were compared: African mammal tours, special themed tours, and tours including animal feedings. All tour types produced positive increases in both connection to nature and attitudes toward species conservation, despite participants already having high baseline scores. Effect sizes were small (r < 0.3) but statistically significant [36]. The study concludes: "This study provides important evidence that learning at educationally prepared locations, such as zoos in our example, can play an important role in increasing environmental psychological variables."

A study published in *PLOS ONE* (April 2024) describes a multi-phase, evidence-based process used by Denver Zoo to develop a conservation action campaign (CAC) aimed at driving behavior change. The study used the Transtheoretical Model of Behavior Change as a framework. Internal zoo staff and volunteers (n=111) participated in workshops, brainstorming 605 conservation actions consolidated into 164 unique actions. Surveys were sent to 3,192 zoo visitors (401 completed responses). The research concluded that the campaign should focus on habitat restoration/remediation and purchasing wildlife-friendly coffee and products. The article states: "As place-based conservation organizations, zoos are in a central position to support individuals in making small changes in their lives that will support the protection of wildlife and their habitats" [39].

A study published in *Evaluation and Program Planning* (October 2021) investigated the "When Balloons Fly" conservation-education program at Zoos Victoria in Melbourne, Australia. The program aims to raise awareness about marine debris and encourage the use of bubbles instead of balloons outdoors. The study notes: "With hundreds of millions of annual visitors worldwide, well-run modern zoos and aquariums are change agents with enormous potential to promote sustainability and thus protect biodiversity" [40].

#### Supporting Point 5: Teacher Perceptions and Educational Partnerships

An original research article published in *Frontiers in Education* (December 2025) by Wolf, Dierkes, and Kleespies from Goethe University Frankfurt investigated how teachers and trainee teachers perceive zoos as extracurricular learning places. The study involved 126 teaching students and 303 teachers. Results showed overall neutral to slightly positive attitudes toward zoos across all areas. Strong correlations were found between emotional attitudes, task perception (education, research, conservation), and learning place perception. The study demonstrated that "the perception of the zoo's tasks exerts a more significant influence on the perception of the zoo as an extracurricular place of learning than do emotional attitudes" [37].

#### In-Depth Reasoning and Analysis

The education argument for zoos and aquariums has been significantly strengthened by the 2025 McNally meta-analysis in *Conservation Biology*, which represents the highest standard of evidence in scientific research. The finding of a small to medium positive effect (d+ = 0.40) with no publication bias provides robust support for the claim that zoo and aquarium visits can promote conservation-related learning and behavioral intentions.

It is important to acknowledge that a 2010 critical research article by Marino et al. published in *Society & Animals* evaluated the methodological soundness of a 2007 study by Falk et al. (conducted by the AZA) and found "no compelling evidence for the claim that zoos and aquariums promote attitude change, education, or interest in conservation in visitors" [43]. However, the field has responded robustly to this critique. The 2025 meta-analysis directly addresses this criticism with methodologically rigorous analysis across 56 studies. Between 1974 and 2013, zoo staff co-authorship on conservation research papers rose from 0% to 42%, demonstrating the profession's increasing commitment to evidence-based practice [28].

The quality of educational programming varies significantly across institutions. The Global Study of Zoo Education Communication (2020) found that 97% of zoos use signs at animal enclosures, 95% use at least one person-to-person education strategy, and 54% offer animal shows. However, the study noted "large variation in quality and quantity of person-to-person education," with some animal shows containing "very limited educational content" and docents sometimes lacking basic knowledge [38]. This suggests that the educational value of zoos depends heavily on institutional commitment to quality programming.

The knowledge-behavior gap remains a challenge. As Learmonth et al. (2021) note: "As has often been reported, increasing visitors' biodiversity and conservation knowledge does not necessarily result in the adoption of pro-environmental behaviors, but often it does increase the intention to adopt these sorts of behaviors" [45]. The 2025 meta-analysis confirms that most studies measure knowledge and attitudes rather than actual behavior change, indicating a need for more research on long-term behavioral outcomes.

### Argument 3: Research and Economic Value — Zoos and Aquariums Generate Significant Scientific Research and Economic Benefits

**Thesis Statement:** Accredited zoos and aquariums are major contributors to scientific research, veterinary medicine, and local economies, generating billions in economic output and supporting hundreds of thousands of jobs while advancing knowledge in animal health, behavior, and conservation science.

#### Supporting Point 1: Substantial Economic Impact

According to the Sponsorship Marketing Association Intelligence Report (June 2026), AZA-accredited institutions attract 209 million+ annual visitors worldwide (180 million+ in the U.S.), surpassing all major professional sports leagues combined. The U.S. zoo and aquarium industry generated $3 billion in operating revenue (2025) with 6% CAGR (2020–2025). There are 229 AZA-accredited facilities across 46 states, supporting 254,000+ jobs and $35 billion in total economic impact. AZA institutions invested $341.1 million in conservation in 2024 [44].

A 2013 economic impact report by Dr. Stephen S. Fuller (George Mason University) calculated that direct spending of $3.75 billion contributed $10.58 billion to U.S. GDP, generated $3.43 billion in new personal earnings, and supported 103,833 full-time equivalent jobs nationwide. When including visitor spending, combined U.S. total reached $17.23 billion in total output, $5.4 billion in personal earnings, and 165,959 jobs. Globally, all AZA-accredited zoos and aquariums generated $18.09 billion in total output, $5.66 billion in earnings, and 174,700 jobs [45]. A SmartAsset article notes that AZA-accredited zoos drew 183 million visitors in 2018, employed 198,000 people, and generated an estimated $22.5 billion in total economic output [46].

A 2026 global zoo overview from Otto Environmental reports that the AZA reports over 700 million annual visitors globally, contributing $350 million to conservation. The U.S. market size is projected at $3.0 billion with 6.0% CAGR through 2025, continuing into 2026 [47].

#### Supporting Point 2: Case Studies Demonstrating Economic Impact

**The Florida Aquarium (Tampa Bay, 2023):** The Tampa Bay Regional Planning Council analyzed the economic and tourism impact of The Florida Aquarium. Key findings: Record-breaking attendance of over one million visitors, with out-of-town visitors accounting for 45% of attendance. The Aquarium directly supported 249 full-time equivalent jobs and contributed to 407 total jobs. It generated a total economic output of $53.6 million in Hillsborough County, with a Gross County Product contribution of $28.8 million [48].

**Little Rock Zoo (Arkansas, 2021):** A study showed that a $50 million investment from the Rebuild the Rock initiative would generate over $659 million in economic activity for the Little Rock area. Overall economic activity related to the Zoo from 2015 to 2030 would approach $1 billion. Zoo Director Susan Altrui said: "Investment in the Zoo has a real financial return and it's about more than just making kids smile and providing great learning experiences. This is about driving real, tangible tourism dollars to our city and fueling economic opportunity" [49].

**Erie Zoo (Pennsylvania):** The Erie Zoo generates $21.2 million in annual economic impact, supporting 250 jobs, and contributing $1.3 million in state and local taxes. 84% of this impact ($17.8 million) stays in the Erie County economy. Despite attracting 310,000+ yearly visitors, the Zoo faces challenges due to lost AZA accreditation. A modernization effort could double its economic impact to $46.1 million annually. Perry Wood, Ed.D., Executive Director of Erie County Gaming Revenue Authority, said: "By investing in lead assets like the Erie Zoo, we're investing in Erie's ability to attract and retain creative talent and innovative businesses" [50].

#### Supporting Point 3: Scientific Research Contributions

A study published in *FACETS* (March 2018) was the first to quantify the research productivity of zoos and aquariums. The authors found that 228 AZA-accredited institutions published 5,175 peer-reviewed manuscripts over 21 years (1993–2013), with publication output increasing from 114 in 1993 to 437 in 2013. Most publications were in zoology (31.9%) and veterinary science (31.6%). The top publishing institutions were the Smithsonian National Zoological Park, Mote Marine Laboratory and Aquarium, and Chicago Zoological Society. The study recommends that institutions aspiring for higher research productivity need a dedicated research focus and adequate institutional support [52].

Contemporary research activity (2021–2026) covers diverse areas:

**Veterinary Science & Animal Health:** Pain indicators validated in anurans (González et al., 2025); brushing alleviates stress in Malayan tapirs (Ishida et al., 2025); African swine fever susceptibility across pigs and peccaries (Friedrichs et al., 2025); cancer risk across mammals showing carnivorous mammals have highest cancer-related mortality (Vincze et al., 2021); zoos as sentinels for disease monitoring under the OneHealth framework (Van Leeuwen et al., 2023) [53].

**Animal Welfare Science:** Utilizing feather hormone measurements to study avian welfare (Kozlowski et al., 2026); effects of environmental factors on abnormal behaviors in captive brown bears (Soriano, Vinyoles & Maté, 2026); perception of felid welfare by zookeepers (Ogle & DeSmet, 2023); the 2020 Five Domains Model update adding human-animal interactions (Mellor et al., 2020) [54].

**Conservation Research:** Brookfield Zoo Chicago published research on polar bear maternal care, bottlenose dolphin welfare, behavioral diversity as a welfare indicator, pangolin nest box behavior, sihek chick growth and development, and browse provision impacts on animal health [56]. San Diego Zoo Wildlife Alliance has published on wildlife translocation and reintroduction, reproductive technologies for endangered species, giant panda behavior, primate conservation, genetic and genomic research (including the Zoonomia Consortium), and human-wildlife conflict [57].

#### Supporting Point 4: The Frozen Zoo® and Biobanking

The San Diego Zoo Wildlife Alliance's Frozen Zoo® is the world's largest animal biobanking facility, cryopreserving genetic material from thousands of species since 1975. This resource has been instrumental in the black-footed ferret cloning program (cells cryopreserved since 1988) and the Przewalski's horse cloning program (cells cryopreserved since 1980), as well as ongoing genetic rescue efforts for dozens of species [18][19]. The value of such biobanks for future conservation, particularly as extinction rates accelerate, cannot be overstated.

#### In-Depth Reasoning and Analysis

The economic argument for zoos and aquariums is straightforward and well-documented. At $35 billion in total economic impact and 254,000+ jobs, AZA-accredited institutions represent a significant economic sector. The multiplier effects—where zoo spending ripples through local economies—are particularly important for cities and regions that use zoos as anchor attractions for tourism development.

The research contributions are substantial but concentrated among a relatively small number of institutions. The FACETS study found that only 42 AZA-accredited institutions (18% of AZA members) contributed animals to conservation translocations, and the top publishing institutions account for a disproportionate share of research output. This suggests that while the zoo community collectively contributes to science, the distribution of research activity is highly uneven.

The economic model also has vulnerabilities. During COVID-19, zoos and aquariums were severely impacted by closures. Oakland Zoo lost roughly $2 million per month, and the National Aquarium in Baltimore lost over $8 million over three months, furloughing one-third of its staff [51]. This dependency on gate revenue creates a tension between animal welfare and financial sustainability.

The World Travel & Tourism Council (WTTC) reports that in 2025, Travel & Tourism's contribution to global GDP totaled US$11.6 trillion (9.8% of the global economy), supporting 366 million jobs globally. Zoos and aquariums are a significant component of this tourism economy [60]. Industry expert Mark Giovino notes: "What was once a transactional, community-based donation has shifted to longer-term partnerships. Brands have recognized this because they're following the consumer" [44].

## Part II: Con Arguments Against Zoos and Aquariums

### Argument 1: Animal Welfare — Captivity Causes Significant Physical and Psychological Harm to Animals

**Thesis Statement:** Captivity in zoos and aquariums causes demonstrable physical and psychological harm to animals, including stereotypic behaviors, reduced lifespan for many species, chronic stress, and brain damage, and these welfare costs cannot be adequately addressed through enclosure modifications or husbandry improvements.

#### Supporting Point 1: Stereotypic Behaviors and Psychological Harm ("Zoochosis")

"Zoochosis is the name of the mental illness that develops in animals held captive in zoos. Most often, it is seen in what are called stereotypical behaviors, which are often obsessive, repetitive actions that serve no purpose" [3]. The Ballard Brief (Spring 2021) documents that captivity causes chronic stress, elevated allostatic load, anxiety, depression, PTSD-like symptoms, stereotypic behavior (pacing, self-mutilation, regurgitation), hyper-aggressive behavior, and learned helplessness [1].

The neuroscientist-authored article "The neural cruelty of captivity" states: "The repetitive, stereotypic behaviors that many animals adopt in captivity are caused by an imbalance of two neurotransmitters, dopamine and serotonin" [2]. Kelly, Freeman & Rose (2025) conducted a systematic review and found that abnormal behavior is consistently higher in zoo-housed animals compared to their wild counterparts [6].

**Polar Bear Stereotypic Behavior:** Among bears, research by Canino and Powell (2010) proposed that "polar bears (*Ursus maritimus*) are the most likely of the Ursids to engage in stereotypical behaviour" [38]. A 2021 enrichment study at Aalborg Zoo in Denmark found that an adult female polar bear "displayed significantly more stereotypical behaviour (pacing) than her cubs" and "had the lowest behavioural diversity index (0.486), correlating with lower welfare indicators" [38]. The Born Free USA 2023 report "Born to Roam: The Suffering of Polar Bears in Zoos" documents that at least 300 polar bears were held in captivity worldwide as of 2022. The report argues that polar bears' welfare "is irreparably compromised when held in zoos; no amount of husbandry adjustments or enclosure renovations can possibly meet their needs" [37].

**Cetacean Stereotypic and Abnormal Behaviors:** A 2025 review article in *PeerJ* by Marino et al. titled "An update on captive cetacean welfare" (October 31, 2025) found that more than 3,500 cetaceans are housed in concrete tanks or sea pens across approximately 350 facilities in 50 countries. Approximately 87% of captive cetaceans are common bottlenose dolphins. The review states: "Despite efforts by some facilities to improve conditions, serious welfare challenges persist, raising critical ethical concerns about the well-being of captive cetaceans" [20]. A 2020 review in the *Journal of Veterinary Behavior* (Marino et al., "The harmful effects of captivity and chronic stress on the well-being of orcas") found that "in captivity, orcas exhibit a wide range of abnormal behaviors and often die at an early age from infections and other health conditions that are uncommon in a wild setting" [27]. A 2014 study found a captive male orca spent nearly 70% of his time motionless [28].

**Brain Damage from Captivity:** The article "The neural cruelty of captivity" presents evidence that keeping large mammals in zoos and aquariums causes significant brain damage. Key findings: "Subsisting in confined, barren quarters that lack intellectual stimulation or appropriate social contact seems to thin the cerebral cortex—the part of the brain involved in voluntary movement and higher cognitive function, including memory, planning and decision-making." "Prolonged stress elevates stress hormones and damages or even kills neurons in both brain regions [hippocampus and amygdala]." "As my own research and work by many other scientists shows, caging large mammals and putting them on display is undeniably cruel from a neural perspective. It causes brain damage" [2]. A September 2021 paper in *Reviews in the Neurosciences* (Jacobs et al., 2021) hypothesizes about negative impacts of captivity on brains of large-brained mammals including cetaceans and elephants [26][29].

**The Visitor Effect:** The peer-reviewed article "The Visitor Effect on Zoo Animals: Implications and Opportunities for Zoo Animal Welfare" (Sherwen & Hemsworth, 2019, *Animals*) reviews decades of research demonstrating that zoo visitors can have negative impacts on animal behavior and welfare. The article states: "The majority of studies on this topic have concluded that the effects of visitors on animal behaviour and welfare can be interpreted as negative." "If an animal perceives humans as an enemy (predator or competition), this could lead to fear responses being frequently evoked and, as a result, poor welfare outcomes" [4].

#### Supporting Point 2: Reduced Lifespan Compared to Wild Counterparts

**Elephants:** A landmark study published in *Science* (2008) found that female African elephants in Kenya's Amboseli National Park survived to a median age of 56 years, compared to just 17 years for elephants in zoos—nearly three times longer. The same study found that zoo Asian elephant females live a median of 19 years vs. 42 years for working elephants in Myanmar [14][15]. Lead author Georgia Mason (University of Guelph) stated: "We're worried that the whole system basically doesn't work and improving it is essential" [15]. Common health problems for elephants in zoos include herpes, tuberculosis, arthritis, and obesity [14]. A 2018 study in *Nature Communications* found that wild-captured Asian elephants had significantly higher mortality rates compared to captive-born elephants, with median lifespans reduced by 3.17 to over 7 years. The increased mortality risk persisted for roughly a decade after capture [16].

**Orca Survival:** Jett and Ventre (2015) found overall median survival of captive orcas was 6.1 years, with U.S. facilities (12.0 yr) higher than foreign (4.4 yr), and captive-born (14.1 yr) higher than wild-captured (5.5 yr) [29]. A 1979 review showed infectious diseases were the primary cause of death in captive orcas, a pattern that remains largely unchanged [27].

**Counterpoint on Marine Mammals:** A study published October 18, 2023 in *Proceedings of the Royal Society B* found that four marine mammal species (harbor seal, California sea lion, polar bear, and common bottlenose dolphin) today live on average 1.65–3.55 times longer than their wild counterparts. The study analyzed 8,864 individuals from 1829–2020. The authors stated: "Thus, our results contradict arguments of poor or lower survival in zoological institutions than in natural habitats" [18]. However, the Animal Welfare Institute (AWI) counters: "No marine mammal species is known to definitively live longer in captivity than in the wild" [40]. Dr. Lori Marino and colleagues, in their comprehensive 2025 *PeerJ* review, note that despite incremental improvements, most cetaceans in captivity continue to suffer from major welfare problems [20].

**Other Mammals:** A 2026 analysis of 41,244 big cats over 148 years (Øgelund Nielsen et al., 2026) showed zoo-housed individuals now live nearly 5 years longer on average, with improvements in husbandry and veterinary care [6].

#### Supporting Point 3: Compromised Physical Health and Inadequate Enclosures

**Inadequate Enclosure Sizes:** "Wild orcas swim up to 100 miles a day and dive up to 850 feet, yet no orca tank measures longer than 220 feet or deeper than 50 feet" [1]. Wild elephants travel 15–120 miles daily, while zoo elephants average only 3 miles [2]. "Wild cetaceans (whales and dolphins) travel 40–100 miles a day, achieve speeds of 30 miles per hour, and dive hundreds of feet deep. Even in the largest facilities, they have less than 0.0001% (one millionth) of their natural habitat range" [28]. Approximately 81% of captive cetacean habitats are concrete pools [20]. US regulations for marine mammal enclosure sizes remain almost 40 years out of date despite proposed updates in 2016 that were withdrawn [40].

The Born Free USA "Born to Roam" report (2023) states polar bears "are confined to spaces that are a tiny fraction of their wild home ranges, that are typically many degrees too warm for these arctic-adapted animals" [37].

**Inadequate Monitoring and Regulation:** APHIS (the federal body enforcing animal welfare laws) is severely under-resourced, with only 104 inspectors for over 2,000 facilities [1]. WAZA and AZA guidelines are voluntary. Less than 10% of US zoos are AZA-accredited [1]. Six US states exempt animal exhibition from cruelty laws [1].

**Health Impacts of Wild Capture:** A 2018 study in *Nature Communications* analyzing 5,150 Asian elephants found that wild-captured elephants had significantly higher mortality rates compared to captive-born elephants, regardless of capture method. The increased mortality risk persisted for several years after capture, with excess mortality reducing by half every 1.92 years but remaining noticeable for roughly a decade [16]. Bottlenose dolphins are six times more likely to die immediately after capture or transfer [28][40].

#### In-Depth Reasoning and Analysis

The animal welfare argument against zoos and aquariums is supported by extensive scientific evidence, though it is important to note that welfare outcomes vary significantly across species and institutions. The evidence is strongest for large, wide-ranging species such as elephants, polar bears, and cetaceans, which are evolutionarily adapted to traverse vast distances and cannot have their needs met in even the largest enclosures.

The 2025 Marino et al. review in *PeerJ* represents the most comprehensive recent assessment of cetacean welfare, documenting persistent welfare challenges across approximately 350 facilities worldwide. The brain damage evidence from "The neural cruelty of captivity" article adds a dimension beyond behavioral observations, suggesting that captivity can fundamentally alter neurological structure.

However, the counterpoint from the 2023 *Proceedings of the Royal Society B* study showing improved longevity for some marine mammals in zoological institutions complicates the narrative. This suggests that for some species, modern zoo conditions may provide benefits in terms of veterinary care and protection from predators that extend lifespan. The debate is species-specific, and generalizations about "all zoos" or "all animals" are scientifically imprecise.

The visitor effect literature is particularly concerning because it demonstrates that the very presence of humans—the core purpose of zoos as public attractions—can be a source of stress for animals. This creates a fundamental tension between the entertainment/education function of zoos and animal welfare. Sherwen & Hemsworth (2019) note that welfare outcomes depend on how individual animal factors interact with environmental features, but the majority of studies find negative effects [4].

The inadequate regulation and enforcement problem is systemic. With only 104 inspectors for over 2,000 facilities, the U.S. Department of Agriculture's Animal and Plant Health Inspection Service (APHIS) cannot adequately monitor animal welfare. The voluntary nature of AZA accreditation means that the vast majority of zoos (over 90% in the US) operate without the oversight of the most rigorous standards.

### Argument 2: Ethics — Keeping Animals in Captivity Is Fundamentally Unethical, Violating Their Rights to Freedom and Autonomy

**Thesis Statement:** Philosophical and ethical arguments against zoos and aquariums, grounded in animal rights theory, wild-animal sovereignty, and critiques of commodification, maintain that confining animals for human purposes is inherently wrong regardless of the benefits, and that the zoo industry's treatment of animals as property and commodities cannot be ethically justified.

#### Supporting Point 1: Tom Regan's Rights-Based Argument

Philosopher Tom Regan, in his seminal work "The Case for Animal Rights," argues that the fundamental wrong is "the system that allows us to view animals as our resources, here for us to be eaten, or surgically manipulated, or exploited for sport or money" [12]. Regan's "rights view" holds that all individuals who are "experiencing subjects of a life" possess equal inherent value and an equal right to be treated with respect, not as resources. He argues that animals are such subjects and therefore have inherent value equal to humans [12].

Regan's abolitionist conclusion is definitive: "The best we can do when it comes to using animals in science is—not to use them. That is where our duty lies, according to the rights view" [12]. Most famously, Regan states: "It is not larger, cleaner cages that justice demands... but empty cages. The philosophy of animal rights asks for nothing more, but neither will it be satisfied with anything less" [14].

The Lincoln-Douglas debate resolution "Resolved: Keeping Wild Animals in Zoos is Immoral" identifies the master crux as "Rights vs. welfare framework"—"Is the wrong of captivity a matter of consequences—weighing animal suffering against human education and species conservation on a great cosmic ledger—or is it a matter of rights and duties, where confining a being against its nature is simply wrong no matter how the math comes out?" [11]. The debate guide notes: "The affirmative's strongest in-principle argument is the rights or sovereignty claim (Regan, Donaldson & Kymlicka), which, if sound, defeats even a perfect zoo" [11].

#### Supporting Point 2: Wild Animal Sovereignty and Autonomy

The concept of wild-animal sovereignty, developed by political theorists Donaldson and Kymlicka in "Zoopolis," argues that wild animals are sovereign communities and should not be confined without their consent [11]. Jamieson's 1985 essay argues there is a moral presumption against captivity that benefits fail to overcome—the basic liberty/autonomy argument [11]. The liberty argument holds that the fundamental wrong of captivity is the denial of autonomy and freedom to animals who are adapted to roam vast distances.

This philosophical framework challenges even the "best" zoos. If wild animals have a right to sovereignty, then no amount of enrichment, enclosure size, or conservation success can justify their confinement. The argument is deontological—it focuses on the inherent wrongness of the act rather than its consequences.

#### Supporting Point 3: Commodification and Spectacle

John Berger's essay "Why Look at Animals?" describes zoo animals as "living monuments" to their own disappearance—rendered marginal and spectacle [11]. The zoo industry generates over $2 billion annually in the US. Animals like orcas are worth about $10 million each, incentivizing establishments to prioritize profit over welfare [1].

The article "Caged for Capital: Zoos Prioritize Profit Over Animal Conservation" (Born Free USA, June 5, 2025) highlights the case of Columbus Zoo where an investigation led to five convictions of former executives for stealing over $2.3 million in zoo funds for personal use [43]. The article notes that AZA members collectively spent $4.9 billion on operations and construction in 2018 but only $5.2 million on big cat conservation in 2019—just 0.1% of operational expenses. Between 2009 and 2019, the Indianapolis Zoo spent an average of just 1.04% of its budget on conservation, less than the CEO's $370,282 salary [43]. The most supportive zoos typically allocate only 3-5% of their budget to conservation work [43].

Critics argue that "Zoos buy, trade, borrow, and sell animals as if they were property. True sanctuaries do not buy, sell, breed, or trade animals—ever" [3]. "Zoos use animals to make money. Sanctuaries spend money to keep animals safe and happy" [3].

#### Supporting Point 4: Ethical Problems with Breeding and Surplus Culling

Captive breeding programs raise ethical issues: animals are bred for display, creating surplus animals that are then culled. The EAZA estimates approximately 1,735 "surplus" animals killed per year, though other estimates range from 3,000–5,000 healthy animals culled by European zoos each year [11][12][41]. Copenhagen Zoo's Scientific Director Bengt Holst estimates they cull 20–30 animals per year, including larger species like leopards, tigers, lions, bears, antelopes, and hippos [41]. The 2014 case of Marius the giraffe (killed at Copenhagen Zoo) sparked international debate about surplus culling [11][41].

A 2024 *PNAS* opinion article argues that zoos should embrace planned animal death (culling) as a necessary tool. A recent population viability assessment of zoo breeding programs in North America predicted a decline in 64% of 137 species over the next 25 years. The article states: "We live at a time when outsourcing unpleasant truths about animal deaths is no longer adequate. To do so shirks our duty of care to species under human protection" [42].

#### Supporting Point 5: The "Empty the Tanks" Movement and Shifting Public Attitudes

The "Empty the Tanks" global campaign aims to end the captivity of dolphins and whales while promoting ocean health [16]. Empty the Tanks 2026 grew to 38 locations across 13 countries advocating for an end to dolphin and whale captivity [17]. The movement began 14 years ago as a peaceful movement to be a voice for dolphins and whales in captivity [18]. Empty The Tanks is "a call to end the system of confining cetaceans in tanks as a normal, accepted practice. It is an appeal to implement a long-term plan" [19].

The 2013 documentary *Blackfish* revealed the devastating impact of captivity on orcas. SeaWorld's stock fell 33% by 2014 following the film's release [39]. In 2016, SeaWorld announced it would end its orca breeding program, making the 22 orcas at SeaWorld the last generation in captivity [39]. Research by Laure Boissat (Oxford University, published in *People and Nature*) identified three factors explaining the documentary's impact: (1) wide distribution via CNN and Netflix, (2) its ability to engage hearts and minds on animal suffering, and (3) its timely release in a cultural climate receptive to animal welfare concerns [39]. World Animal Protection successfully pressured Expedia Group to update its animal welfare policy in 2021 to prohibit selling tickets to captive whale and dolphin entertainment [39].

Legislative changes have followed shifting public sentiment. The U.S. SWIMS Act, Canadian Bill S-203, French breeding ban, Belgian ban on dolphinaria, and Mexican Senate ban on marine mammal entertainment all reflect growing opposition to cetacean captivity [20]. California passed legislation banning captive orca breeding in 2016 [40].

#### In-Depth Reasoning and Analysis

The ethical argument against zoos and aquariums operates on a fundamentally different plane from the consequentialist arguments about welfare or conservation effectiveness. Where animal welfare arguments ask "Does captivity cause suffering?" and "Can suffering be mitigated?", the ethical argument asks "Is it right to confine animals at all?" Tom Regan's rights-based framework provides a clear answer: no, because animals have inherent value that is violated when they are treated as means to human ends.

The strength of the ethical argument lies in its ability to critique even a hypothetical "perfect zoo" where all welfare concerns are addressed. If animals have a right to freedom and autonomy, then even a zoo with perfect veterinary care, ideal enclosure design, and optimal social groupings would still be unethical because it deprives animals of their liberty. This is why the debate guide identifies the "rights vs. welfare framework" as the master crux [11].

The commodification critique adds another dimension. The zoo industry's treatment of animals as property—bought, sold, traded, and valued at $10 million for an orca—raises fundamental questions about the relationship between humans and animals. The Columbus Zoo embezzlement case and the data on low conservation spending relative to operational budgets suggest that profit motives often override conservation and welfare commitments.

The surplus culling problem is particularly ethically challenging because it reveals an uncomfortable contradiction: zoos that claim to be saving species are simultaneously killing healthy animals because they have bred "too many" for their display purposes. The EAZA estimates of 1,735–5,000 healthy animals culled annually in Europe alone represent a significant ethical cost.

However, the ethical argument is not universally accepted. Utilitarian philosophers like Peter Singer would argue that the suffering of animals in captivity must be weighed against the benefits of conservation and education. Singer's framework is consequentialist—the wrongness of zoos depends on whether the suffering they cause outweighs the benefits they provide [11]. This creates a genuine philosophical debate between deontological (rights-based) and consequentialist (welfare-based) approaches.

The abolition vs. reform debate is also relevant. As one source notes: "One day we may look back at zoos and marvel at their barbarity. Or, one day we may look back at zoos and be grateful for the species they saved from extinction. Of these two scenarios, only time will tell" [13]. The rapid shift in public attitudes toward cetacean captivity, as evidenced by the *Blackfish* effect and subsequent legislation, suggests that society's ethical standards are evolving. What was once considered acceptable entertainment is increasingly viewed as unjustifiable.

### Argument 3: Questionable Effectiveness — The Conservation and Education Claims of Zoos and Aquariums Are Overstated and Frequently Lack Empirical Support

**Thesis Statement:** The conservation and education benefits claimed by zoos and aquariums are significantly overstated—only a small percentage of zoo animals are threatened species, conservation spending is minimal relative to operating budgets, reintroduction success rates are low, and educational impacts are often superficial, failing to translate into meaningful behavioral change.

#### Supporting Point 1: Low Percentage of Threatened Species in Zoos

Conde et al. (2013, *PLOS ONE*) found that only 23% of zoo vertebrate species are threatened [11]. Research by Freedom for Animals found that only 9% of animals held captive in UK zoos are endangered and 17% are threatened, meaning the vast majority of animals kept in zoos are not threatened in the wild [12]. A BBC Horizon documentary "Should We Close Our Zoos?" (2016) reported that "less than 10% of zoo species are endangered in the wild" [12]. The Ballard Brief states that "fewer than 5-10% of zoos are involved in wildlife conservation programs" [1].

Critics argue that zoos primarily keep "charismatic megafauna" (lions, bears, giraffes, elephants) to attract visitors, not for conservation purposes [3]. As of 2019, there were between 1.5 to 3 times more tigers in captivity in the United States alone than in the wild worldwide [1]. "There are more tigers in American backyards than in the wild" [12].

#### Supporting Point 2: Low Conservation Spending Relative to Operating Budgets

The Born Free USA article "Caged for Capital" (June 2025) provides damning statistics: AZA members collectively spent $4.9 billion on operations and construction in 2018 but only $5.2 million on big cat conservation in 2019—just 0.1% of operational expenses. Between 2009 and 2019, the Indianapolis Zoo spent an average of just 1.04% of its budget on conservation, less than the CEO's $370,282 salary [43]. The most supportive zoos typically allocate only 3-5% of their budget to conservation work [43]. Born Free (2021) found that UK zoos spend approximately 4.2% of income on in-situ conservation [11].

These figures stand in stark contrast to the conservation narrative promoted by zoos. If conservation is truly the "higher purpose" of modern zoological facilities, as the 2021 *Frontiers in Environmental Science* article argues [6], then spending less than 5% of budgets on conservation while generating over $2 billion annually in the US raises serious questions about priorities.

Neuroscientist Lori Marino states: "There is a commonly held misconception that zoos are not only saving wild animals from extinction but also reintroducing them to their wild habitats. The confusion stems from many sources, all of them zoo-based... In reality, most zoos have had no contact of any kind with any reintroduction program" [1].

#### Supporting Point 3: Low Reintroduction Success Rates

**General Success Rates:** Beck et al. (1994) found approximately 11% success for captive-born reintroductions [11]. Griffith et al. (1989, *Science*) found 38% vs. 75% success for captive-born vs. wild-caught reintroductions [11]. "Previous reviews found that the average success rate of reintroductions ranges from 11 to 53%" [19].

**Carnivore Reintroductions:** A study of 45 carnivore reintroductions worldwide found that "reintroduction projects were significantly more likely to succeed when they used a wild source population compared to a captive source." "Captive-born carnivores were also more likely to starve to death than their wild-bred counterparts, as well as become more susceptible to viruses and diseases." Captive-born animals show deficiencies in natural behaviors like hunting/foraging, social interactions, and lack fear of humans. Human means (shooting, poisoning, vehicle collisions) were the most common cause of death for reintroduced carnivores [19].

**Limited Zoo Contributions to Translocations:** The 2018 *Conservation Biology* study analyzing the North American Conservation Translocations database found that of 279 animal species translocated, 162 species (58%) involved captive-bred populations. However, captive breeding by zoos contributed to only 40 species (14% of all translocated species, 25% of those bred in captivity). Zoo contributions varied by taxon: highest for amphibians (42%), followed by terrestrial invertebrates (29%), mammals (19%), birds (17%), reptiles (15%), fish (2%), and marine invertebrates (0%). Only 54 zoos contributed animals; 50 were North American, 42 AZA-accredited (only 18% of AZA institutions). While proportional involvement increased from 1974 to 2014, the overall contribution remains low [25].

**Captive Breeding Challenges:** A 2021 study in *Nature Communications* (Farquharson, Hogg, & Grueber) analyzed offspring survival over generations of captive breeding in 15 vertebrate species. Key finding: "Even accounting for inbreeding, we find that the impacts of increasing generations in captivity are highly variable across species, with some showing substantial increases or decreases in offspring survival over generations." The study notes: "Crucially, our multispecies analysis reveals that responses to captivity could not be predicted from species' evolutionary (phylogenetic) relationships." "Even under best-practice captive management, generational fitness changes that cannot be explained by known processes (such as inbreeding depression), are occurring" [21].

**Critique of Success Stories:** Critics argue that the celebrated success stories are the exception rather than the rule. "A few celebrated captive breeding and release projects are regularly cited to demonstrate the success of this kind of intervention... however, closer inspection reveals that the effectiveness of these and many other captive breeding programs is, at best, limited and often end up simply providing new animals for the zoo and aquarium industry" [5]. The California condor program cost over $35 million since 1987, making it "the most expensive captive program ever" [23][24]. "Over and over again the same conclusion is reached: captive breeding and reintroduction programs cannot succeed without long-term wild habitat preservation and protection" [23].

#### Supporting Point 4: Education Effectiveness Is Often Superficial

**The Knowledge-Behavior Gap:** As Learmonth et al. (2021) note: "As has often been reported, increasing visitors' biodiversity and conservation knowledge does not necessarily result in the adoption of pro-environmental behaviors, but often it does increase the intention to adopt these sorts of behaviors." "Two of the largest barriers that visitors report for these low rates of behavioral change are that they are often uncertain how they should become involved other than donating money, and that they lack the appropriate education, materials, or relevance to their daily lives to adopt the required behaviors outside of an immediate zoo visit context" [45].

**Variation in Educational Quality:** The Global Study of Zoo Education Communication and Effectiveness (NAAEE) surveyed 176 zoos across 50 countries and found that 97% of zoos use signs at animal enclosures, 72% use pamphlets, and 69% use worksheets. For educational activities, 95% use at least one person-to-person strategy. However, the study found "large variation in quality and quantity of person-to-person education." Some animal shows had "very limited educational content and appeared mainly for entertainment." Docents were noted to be "friendly and approachable, but 'lacked even basic knowledge about the animals or facilities within the zoo'" and sometimes acted more as crowd control [8]. Visitor interviews showed that 95% read at least some exhibit signs, but the most common reason for not reading all signs was "watching animals" (33% of responses). The quality of signage varied widely in terms of educational content, readability, design, and condition [8].

**Entertainment vs. Education:** A study found that fewer than half of reviewed dolphinaria provided any information on conservation or provided educational materials for children or teachers [28]. A 2018 survey found that supporters of cetacean captivity were significantly more likely to believe cetacean conservation was NOT important [40].

**Marino et al. (2010) Critique:** The critical research article by Marino, Lilienfeld, Malamud, Nobis, and Broglio published in *Society & Animals* evaluated the methodological soundness of the 2007 Falk et al. study (conducted by the AZA) and found that it "contains at least six major threats to methodological validity that undermine its conclusions." They found "no compelling evidence for the claim that zoos and aquariums promote attitude change, education, or interest in conservation in visitors, although further investigation of this possibility using methodologically sophisticated designs is warranted" [43].

#### In-Depth Reasoning and Analysis

The argument that zoos' conservation and education claims are overstated is supported by significant empirical evidence. The 2018 *Conservation Biology* study's finding that zoo contributions to conservation translocations are limited to 14% of species and 18% of AZA institutions provides a sobering counterpoint to the narrative of zoos as modern-day Noah's Arks. The low percentage of threatened species in zoos (23% or less) and the minimal conservation spending relative to operating budgets (typically 1-5%) suggest that conservation is not the primary function of most zoos.

However, it is important to note that the 14% figure from the translocations study represents a proportional contribution that is not trivial. The 42 zoo contributors to translocations are real institutions making real contributions. The 2025 McNally meta-analysis in *Conservation Biology* provides robust evidence of educational impact, directly countering the Marino et al. (2010) critique. The meta-analysis is methodologically superior to any single study, and its finding of positive effects with no publication bias should be given significant weight.

The knowledge-behavior gap is a well-documented challenge in environmental education generally, not just in zoos. The 2025 meta-analysis found that most zoo education studies measure knowledge and attitudes rather than behavior, which is a limitation of the literature rather than a definitive failure of zoos. The Denver Zoo study (2024) and Zoos Victoria study (2021) demonstrate that some institutions are actively working to bridge this gap through evidence-based program design.

The critique of success stories also has merit. The California condor program's $35 million cost is substantial, and the early reintroduction challenges (lead poisoning, power line collisions) highlight the difficulty of captive-to-wild transitions. However, the program's ultimate success—607 birds today from 22 in 1982—is objectively remarkable. The Arabian oryx program's challenges with poaching and the Oman population's near-collapse demonstrate that reintroduction is only part of the solution; habitat protection is equally essential.

The education quality variation is a genuine concern. The Global Study's finding that some animal shows have "very limited educational content" and that docents sometimes lack basic knowledge suggests that not all zoos are equally committed to education. However, the same study found that 95% of visitors read at least some exhibit signs, and the 2022 Opel-Zoo study found that guided tours produce positive effects on nature connectedness and conservation attitudes. The quality of education is a function of institutional commitment, not an inherent limitation of zoos as educational venues.

## Conclusion

The debate over whether zoos and aquariums should exist does not yield a simple yes or no answer. The evidence reveals a complex landscape where the merits and drawbacks vary significantly depending on the specific institution, species, and practices in question.

**For zoos and aquariums:** The conservation case is strongest for AZA-accredited institutions that participate in SSP and SAFE programs, with documented species recovery stories for the California condor, Arabian oryx, black-footed ferret, Przewalski's horse, and numerous coral species. The 2025 meta-analysis provides robust evidence of educational impact, and the economic contribution of $35 billion and 254,000+ jobs is substantial. The Frozen Zoo biobanking efforts represent an irreplaceable resource for future conservation.

**Against zoos and aquariums:** The animal welfare evidence is compelling for large, wide-ranging species, with stereotypic behaviors, brain damage, and reduced lifespans documented across multiple studies. The ethical arguments—particularly Regan's rights-based framework and the sovereignty argument—challenge even the best zoos on principle. The low percentage of threatened species in zoos, minimal conservation spending relative to budgets, and limited contribution to translocations raise serious questions about whether the stated conservation mission aligns with actual practice.

The most defensible position may be a conditional one: zoos and aquariums that prioritize animal welfare, focus on threatened species, invest substantially in conservation, and provide high-quality educational programming can justify their existence. Institutions that keep animals primarily for entertainment, fail to meet welfare standards, or use conservation as a marketing tool without meaningful investment cannot. The growing movement toward marine mammal sanctuary retirement, the shift away from cetacean breeding programs, and the increasing public skepticism documented by the *Blackfish* effect suggest that society's expectations are evolving.

The future of zoos and aquariums likely lies in transformation rather than abolition or preservation of the status quo. Increasing focus on species that genuinely benefit from captive breeding, improved welfare standards, greater investment in in-situ conservation, and more rigorous educational programming could address many of the criticisms. The alternative—a world without zoos—would require alternative mechanisms for the species recovery, research, education, and economic benefits that zoos currently provide. Neither path is without challenges, and the debate will continue to evolve as scientific understanding advances and ethical standards develop.
