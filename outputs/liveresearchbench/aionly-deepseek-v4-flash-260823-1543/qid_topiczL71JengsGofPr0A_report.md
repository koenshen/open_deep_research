# Remote Work and Early-Career Employment: A Comprehensive Analysis of the United States and Europe (2023–2026)

## Executive Summary

The shift toward remote and hybrid work has fundamentally reshaped the labor market for early-career professionals (0–5 years of experience) in both the United States and Europe. While remote work offers flexibility and autonomy, mounting evidence indicates that it is creating significant barriers for recent graduates and young professionals entering the job market. In the United States, research from the Federal Reserve Bank of New York estimates that remote work can explain 64% of the increase in unemployment among young college graduates [1]. In Europe, analysis of 50 million job postings reveals that hiring managers are systematically shifting preferences away from early-career candidates toward more experienced workers when roles are remote [2].

This report examines four critical dimensions of this transformation: (1) the impact on job opportunities and career growth, (2) the skills required for remote versus office-based roles, (3) earning potential and networking implications, and (4) the industries and companies most committed to flexible work arrangements. The analysis draws on extensive academic research, government labor statistics, industry surveys, and policy reports from both sides of the Atlantic.

## 1. How Remote Work Is Affecting Entry-Level Job Opportunities and Career Growth

### 1.1 The Hiring Crisis for Early-Career Professionals

**United States**

The evidence that remote work is systematically disadvantaging early-career job seekers is now overwhelming. A landmark study by Peter John Lambert (LSE/University of Warwick) and Yannick Schindler (Ellison Institute of Technology) found that entry-level hiring dropped 29% and junior-level hiring fell nearly 26% when comparing 2017–2019 to 2023–2025, while senior-level hiring increased by over 5% [3]. Critically, when AI exposure and remote work were analyzed together, remote work continued to predict junior-level hiring declines while AI's impact "attenuates sharply and is often statistically indistinguishable from zero" [3].

The Federal Reserve Bank of New York's Liberty Street Economics published research in June 2026 by Natalia Emanuel, Emma Harrington, and Amanda Pallais that found remote work can explain 64% of the recent increase in unemployment among young college graduates [1]. The unemployment rate for young professionals (college graduates under 29) reached 5.6% in March 2026, which is 2.6 percentage points higher than the overall unemployment rate of 4.2% [1][4]. Youth (under 29) college graduate unemployment rose from 3.1% (2017–19 average) to 3.7% (2022–25 average)—a 20% increase. By contrast, unemployment for experienced college graduates actually fell slightly, from 1.9% to 1.8% over the same period [1].

The rise in young graduate unemployment is concentrated in "remotable" occupations such as software engineering, where the unemployment age gap widened significantly and remained elevated. In non-remotable jobs like mechanical engineering, the gap temporarily ticked up in 2020 but returned to baseline [1]. The authors argue that the timing of the surge predates the rapid diffusion of generative AI, suggesting remote work—not AI—is the primary driver.

The NY Fed researchers concluded that "employers may not want to hire fresh graduates onto distributed teams because it is more difficult to teach them the requisite skills from afar" [1]. A Fortune 500 company study ("The Power of Proximity") found that software engineers in the same office received 18% more coding feedback, and hiring patterns shifted back to junior workers when offices reopened after pandemic closures [3][5].

Despite these challenges, some positive hiring data exists. The National Association of Colleges and Employers (NACE) Job Outlook 2025 report forecasts that employers plan to hire 7.3% more new graduates from the Class of 2025 compared to the Class of 2024 [6]. However, fully remote work has declined to just 8% overall and 4% for entry-level positions. Hybrid work is at its highest reported levels, with 50% of overall and 54% of entry-level positions being performed in this manner [6].

Robert Half data from 2026 shows the number of fully in-office roles rose from 65% in Q4 2025 to 87% in Q2 2026. Of the job postings analyzed in Q2 2026, 87% were fully on-site, while 10% were hybrid and just 3% were fully remote. Entry-level roles have the least access to flexible work (8% hybrid, 2% remote) compared to senior-level positions (12% hybrid, 4% remote) [7].

**Europe**

Research published in Harvard Business Review (August 2026) by Zhenyu Liao, Letian (LT) Zhang, and Shinan Wang analyzed 50 million European job postings and conducted experiments with 1,200 hiring managers [2]. The key finding: remote work is shifting hiring preferences away from early-career candidates toward more experienced workers. In traditional office settings, hiring managers were more willing to take a chance on recent graduates, confident that mentorship, team dynamics, and informal learning could develop their potential. However, remote work environments reduce these developmental opportunities, making managers less likely to hire inexperienced candidates who need close supervision and on-the-job training [2].

The United Kingdom is experiencing a particularly acute crisis for young people. A UK government interim report (August 2026) found that nearly one million young people aged 16–24 in the UK are not in education, employment, or training (NEET)—representing 1 in 8 young people [8]. The NEET rate has barely fallen below 10% in 25 years and is now rising. Nearly 60% of NEET young people are economically inactive (not looking for work), not just unemployed. Six in 10 NEET young people have never had a job (up from 4 in 10 in 2005). Among 24-year-old NEETs, 45% have never worked. The cumulative annual cost of NEET young people is estimated at £125 billion [8].

Youth NEET rates in the UK have become an outlier compared to EU countries—only Romania had a higher rate by 2025. The proportion of NEETs citing a work-limiting health condition has increased by 70% over the past decade. Among disabled NEETs, mental health as the primary condition has almost doubled to over 40%. The forecast suggests the NEET rate could exceed 16% (over 1.25 million young people) within 5 years. Structural causes include a decline in entry-level roles, increasingly automated and less human recruitment processes, and a fall in apprenticeship starts for young people of over 40% [8].

In the broader European Union, youth employment rates are higher than at any time since 2007, and the percentage of young people not in employment, education or training (NEET) is historically low at 11.7% in 2022 [9]. Youth unemployment stood at 14% by the end of 2023, and the proportion of discouraged workers among NEET young people is at its lowest recorded level [9]. However, 36% of 15–29 year-olds had temporary contracts in 2022, nearly half of young people want to change jobs within a year, and young people are less satisfied with their jobs than older cohorts [9]. Housing difficulties are among the main obstacles to young people becoming independent, and among young respondents across the EU, 40% would move abroad in the next three years if they could [9].

### 1.2 Onboarding Experiences

The challenges of remote onboarding for early-career professionals are well-documented. In the United States, 36% of remote workers found onboarding confusing [10]. Forty-four percent of new hires regret their decision after the first week in a remote environment, 43% waited over a week for basic tools, and 70% decide whether to stay within the first month [11]. Remote employees are 117% more likely to plan to leave than on-site employees [11].

In Europe, researchers describe the phenomenon of "arrested development" for remote entry-level workers, referring to the lack of informal feedback and "water cooler" learning opportunities [12]. A study revealed that employees who began their roles remotely were 7% less productive than those who started on-site [12]. Young workers (16–19) stay at jobs about 8 months on average, with miscommunication and generational differences being major factors [12].

### 1.3 Promotion Trajectories

Research has found that hybrid workers face a 7.7% lower probability of promotion and 7.1% lower salary increase due to "statistical discrimination"—managerial bias based on assumptions, not actual performance [13]. When managers were informed that hybrid and on-site workers had identical performance, the penalty vanished entirely [13].

A study of Fortune 500 companies found that entry-level remote employees had a 50% lower promotion rate than on-site colleagues [12]. A global survey of 4,000 executives by IMD found that over a quarter believed remote work hindered career development [12]. KPMG's 2023 CEO Outlook report found that nearly 90% of 1,325 CEOs of large companies in 11 countries are considering linking promotions, raises, and other rewards with in-office attendance [14]. Thirty percent of remote/hybrid workers feel that working from home lowers their chances for professional growth [15].

The NY Fed researchers emphasize that early-career unemployment can have lasting consequences: "Research finds that individuals who began looking for jobs in slacker labor markets tend to have lower earnings and slower career progression" [1].

### 1.4 Mentorship Access

Mentorship is one of the areas most profoundly affected by remote work. At a Fortune 500 tech firm, software engineers got about 20% more feedback when sitting near colleagues than when distant [5]. After the pandemic, feedback plummeted, hitting young workers hardest. The company shifted from hiring new grads to hiring workers a decade older on average, then resumed hiring younger workers after implementing a "pretty aggressive" return-to-office policy [5].

Feedback on coding work increased 18.3% when workers were in the office, improving the quality of output. Younger workers disproportionately benefited from in-person mentorship and feedback sessions, while periods of flexible work at their company had "scarring effects" on young graduates' development [3]. In a remote setting, junior employees often struggle with reaching out to their mentors, unsure of the appropriate mode of communication [16].

The statistics on mentorship are striking: 83% of Gen Z workers say having a workplace mentor is crucial to their career development, yet only 52% report having one [17]. Fifty-six percent of American workers say they do not have a mentor, and 34% say a lack of mentorship has held them back in their careers [17]. Employees participating in mentorship programs are 49% less likely to leave, and employees with mentors are 5 times more likely to be promoted [18]. Sixty-five percent of early talent would leave their existing company for one with more mentoring opportunities [18]. Only 15% of employees say their manager helped them build a career plan in the past six months—a decline of 5 percentage points from 2024 [19]. Forty-four percent of workers aged 18 to 34 are considering getting a new job due to insufficient learning and development opportunities [19].

In Europe, formal mentoring programs are emerging to address these gaps. The ENGAGE.EU Mentoring Programme is a 6-month international initiative connecting early-career professionals with experienced mentors from ten universities [20]. MentorCruise connects individuals with remote work mentors from companies like Airbnb, Amazon, Meta, Microsoft, Spotify, and Uber, reporting a 97% satisfaction rate and 2 times faster goal achievement [21].

## 2. Skills Required for Remote Positions Compared to Traditional Office Jobs

### 2.1 Soft Skills and Self-Management Competencies

Research from Stanford, the Carnegie Foundation, and Harvard University found that 85% of professional success can be attributed to soft skills, with a minority coming from technical expertise [22]. In remote environments, these soft skills become even more critical because collaboration happens entirely through digital channels.

The top 5 skills remote employers are looking for in 2025 include: communication (mastering Zoom, Slack, and written communication; articulating thoughts clearly across email, chat, and video calls); time management and self-management (using Trello, Asana, Clockify; organization, task prioritization, meeting deadlines); proficiency with collaboration tools (Google Workspace, Microsoft Teams, Notion); adaptability (thriving in dynamic environments, staying calm under pressure, proactive concern resolution); and self-motivation (delivering results with minimal supervision, discipline) [10][22].

Additional soft skills identified by multiple sources include emotional intelligence, problem-solving, collaboration, building connections, cultural sensitivity, attention to detail, professionalism, and reliability [10][22]. For entry-level remote jobs, many roles prioritize transferable skills such as communication, organization, and digital literacy over formal qualifications [23].

The National Careers Service in the UK highlights communication, organization, and digital skills as key attributes valued by modern employers for remote roles [23].

### 2.2 Technical and Digital Skills

**AI Skills**

The 2024 Microsoft/LinkedIn Work Trend Index found that 75% of knowledge workers now use AI at work, with 46% adopting it within the past six months [24]. Sixty-six percent of leaders wouldn't hire someone without AI skills, and 71% would choose a less experienced candidate with AI skills over a highly experienced one without [24]. Only 39% of users have received AI training from their company, and only 25% of companies expect to offer it this year [24]. LinkedIn data shows a 142 times increase in members adding AI skills like Copilot and ChatGPT to their profiles, and a 160% increase in nontechnical professionals using LinkedIn Learning courses to build their AI aptitude [24]. AI mentions in LinkedIn job posts drive a 17% bump in application growth [24].

The World Economic Forum's Future of Jobs Report 2025 finds that 39% of existing skill sets will be transformed or become outdated over the 2025–2030 period [25]. Analytical thinking remains the most sought-after core skill. AI and big data, networks and cybersecurity, and technological literacy are the fastest-growing skills. If the global workforce were 100 people, 59 would need training by 2030. Skill gaps are the biggest barrier to business transformation, cited by 63% of employers [25].

**Digital Skills in Europe**

In Europe, the demand for digital skills is particularly acute. Between 2021 and 2022, 46% of European companies said they took action to embrace more digital processes [26]. However, four out of ten workers in Europe lack basic digital skills, according to the European Commission [26]. More than 90% of jobs in Europe require basic digital knowledge, yet approximately 32% of Europeans still lack basic digital skills [27]. The EU aims to ensure that at least 80% of the EU population possesses basic digital skills by 2030 [27].

An MDPI study published in *Economies* (2025) examined the impact of specific digital skills on employability across 32 European countries [28]. The findings indicate that three proxies for digital skills—communication and collaboration, digital content creation, and safety—significantly and positively influence employability. The results point out that greater employability of the workforce above the basic level of digital skills is required. It is not even desirable that the worker has only a basic level of digital skills because it is negatively related to their employability [28].

Communication and collaboration skills at above-basic levels have a statistically significant positive influence on employment rate (β=1.305, p<0.01). Digital content creation at above-basic level has a significant positive influence (β=0.278, p<0.01). Safety skills at above-basic level have a significant positive influence (β=0.421, p<0.001) [28].

The most in-demand digital skills in Europe fall into two categories: productivity tools (Microsoft Office, Excel, PowerPoint—the most sought-after across France, Italy, Germany, the UK, and Spain) and advanced digital skills (programming languages, automation, computer science) [26]. In Germany, almost half of job postings requested at least one digital skill (2019–2022), followed by other countries at approximately 40%. France stands out with a growth of 14%, while Germany's gain of 2% is much more modest. Job postings in Spain request the highest volume of digital skills (a skills degree of 32%), and Germany takes second place, with digital skills taking up 27% of all skills requirements in job postings [26].

The Digital Skills Indicator 2.0 (DSI) from Eurostat measures digital competence across five areas: information and data literacy, communication and collaboration, digital content creation, safety, and problem-solving [27]. Age is a significant factor, with a clear trend showing higher digital skills in younger age groups and a decline as age increases [27]. However, data suggests significant variation between Member States, ranging from 30% to 80% of inhabitants having at least basic digital skills [27]. Citizens in Denmark, the Netherlands, and Sweden have the highest confidence (exceeding 90%) in digital technologies' importance by 2030 [27].

**In-Demand Hard Skills for Remote Roles**

In-demand hard skills for remote roles in 2026–2027 include digital marketing (8% projected hiring increase 2023–2033, US BLS), cybersecurity (33% projected hiring increase for Information Security Analysts, US BLS; 70% of cybersecurity professionals work remotely), data analytics (among the highest-paying remote jobs, a competitive field requiring certifications), and business communication (a transferable skill for career growth) [22].

Hiring trends for 2026–2027 show employers increasingly prioritizing outcome-based performance, asynchronous collaboration, and digital autonomy. Key qualifications include proficiency with collaboration platforms, documentation-first workflows, self-directed learning, and AI literacy. Recruiters favor candidates with globally recognized, self-paced online certifications [22].

### 2.3 Skills-Based Hiring

Skills-based hiring is on the rise. The NACE Job Outlook 2025 report found that for the first time, nearly two-thirds of employers reported using skills-based hiring, with over half using it always or most of the time [6]. Interviewing and screening are the most common application points. Fifty-nine percent of employers require certifications or training for entry-level workers, and 78% would pay more for certified applicants; 55% would pay 10–20% more [29]. Top lacking skills among entry-level workers include time management, work ethic and dependability, communication, positivity, and professionalism [29]. Forty-seven percent of employers indicated they will screen by GPA—up from 37% and 38.3% in 2023 and 2024, respectively [6]. Only 35% of employers believe new graduates are adequately prepared for the workforce, citing gaps in digital skills and adaptability [30].

### 2.4 Specific Skills for Entry-Level Remote Roles

Entry-level remote roles typically require some experience but usually not a lot [31]. Many employers today use applicant tracking systems (ATS) to filter job applications, searching for keywords that match the original job description [31]. Key skills for specific entry-level remote roles include: SOC Analyst (technical skills and certifications like CompTIA Security+; bachelor's degree often preferred but not always required); Data Analyst (ability to sort and interpret data; degree preferred but certificates can substitute); Web Developer (HTML, JavaScript, and multimedia tools; no specific degree required); Social Media Manager (bachelor's degree in advertising, business, or communications often preferred); and Project Coordinator (no specific degree required; professional certifications can help) [31].

For beginners, the most accessible remote jobs include Virtual Assistant, Data Entry Clerk, Customer Service Representative, Online Chat Agent, Content Writer, Proofreader, Social Media Assistant, and Online Research Assistant. These roles prioritize transferable skills over formal qualifications [23].

## 3. Earning Potential and Professional Networking

### 3.1 Salary Differentials and Compensation Parity

**United States**

The relationship between remote work and compensation is complex and varies significantly depending on the analysis. According to CNBC (March 2024), salaries for fully in-office roles in the U.S. have climbed significantly, with companies offering an average of $82,037 for in-person positions—a nearly 40% jump from $59,085 in 2023 [32]. Data from ZipRecruiter shows that workers who switched from a remote job to an in-office job in 2023 received a 29.2% pay bump, nearly double that of those who left an in-person job to work remotely. Hybrid roles pay $59,992 on average (up from $54,034 in 2023), while remote jobs average $75,327 (up from $69,107) [32].

However, when controlling for job title and experience, remote salaries are essentially the same as non-remote jobs. Bloomberry's analysis of 1 million fully remote job postings found that non-remote jobs were only 0.6% higher on average when controlling for job title and experience [13]. BLS research (October 2024) found no statistically significant relationship between the growth in remote work and the growth in hourly compensation. The productivity gains from remote work accrued to businesses, not workers. A 1 percentage-point increase in remote workers is associated with a 0.08 percentage-point increase in TFP growth (2019–2021), but this did not translate into higher worker compensation [33].

Remote workers earn approximately 12–13% more than on-site workers overall, but with a 6% "work-from-home premium" after controlling for worker characteristics [34]. This is likely because remote workers tend to be more educated and in higher-paying roles. High-income U.S. households ($200K+) telework at 73.1%, while low-income households (<$25K) telework at only 12.7% [34]. Remote work is strongly associated with higher income and education levels.

Despite the complexity of the data, 59% of workers would take a pay cut to work remotely, and 58% said they would take a pay cut to work remotely according to a 2025 survey [15][22]. Julia Pollak, ZipRecruiter's chief economist, notes: "If employers can't compete on flexibility, they're having to compete more aggressively on pay. The hope is that better pay might be enough to draw people back to the office" [32].

Entry-level remote jobs can pay up to $120,000 in 2026, according to Nathan Soto, a career expert at Resume Genius [35]. The highest-paying entry-level remote roles include Software Developer ($108,896/year), Aeronautical Engineer ($103,595/year), Product Manager ($105,434/year), UX Designer ($99,082/year), Automation Engineer ($95,994/year), and Auditor ($91,866/year) [36]. FlexJobs data shows entry-level remote jobs paying well include Software Engineer ($72K–$142K), UX Designer ($60K–$118K), Product Manager ($68K–$141K), Project Manager ($56K–$123K), Security Analyst ($56K–$107K), and Computer Programmer ($53K–$120K) [37].

**Europe**

In Europe, remote roles pay 5–15% less on average than on-site roles [38]. Workers save approximately 4,000–6,000 EUR/year on commuting, and employers save approximately 10,000 EUR/year per remote worker [38]. Twenty-two percent of workers would accept a 15%+ salary cut for remote work [38]. According to Robert Half's 2025 Salary Guide, 66% of managers are willing to increase starting salaries for in-office work, with 59% offering up to 20% more pay to work in office 4–5 days per week [39].

Global salary comparisons reveal significant disparities. The global average software engineer salary is $70,877, with a 12% premium for AI skills [40]. The United States leads globally at $110,140 average. In Europe, Switzerland leads at $97,518 (Zurich $110,123), followed by Denmark ($63,680, Copenhagen $78,000), the United Kingdom ($55,275, London $73,993), Germany ($52,275, Munich $66,880), and Sweden ($50,437, Stockholm $54,000) [40].

At equal salary levels, European employment costs are generally higher than US costs due to extensive employer social contributions (e.g., Paris employer costs reach 59.1% above base salary vs. 10–20% in most US cities). At local market rates, European total employment costs are 26–48% lower than US costs because European salaries are significantly below US salaries. Net pay differences are even larger than cost differences. A Seattle developer takes home $101,262 (nearly 2.6 times a Paris developer at $38,365). However, cost of living can reverse perceived advantages: London developers have twice the disposable income after expenses ($25,080) vs. New York ($11,894), despite New York's 46% higher net pay [41].

US companies typically pay 60–80% of US local salaries for remote European workers. Most engineers optimize for the $120,000–$180,000 sweet spot with excellent work-life balance rather than pushing for maximum income [42].

### 3.2 Professional Networking, Informal Learning, and Social Capital

**Informal Learning**

The impact of remote work on informal learning is one of the most significant but underappreciated consequences for early-career professionals. Informal learning accounts for 91% of the time workers spend on activities from which they learn, compared to formal training courses [43]. Workers on average perceive the learning intensity of an hour of informal learning to be as high as that of an hour of formal training [43].

A master's dissertation from the University of Porto (2023/2024) found that remote work can reduce informal learning opportunities, potentially reducing both vertical and horizontal mobility and increasing disengagement with the company [44]. Remote work creates "out of sight, out of mind" dynamics, reducing spontaneous interactions ("learning by the water cooler") and making employees rely more on "strong ties" than "weak ties" (which offer new information). Face-to-face communication is the richest channel for processing in-depth information, according to media richness theory [44].

Knowledge spillovers among peers matter significantly. New hires in teams with experienced peers need 110 hours to become proficient vs. 161 hours for less experienced teams. Training some workers can improve untrained coworkers' performance by 2.5% [43].

**Networking**

Forty percent of individuals find networking difficult, and 50–80% of jobs are filled through networking [18]. A study by the Wharton School (2026) found that 25% of mentees experienced a salary grade change compared to only 5% of those without mentoring programs [19]. Eighty-three percent of professionals who have had a mentor reported being satisfied with their career progression [19].

In Europe, 26% of remote workers cite a negative impact on mental health, 67% feel less connected to colleagues, and 75% are more likely to feel lonely [45]. Twenty-two percent find it hard to switch off after work, and 55% suffer from video call fatigue [45]. Twenty-nine percent cite communication gaps, 22% report loneliness, and 38% of managers struggle with collaboration [46].

**Generational Differences**

The desire for mentorship is particularly strong among younger generations. Eighty-three percent of Gen Z workers believe having a workplace mentor is important for their career, yet only 52% report having one [17]. Forty-nine percent of Millennials are looking at quitting within two years, mainly due to lack of career advancement and learning opportunities. Millennials with mentors are 68% more likely to stay 5+ years [17].

While Gen Z craves in-person mentorship and opportunities for growth, research shows they also appreciate the flexibility of remote work [16]. Gallup found Gen Z to be the age group least likely to prefer a fully remote workplace setup, citing in part the lack of interaction with coworkers [3]. Gen Z comes to the office 3 days per week on average [47].

**The "Arrested Development" Challenge**

Frederik Anseel of UNSW Business School describes the lack of informal feedback and "water cooler" learning opportunities for remote entry-level workers as an "arrested development" issue [12]. This phenomenon is particularly concerning because early-career experiences can have lasting consequences. Research consistently finds that individuals who begin looking for jobs in weaker labor markets tend to have lower earnings and slower career progression throughout their working lives [1].

## 4. Industries and Companies Most Committed to Flexible Work Arrangements

### 4.1 Top Industries for Remote Work

**United States**

The technology sector leads remote work adoption in the United States. According to multiple data sources, the technology industry has 67.8% of workers in remote roles, followed by agencies and consulting at 50.6% and finance and insurance at 48.7% [48]. The computer and IT industry leads in remote adoption at 89% of jobs, followed by business and finance at 86% and architecture and engineering at 82% [49].

Finance and Insurance has the highest U.S. telework rate at 61.5%, followed by Professional and Technical Services at 54.8%, and IT and Communications at 46.7% [34]. According to the California Legislative Analyst's Office (May 2026), four job groups are "heavily remote": technology (38% remote), finance and accounting (24%), business and government operations (30%), and sales and marketing (31%) [50]. These groups include roughly 2.6 million California workers (1 in 7 statewide) and pay wages twice as high as other jobs [50].

The most common remote job postings in 2023 included Accountant, Executive Assistant, Financial Analyst, Project Manager, Customer Service Representative, Software Engineer, Customer Success Manager, Accounting Manager, Product Designer, and Writer [51]. Technical writers, customer success specialists, and product designers are most likely to be remote. Account executives, software engineers, and digital marketers have the highest volume of remote openings [13].

**Europe**

In Europe, IT leads with 72% of workers currently hybrid or remote (85% remote-capable), followed by Financial Services (64%), Professional Services (58%), and Marketing and Media (55%) [38]. Manufacturing has only 6% remote adoption, Healthcare 8%, and Retail and Hospitality 4% [38].

In the United Kingdom, the top sectors for remote work are Finance (24.6% of roles), Leisure and Tourism (20.3%), Insurance (17.7%), and Sales (13.6%). Retail, manufacturing, social care, transport, and education offer less than 1% remote roles [52]. IT and communications has the highest rate at 80%+, while accommodation and food services has the lowest at 8% [45].

In Germany, IT services lead at approximately 75% (at least partially working from home), followed by administration and office-based roles at over 70%, financial and insurance services at approximately 50%, and education at approximately 30%. Healthcare is at approximately 6%, and trades and manufacturing are near 0% [53].

### 4.2 Companies Most Committed to Remote Work

**Remote-First Companies in the Technology Sector**

The most committed remote-first companies are predominantly in the technology sector. GitLab operates with a fully remote team across 60+ countries, employing 2,000+ people with a transparent location-based salary calculator and a 2,000-page public handbook. Salary ranges for entry-level include $115,000–$194,000 for a Fullstack Engineer in the US [54][55][56]. Zapier has been remote-first since 2011, with 800+ employees across 38 countries, profit sharing, and a $10,000 annual stipend [54][55][56]. Buffer operates fully remote with approximately 75 employees across 15 countries, offering a 4-day workweek, public salary transparency, and a salary formula that adjusts to cost of living and experience [54][55][56].

Other notable remote-first companies include Automattic (makers of WordPress, 1,900 employees across 90+ countries, location-independent salaries), Doist (makers of Todoist/Twist, approximately 100 employees across 35+ countries, 40 days PTO), Atlassian (7,000+ remote workers across 13 countries), GitHub (2,000+ distributed employees), and Help Scout (approximately 140 employees across 115+ cities, location-agnostic pay, month-long sabbatical) [54][55][56].

Over 750 remote-first and remote-friendly companies and startups are listed on NoDesk, including 1Password, Atlassian, GitHub, GitLab, Figma, Cloudflare, 15Five, Gusto, Brex, Deel, Coursera, Duolingo, and others [57].

**Big 4 Consulting Firms**

The Big Four accounting firms (KPMG, Deloitte, Ernst & Young, and PwC) are maintaining hybrid work policies despite corporate and government pushes for full-time in-office work [58]. KPMG US has approximately 57% of its workforce in-person each week with no firm-wide mandate. PwC US employees spend about 50% of their time in-person. EY US found that teams spending 40%–60% of time in-person achieves an optimal balance. Deloitte uses a team-led model focused on co-location when it matters most [58].

Industry experts note that rigid return-to-office policies could worsen pipeline and retention issues, particularly affecting women and minority groups. A PCAOB report noted that hybrid work may make learning harder for entry-level audit employees [58]. The firms acknowledge challenges with hybrid setups, including difficulties with soft skills training and relationship building, but view flexibility as essential for addressing diverse workforce needs and talent competition [58].

**Investment Banking**

Investment banking is traditionally the least flexible financial sector for junior staff, but some firms are adopting hybrid models. Lazard offers optional hybrid with remote on Mondays and Fridays and one month per year of work from anywhere [59]. Morgan Stanley has hybrid working arrangements [59]. Piper Sandler offers protected Saturdays (no work from Friday 8 p.m. to Sunday 9 a.m.) and remote work support [60]. Baird lists global investment banking positions as hybrid roles [61].

However, JPMorgan Chase has moved to end remote work entirely and call employees back to the office full-time, representing a significant shift in the industry [58].

**European Graduate Programs**

Several European companies offer structured graduate programs with remote or hybrid flexibility. Avery Dennison's European Graduate Program is a two-year rotational program offering challenging assignments within the graduate's area of expertise, often across two different European countries. The program includes a mentoring program, on-the-job learning, and classroom/virtual training, offered in Operations/Manufacturing, Data Science, Supply Chain, Research & Development, Commercial (Marketing & Sales), and Finance [62].

The Wikimedia Foundation is a remote-first organization with staff members including contractors based in 40+ countries. It hires in France, Germany, Netherlands, Poland, Spain, Switzerland, and the UK, with roles such as Software Engineer III ($92,267–$144,201 US) and a Legal Fellow (Fall 2026) for current law students or recent graduates [63][56].

### 4.3 US vs. Europe Comparison

**Adoption Levels**

The United States and Europe have different patterns of remote work adoption. Among U.S. remote-capable employees, 55% are hybrid, 26% are fully remote, and 19% are fully on-site [47]. In Europe, the EMEA region is 75% hybrid, making it more flexible than North America at 60% hybrid [47]. European countries average 1–1.5 work-from-home days per week, compared to 1.5–2 days in North America, the UK, and Australia [64].

The Netherlands is the European leader with 52% working from home sometimes or most of the time [65]. Nordic countries also lead adoption, with Finland at 35% remote in 2023 [65]. Southern Europe lags behind, with Italy at 10% and Spain at 12% [66]. France is an outlier with fewer than 3% fully remote, despite strong labor protections [67].

**Legal and Policy Frameworks**

Europe has significantly stronger labor protections that influence remote work. The EU has a 2002 Framework Agreement on Telework, and several European countries have implemented "right to disconnect" laws. As of 2026, nine European countries have statutory right-to-disconnect frameworks, though no EU directive exists to harmonize them [68].

France was the first country to legislate a formal right to disconnect in 2017. Companies with 50+ employees must negotiate disconnection agreements, and fines can reach €3,750 per employee plus GDPR penalties of 4% of global turnover [69][70]. Spain's right to disconnect applies to all workers with penalties ranging from €40,001 to €20,000,000 [69]. Portugal has the most employer-restrictive disconnection law globally, prohibiting employers from contacting workers outside working hours except in situations of force majeure, with fines from €2,040 to €61,200 [69][70].

Germany has no standalone right-to-disconnect law, but protection comes from the 11-hour mandatory rest period under the Working Hours Act and works council co-determination rights [71][72]. The UK's Employment Relations (Flexible Working) Act 2023 provides a day-one right to request flexible working from April 2024 [73].

In the United States, there is no federal right-to-disconnect law. California's AB 2751 was expected to be reintroduced [69]. Since January 2025, over 212,000 women aged 20 and up have exited the U.S. workforce, a trend partially attributed to the reduction of remote work options following a presidential memorandum instructing executive agencies to end remote work arrangements [74].

**Cultural Attitudes**

Cultural attitudes toward remote work also differ. Seventy-four percent of EU workers prefer to work from home at least occasionally [66]. Eighty-three percent of global workers prefer hybrid arrangements [46]. WEC-Europe (representing the HR services industry) argues against EU legislative intervention on telework and the right to disconnect, advocating instead for flexible, non-binding guidance, noting that 83% of respondents acknowledge that workers now place as much value on flexibility around where and when they work as they do on compensation [75].

English-speaking countries (Canada, UK, US, Ireland) are more likely to allow remote work than non-English-speaking European countries [67]. The United States also has a more pronounced return-to-office push, with major companies like Amazon, Dell, JPMorgan Chase, Boeing, AT&T, and UPS implementing full-time office mandates [47]. In contrast, European companies tend to maintain hybrid arrangements [47][58].

**Access Inequality**

In both regions, remote work access is highly unequal. People with degrees are about 10 times more likely to work hybrid than those without a degree in the UK [52][76]. Workers earning over £50,000 are significantly more likely to work hybrid (45%) or fully remote (24%) compared to those earning under £20,000 (8% hybrid, 9% remote) [52]. In the United States, telework participation rises sharply with education: less than high school (3.5%), high school graduates (8.5%), some college (18.3%), bachelor's degree or higher (40.4%), and advanced degrees (43.6%) [34].

---

## 5. Conclusion

The evidence is clear and mounting: remote work is fundamentally reshaping the labor market for early-career professionals in ways that are largely negative for those just entering the workforce. In both the United States and Europe, employers are shifting hiring preferences away from inexperienced candidates when roles are remote, creating a significant barrier to entry for recent graduates.

The Federal Reserve Bank of New York's finding that remote work can explain 64% of the increase in youth unemployment among college graduates is particularly striking [1]. The Harvard Business Review research on 50 million European job postings confirms that this is a transatlantic phenomenon [2]. The UK government's interim report on nearly one million young people NEET underscores the severity of the crisis [8].

The implications extend beyond hiring. Once employed, early-career remote workers face lower promotion rates, reduced mentorship access, and diminished informal learning opportunities. The "arrested development" phenomenon described by researchers [12] threatens to create lasting career damage for a generation of workers who entered the labor market during this period of transition.

The skills required for remote work are also shifting. AI literacy is becoming essential, with 66% of leaders saying they wouldn't hire someone without AI skills [24]. Digital skills are increasingly important in both regions, though Europe faces a more acute digital skills gap, with four out of ten workers lacking basic digital skills [26].

Compensation presents a mixed picture. While remote workers earn more on average due to their concentration in higher-paying roles and industries, when controlling for job title and experience, remote salaries are essentially the same as non-remote jobs [13]. The productivity gains from remote work have largely accrued to businesses, not workers [33].

The most committed industries and companies are predominantly in technology, with firms like GitLab, Zapier, Buffer, and Automattic leading the way in remote-first employment. The Big Four consulting firms are maintaining hybrid models despite industry-wide RTO pushes. However, the trend toward hybrid rather than fully remote work is clear, with fully remote work declining from 34% in 2022 to 9% in 2025 [15].

Europe's stronger labor protections, including right-to-disconnect laws and the UK's Employment Relations (Flexible Working) Act, provide a more supportive regulatory environment for remote workers. However, the United States has higher adoption rates of fully remote work and a more developed remote-first company ecosystem.

The future for early-career professionals is uncertain. The technology sector leads projected growth at 35%, followed by finance at 20% and healthcare at 15% [66]. Global projections suggest 92 million fully remote jobs by 2030, up from 73 million in 2023 [34]. However, unless employers and policymakers address the structural barriers facing early-career workers in remote environments—through improved mentorship programs, better onboarding practices, and more intentional career development—the current generation of young professionals may face lasting career consequences from this transformation.
