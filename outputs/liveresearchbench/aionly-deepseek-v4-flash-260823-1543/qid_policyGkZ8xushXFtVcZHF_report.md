# OCR HIPAA Enforcement Actions: Comprehensive Analysis of High-Penalty Cases (August 2021 – August 2026)

## Executive Summary

This report provides a detailed analysis of the most recent Office for Civil Rights (OCR) HIPAA enforcement actions from approximately August 2021 through August 2026, focusing specifically on cases involving penalties exceeding $1 million. The analysis covers seven high-penalty cases against covered entities (health plans, hospitals, physician practices, and medical suppliers), totaling over $18 million in financial penalties. The research identifies risk analysis failures as the most frequently cited violation across all cases, and documents the specific corrective action requirements that OCR imposes through resolution agreements and corrective action plans (CAPs).

---

## Section 1: Top 10 Security Rule Violations Leading to Large Penalties

Based on the seven enforcement actions with penalties exceeding $1 million identified during this period, the following is a ranked list of HIPAA Security Rule violations by frequency of citation. The analysis draws directly from official OCR resolution agreements, civil money penalty determinations, and case summaries posted on the HHS/OCR website.

### Rank 1: Failure to Conduct an Accurate and Thorough Risk Analysis (45 CFR § 164.308(a)(1)(ii)(A))

**Frequency:** 7 out of 7 cases (100%)

This is the cornerstone violation of the HIPAA Security Rule and appears in every single high-penalty case during this period. The regulation requires covered entities and business associates to conduct an accurate and thorough assessment of potential risks and vulnerabilities to the confidentiality, integrity, and availability of all electronic protected health information (ePHI). OCR has stated that inadequate risk analysis is involved in roughly 90% of all HIPAA Security Rule enforcement actions [1].

**Cases citing this violation:** Excellus Health Plan (2021), Banner Health (2023), L.A. Care Health Plan (2023), Montefiore Medical Center (2024), Gulf Coast Pain Consultants (2024), Solara Medical Supplies (2025), Warby Parker (2025) [2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8]

### Rank 2: Failure to Implement Information System Activity Review (45 CFR § 164.308(a)(1)(ii)(D))

**Frequency:** 6 out of 7 cases (86%)

This provision requires entities to implement procedures for regularly reviewing records of information system activity, such as audit logs, access reports, and security incident tracking reports. In many cases, entities failed to detect breaches for months or years because they were not monitoring system activity. For example, hackers roamed inside Excellus's systems undetected for over 18 months, and Montefiore did not discover that an employee had been stealing patient data for six months until the NYPD notified the hospital [2, 5].

**Cases citing this violation:** Excellus (2021), Banner Health (2023), L.A. Care (2023), Montefiore (2024), Gulf Coast Pain Consultants (2024), Warby Parker (2025) [2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 8]

### Rank 3: Failure to Implement Risk Management Measures (45 CFR § 164.308(a)(1)(ii)(B))

**Frequency:** 4 out of 7 cases (57%)

This provision requires entities to implement security measures sufficient to reduce risks and vulnerabilities identified in the risk analysis to a reasonable and appropriate level. It is not enough to simply identify risks; entities must take documented action to mitigate them. OCR Director Paula M. Stannard has stated that the risk analysis enforcement initiative will evolve in 2026 to expand to include risk management, meaning OCR now expects entities to prove they acted on identified risks with documented remediation efforts [1, 9].

**Cases citing this violation:** Excellus (2021), L.A. Care (2023), Solara Medical Supplies (2025), Warby Parker (2025) [2, 4, 7, 8]

### Rank 4: Failure to Implement Access Controls (45 CFR § 164.312(a)(1))

**Frequency:** 2 out of 7 cases (29%)

This standard requires entities to implement policies and procedures for authorizing access to ePHI, ensuring that only authorized persons or software programs with a legitimate need can access ePHI. In the Excellus case, inadequate access controls allowed hackers to roam undetected for over a year. In Gulf Coast, the entity failed to terminate a former contractor's access, allowing unauthorized access to the EMR system [2, 6].

**Cases citing this violation:** Excellus (2021), Gulf Coast Pain Consultants (2024) [2, 6]

### Rank 5: Failure to Implement Audit Controls (45 CFR § 164.312(b))

**Frequency:** 2 out of 7 cases (29%)

This standard requires entities to implement hardware, software, and/or procedural mechanisms that record and examine activity in information systems containing or using ePHI. Without audit controls, entities cannot detect unauthorized access, insider threats, or data exfiltration. In both L.A. Care and Montefiore, the inability to record and examine system activity directly contributed to breaches going undetected for extended periods [4, 5].

**Cases citing this violation:** L.A. Care (2023), Montefiore Medical Center (2024) [4, 5]

### Rank 6: Failure to Implement Person or Entity Authentication (45 CFR § 164.312(d))

**Frequency:** 1 out of 7 cases (14%)

This standard requires entities to implement procedures to verify that a person or entity seeking access to ePHI is the one claimed. OCR found that Banner Health failed to implement sufficient authentication procedures, contributing to the hacker's ability to gain unauthorized access [3].

**Cases citing this violation:** Banner Health (2023) [3]

### Rank 7: Failure to Implement Transmission Security (45 CFR § 164.312(e)(1))

**Frequency:** 1 out of 7 cases (14%)

This standard requires entities to implement technical security measures to guard against unauthorized access to ePHI transmitted over an electronic communications network. Banner Health was cited for failing to implement sufficient technical security measures to protect ePHI during transmission [3].

**Cases citing this violation:** Banner Health (2023) [3]

### Rank 8: Failure to Implement Workforce Access Procedures (45 CFR § 164.308(a)(3)(ii)(B))

**Frequency:** 1 out of 7 cases (14%)

This standard requires entities to implement policies and procedures for authorizing, establishing, and modifying a workforce member's access to ePHI based on their role and need-to-know. Gulf Coast Pain Consultants failed to implement proper procedures, allowing a former contractor to retain access [6].

**Cases citing this violation:** Gulf Coast Pain Consultants (2024) [6]

### Rank 9: Failure to Terminate Former Workforce Members' Access (45 CFR § 164.308(a)(3)(ii)(C))

**Frequency:** 1 out of 7 cases (14%)

This standard requires entities to implement policies and procedures for terminating access to ePHI when a workforce member's employment ends. In Gulf Coast's case, a former contractor was able to impermissibly access the EMR system on three occasions because the entity failed to terminate access after the working relationship ended, compromising the ePHI of approximately 34,310 individuals [6].

**Cases citing this violation:** Gulf Coast Pain Consultants (2024) [6]

### Rank 10: Failure to Perform Periodic Evaluations (45 CFR § 164.308(a)(8))

**Frequency:** 1 out of 7 cases (14%)

This standard requires entities to perform periodic technical and nontechnical evaluations in response to environmental or operational changes affecting the security of ePHI. L.A. Care failed to perform such evaluations after introducing and expanding its online payment portal, which ultimately allowed members to view other members' PHI [4].

**Cases citing this violation:** L.A. Care Health Plan (2023) [4]

## Section 2: Common Risk Assessment Failures with Concrete Examples

The analysis of high-penalty cases reveals three distinct types of risk assessment failures that recur across multiple enforcement actions. These failures represent the most significant compliance gaps that OCR identifies in its investigations.

### Failure Type 1: Failure to Conduct an Enterprise-Wide Risk Analysis with Complete Scope

**Description:** The entity either never conducted any risk analysis, or conducted one that failed to cover all locations where ePHI is stored, created, received, maintained, or transmitted. OCR expects a complete inventory of all electronic equipment, data systems, programs, and applications containing ePHI, including endpoints, cloud applications, remote assets, business associate agreement data flows, access-management systems, and audit-logging systems [10].

**Concrete Example 1: Solara Medical Supplies (2025, $3 million penalty)**

OCR found that Solara Medical Supplies, a direct-to-consumer distributor of diabetes supplies, failed to conduct a compliant risk analysis. The Resolution Agreement specifically required Solara to "conduct and complete an accurate, thorough, enterprise-wide analysis of risks and vulnerabilities that incorporates all risks to Solara's ePHI related to electronic equipment, data systems, programs, and applications," including a complete inventory of all electronic equipment, data systems, and applications containing ePHI. The scope and methodology of the risk analysis had to be submitted to OCR within 90 days of the effective date, and the full analysis within 180 days of HHS approval [7, 11].

**Concrete Example 2: Gulf Coast Pain Consultants (2024, $1.19 million penalty)**

The first HIPAA-compliant risk analysis was not conducted until September 30, 2022 — years after the systems were in place and well after the breach had occurred. The Risk Analysis on file did not cover the access-management and audit-logging systems that were ultimately compromised. This case serves as a cautionary tale for small to medium-sized practices, as a small specialty practice was named in a seven-figure settlement because the risk analysis it had on file did not cover the systems that failed [6, 10, 12].

**Concrete Example 3: Montefiore Medical Center (2024, $4.75 million penalty)**

OCR found that Montefiore failed to conduct an accurate and thorough risk analysis of potential risks and vulnerabilities to ePHI, which contributed to an employee being able to steal and sell the ePHI of 12,517 patients over six months without detection. The failure to conduct a comprehensive risk analysis meant that the entity did not identify the vulnerabilities that allowed the malicious insider to access and exfiltrate patient data undetected [5, 13].

### Failure Type 2: Failure to Update Risk Assessments at Regular Intervals or After Material System/Environment Changes

**Description:** Entities either fail to conduct risk assessments on a recurring basis or fail to update their risk assessments after significant changes to their environment, such as migration to cloud, new EHR implementation, changes in technology, changes in operations, or new identified threats. HHS treats the risk analysis as a "living document" requiring annual or material-change updates [10].

**Concrete Example 1: Gulf Coast Pain Consultants (2024, $1.19 million penalty)**

The first HIPAA-compliant risk analysis was not conducted until September 30, 2022 — years after the systems were in place and well after the contractor had improperly accessed ePHI in 2018-2019. This demonstrates that the entity had no regular cadence of risk assessment updates and only conducted one after the breach investigation was already underway. Additionally, policies for reviewing activity in information systems containing ePHI were not implemented until April 10, 2020, and policies for terminating former workforce members' access were first implemented on April 10, 2020 — both well after the contractor had already accessed the system without authorization [6, 12].

**Concrete Example 2: Excellus Health Plan (2021, $5.1 million penalty)**

The breach went undetected for approximately 17 months (December 2013 to May 2015). OCR found that "policies and procedures requiring regular reviews of information system activity [were] lacking." OCR Director Roger Severino stated: "It took Excellus more than 18 months to discover its systems had been breached. OCR found policies and procedures requiring regular reviews of information system activity to be lacking." This indicates a failure to conduct ongoing, periodic risk assessments and reviews of system activity [2, 14].

**Concrete Example 3: L.A. Care Health Plan (2023, $1.3 million penalty)**

OCR found that L.A. Care failed to perform periodic technical and nontechnical evaluations in response to environmental or operational changes affecting the security of ePHI, specifically after introducing and expanding its online payment portal. The breaches occurred amid LA Care's expansion between 2014-2015, including adding 180,000 new members, launching a new online member portal, and increasing online access to health information. The entity failed to evaluate the security implications of these changes, which ultimately allowed members to view other members' PHI [4, 15].

### Failure Type 3: Failure to Adequately Address Identified Risks / Failure to Link Risk Analysis to a Risk Management Plan

**Description:** Even when entities conduct some form of risk analysis, they fail to take documented action to address the identified risks, or they fail to create an operational link between the risk analysis findings and a risk management plan with specific corrective actions, timelines, and responsible parties. As OCR Director Paula M. Stannard has stated, the risk analysis enforcement initiative has evolved to also cover risk management, and "OCR now expects regulated entities to prove not only that they identified risks, but that they acted on them with documented remediation efforts and ongoing risk management" [1, 9].

**Concrete Example 1: Warby Parker (2025, $1.5 million penalty)**

OCR found that Warby Parker failed to implement security measures sufficient to reduce the risks and vulnerabilities to ePHI to a reasonable and appropriate level. Despite being a sophisticated technology company facing a credential stuffing attack — a well-known and foreseeable threat — Warby Parker did not implement adequate security measures until July 29, 2022, years after the breach began. The company had multiple opportunities to address the vulnerability but failed to do so [8, 16].

**Concrete Example 2: Solara Medical Supplies (2025, $3 million penalty)**

OCR found that Solara failed to implement sufficient security measures to reduce the risks and vulnerabilities to ePHI to a reasonable and appropriate level. The Resolution Agreement specifically required Solara to "develop an enterprise-wide Risk Management Plan to address and mitigate any security risks and vulnerabilities identified in the Risk Analysis." This demonstrates that the entity had not connected its risk analysis findings to actual remediation actions, despite having identified vulnerabilities [7, 11].

**Concrete Example 3: Montefiore Medical Center (2024, $4.75 million penalty)**

OCR found that Montefiore failed to implement procedures to regularly review records of information system activity AND failed to implement mechanisms to record and examine activity in all systems containing ePHI — both of which are fundamental risk management steps that should follow from a proper risk analysis. The inability to detect a malicious insider stealing patient data for six months demonstrates a complete breakdown in the link between risk identification and risk mitigation [5, 13].

## Section 3: Typical Corrective Actions Required in Resolution Agreements and Corrective Action Plans

Based on the analysis of resolution agreements and corrective action plans (CAPs) from the high-penalty cases, OCR consistently requires a standard set of corrective actions. These requirements are documented in official OCR resolution agreements and CAPs posted on the HHS/OCR website.

### Mandatory Enterprise-Wide Risk Analysis

This is the single most recurring requirement across all resolution agreements. OCR requires the entity to conduct a complete, accurate, and thorough enterprise-wide risk analysis of all potential risks and vulnerabilities to ePHI across all electronic equipment, data systems, and applications.

**Specific requirements from the Solara Medical Supplies CAP (2024):** The entity must "conduct and complete an accurate, thorough, enterprise-wide analysis of risks and vulnerabilities that incorporates all risks to Solara's ePHI related to electronic equipment, data systems, programs, and applications," including a complete inventory of all electronic equipment, data systems, and applications containing ePHI. The scope and methodology must be submitted to OCR within 90 days, and the full analysis within 180 days of HHS approval [7, 11].

**Specific requirements from the Montefiore Medical Center CAP (2024):** The CAP required a comprehensive security risk analysis including an inventory of all facilities and systems containing ePHI [5, 17].

### Development and Implementation of Risk Management Plan

OCR requires entities to develop and implement an enterprise-wide risk management plan to address and mitigate identified risks and vulnerabilities, with specific deadlines tied to the completion of the risk analysis.

**Specific requirements from the Solara CAP:** The entity must "develop an enterprise-wide Risk Management Plan to address and mitigate any security risks and vulnerabilities identified in the Risk Analysis," with deadlines tied to HHS approval of the Risk Analysis [7, 11].

**Specific requirements from the L.A. Care CAP (2023):** The CAP required a risk management plan as part of the comprehensive corrective action process [4, 15].

### Revision and Implementation of HIPAA Policies and Procedures

All resolution agreements require entities to develop, revise, and implement written policies and procedures to comply with the HIPAA Privacy Rule, Security Rule, and Breach Notification Rule.

**Specific requirements from the Solara CAP:** The entity must "develop, maintain, and revise its written policies and procedures to comply with the Federal standards that govern the privacy and security of individually identifiable health information (45 C.F.R. Part 160 and Subparts A, C, D and E of Part 164, the Privacy Rule, Security Rule, and Breach Notification Rule)" [7, 11].

**Specific requirements from the L.A. Care CAP:** The CAP required development and distribution of related policies and procedures [4, 15].

### Workforce Training on HIPAA Policies and Procedures

OCR requires entities to augment their HIPAA and Security Training Program, with training materials often subject to HHS approval.

**Specific requirements from the Solara CAP:** The entity must "augment HIPAA and Security Training Program, with training materials submitted to HHS for approval" [7, 11].

**Specific requirements from the Montefiore CAP:** The CAP required employee training on revised policies and procedures [5, 17].

### OCR Monitoring for a Defined Compliance Term

OCR actively monitors the entity's compliance for the duration of the CAP. The compliance term typically ranges from two to three years.

**Two-year compliance terms:** Solara Medical Supplies (2024), Montefiore Medical Center (2024), Regional Women's Health Group (2025), SG Health Plan (2026), Assured Imaging (2026), Consociate Health (2026) [7, 5, 11, 18]

**Three-year compliance terms:** L.A. Care Health Plan (2023), Excellus Health Plan (2021), Elgon, Inc. (2024), MMG Fusion (2026) [4, 2, 15, 19]

### Submission of Compliance Reports to OCR

All CAPs require entities to submit regular compliance reports to OCR, including an Implementation Report (typically within 60-120 days of HHS approval of policies/training) and Annual Reports (typically within 60 days after each reporting period) for the duration of the Compliance Term.

**Specific requirements from the Solara CAP:** Must submit an Implementation Report within 120 days of training approval, plus Annual Reports for each year of the Compliance Term [7, 11].

**Specific requirements from the Elgon, Inc. CAP (2024):** Must submit an Implementation Report and Annual Reports with attestations signed by an owner or officer confirming implementation of policies, workforce training completion, and accuracy of information [19].

### Reporting of Security Incidents and Workforce Violations to OCR

Several CAPs require entities to report workforce member sanctions for HIPAA policy violations to OCR within 30 days.

**Specific requirements from the L.A. Care CAP:** Must report employee HIPAA violations to OCR within 30 days. Must report compliance failures to HHS within 30 days [4, 15].

**Specific requirements from the Solara CAP:** Must report workforce member sanctions for policy violations within 30 days (designated as "Reportable Events") [7, 11].

### Document Retention Requirements

CAPs uniformly require entities to maintain all documents and records relating to CAP compliance for six years from the Effective Date.

**Cases with this requirement:** L.A. Care (2023), Solara Medical Supplies (2024), Elgon, Inc. (2024), Regional Women's Health Group (2025), SG Health Plan (2026) [4, 7, 11, 15, 18, 19]

### Inventory of Systems and Assets Containing ePHI

Several CAPs specifically require the entity to create a complete inventory of all electronic equipment, data systems, and applications containing ePHI as part of the risk analysis process.

**Specific requirements from the Solara CAP:** Must include "a complete inventory of all electronic equipment, data systems, and applications containing ePHI" as part of the risk analysis [7, 11].

**Specific requirements from the Montefiore CAP:** The CAP required an inventory of all facilities and systems containing ePHI [5, 17].

### Implementation of Audit Controls

Several CAPs require entities to implement hardware, software, and/or procedural mechanisms to record and examine activity in all information systems that contain or use ePHI.

**Specific requirements from the Montefiore CAP:** The CAP required implementing audit controls as part of the corrective action process [5, 17].

**Specific requirements from the Premera Blue Cross CAP (2020):** The CAP required revised policies covering access/audit controls [20].

### Annual Certifications of Compliance by Entity Leadership

Several CAPs require annual reports to include attestations signed by an owner or officer confirming implementation of policies, completion of workforce training, and accuracy of reported information.

**Specific requirements from the Elgon, Inc. CAP:** Must submit Annual Reports with attestations signed by an owner or officer confirming implementation of policies, workforce training completion, and accuracy of information [19].

### Breach/Cure Provisions

CAPs include provisions that if the entity breaches the CAP, HHS may impose a Civil Monetary Penalty under 45 C.F.R. Part 160. The entity typically has a cure period (30-60 days) to demonstrate compliance before penalties are imposed.

**Specific requirements from the Solara CAP:** If Solara breaches the CAP, HHS may impose a CMP. Solara has 60 days to cure or demonstrate compliance [7, 11].

**Specific requirements from the Regional Women's Health Group CAP (2025):** The agreement includes breach provisions with a 30-day cure period before HHS may impose civil money penalties [18].

### No Admission of Liability

Resolution agreements typically include a statement that the settlement does not constitute an admission of liability by the settling entity. This is standard across all resolution agreements reviewed [2, 4, 5, 7, 11, 15, 17, 18, 19].

## Conclusion

The analysis of OCR HIPAA enforcement actions from August 2021 through August 2026 reveals a clear and consistent enforcement pattern. Risk analysis failures under 45 CFR § 164.308(a)(1)(ii)(A) are the most frequently cited violation, appearing in 100% of high-penalty cases. The three most common types of risk assessment failures are: (1) failure to conduct enterprise-wide risk analyses with complete scope covering all ePHI locations; (2) failure to update risk assessments at regular intervals or after material system changes; and (3) failure to link risk analysis findings to risk management plans with documented remediation.

OCR consistently requires a standard set of corrective actions in resolution agreements and CAPs, including mandatory enterprise-wide risk analysis, risk management plan development, policy revision, workforce training, OCR monitoring for 2-3 years, regular compliance reporting, incident reporting, document retention for six years, and audit controls implementation. The enforcement data shows that OCR has resolved over 50 HIPAA violation cases under its risk analysis and Right of Access enforcement initiatives, with total penalties in 2024 reaching $9.9 million and in 2025 reaching $6.7 million [1, 21].

The message from OCR is clear: a comprehensive risk analysis is one of the simplest and most effective tools to protect against data breaches, and failing to complete one can directly lead to regulatory scrutiny and meaningful financial consequences. As OCR Director Paula M. Stannard stated in April 2026, "Hacking and ransomware are the most frequent type of large breach reported to OCR. Proactively implementing the HIPAA Security Rule before a breach or an OCR investigation not only is the law but also is a regulated entity's best opportunity to prevent or mitigate the harmful effects of a successful cyberattack" [9].
