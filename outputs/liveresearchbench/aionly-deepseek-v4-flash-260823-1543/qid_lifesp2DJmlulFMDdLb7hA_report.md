# Body Recomposition in Recreationally Active Adults (18–35): A Comprehensive Evidence Synthesis of Training, Nutrition, and Maintenance Interventions (2015–2026)

## 1. Introduction and Scope

This report synthesizes the international peer-reviewed literature and consensus guidelines published between 2015 and 2026 on body recomposition—the simultaneous reduction of adiposity and preservation or gain of lean body mass—in recreationally active adults aged 18–35. The brief addresses eight specific subtopics: (1) protein intake targets and timing, (2) resistance training periodization models, (3) high-intensity interval training (HIIT) versus moderate-intensity continuous training (MICT), (4) caloric deficit magnitude, (5) meal-timing protocols, (6) long-term maintenance strategies, (7) consensus guidelines from major organizations, and (8) evidence quality assessment.

The central conclusion emerging from this evidence base is that **body recomposition is achievable in recreationally trained adults, but it is optimized through the interaction of three primary variables: a modest-to-moderate energy deficit (typically ≤500 kcal/day), elevated protein intake (≥1.6 g/kg/day, with higher intakes up to 2.4–3.1 g/kg/day during aggressive deficits), and structured resistance training performed with adequate volume.** Protein timing, periodization model selection, and the choice between HIIT and MICT are secondary variables that matter less than total daily protein, total training volume, and the magnitude of the energy deficit [1][2][3][4].

## 2. Protein Intake Targets for Body Recomposition

### 2.1 The Dose-Response Evidence Base

The foundational meta-analysis by Morton et al. (2018), published in the *British Journal of Sports Medicine*, pooled 49 randomized controlled trials with 1,863 participants (mean age 35 ± 20 years) and remains the most cited evidence for protein dose-response during resistance training [1][2][3][4]. Key findings:

- Protein supplementation significantly increased fat-free mass (FFM) by **0.30 kg (95% CI: 0.09–0.52, p=0.007)** and one-repetition-maximum (1RM) strength by **2.49 kg (95% CI: 0.64–4.33, p=0.01)** [1][2][3].
- Biphasic regression of 42 study arms (723 participants) revealed a **plateau in resistance-training-induced FFM gains at 1.62 g protein/kg/day (95% CI: 1.03–2.20)**; intakes beyond this threshold provided no further benefit [1][2][3][4].
- Protein supplementation was more effective in resistance-trained individuals (+0.75 kg FFM, 95% CI 0.09–1.40, p=0.03) versus untrained individuals, and less effective with increasing age (−0.01 kg per year, p=0.002); young adults (<45 years) gained 0.55 kg FFM versus only 0.06 kg in older adults [2][3][16].
- The US/Canadian RDA of 0.8 g/kg/day is insufficient for those seeking to gain strength and muscle mass with resistance training [2][16].

These findings are corroborated by more recent meta-analyses. **Nunes et al. (2022)**, analyzing 74 RCTs (*Journal of Cachexia, Sarcopenia and Muscle*), found that increasing daily protein ingestion enhanced lean body mass gains in adults performing resistance exercise (SMD = 0.22, 95% CI 0.14–0.30, p<0.01, moderate evidence)—approximately 1.3–1.4 kg lean mass gain in intervention groups versus ~0.8 kg in controls. In younger adults (<65 years), the effect was significant only at intakes **≥1.6 g/kg/day** [5][6]. **Tagawa et al. (2022)** conducted a dose-response meta-analysis of 82 articles (112 intervention groups, 3,940 participants) and found that muscle strength with resistance training increased by 0.72% per 0.1 g/kg/day increase in total protein intake, **up to 1.5 g/kg/day, with no further gains beyond that threshold** [7].

### 2.2 Protein Intake During Caloric Restriction

The evidence consistently shows that protein requirements increase during energy deficit. The foundational study by **Mettler, Mitchell & Tipton (2010)** randomized 20 young, healthy, resistance-trained athletes to a 40% energy deficit for 2 weeks with either ~1.0 g/kg/day (15% protein) or ~2.3 g/kg/day (35% protein) [11][12][13]:

- Total mass loss was significantly larger in the control group (−3.0 ± 0.4 kg) versus the high-protein group (−1.5 ± 0.3 kg, p=0.036) [12].
- **Lean body mass loss was significantly larger in the control group (−1.6 ± 0.3 kg) versus the high-protein group (−0.3 ± 0.3 kg, p=0.006)** [12][13].
- Fat loss was similar between groups, demonstrating that higher protein selectively preserved lean tissue without impairing fat loss [12].

**Longland et al. (2016)**, published in the *American Journal of Clinical Nutrition*, extended this finding to a true recomposition scenario. Twenty young men per group consumed a ~40% energy deficit for 4 weeks while performing resistance training plus HIIT 6 days/week, randomized to 1.2 g/kg/day or 2.4 g/kg/day protein [14]:

- **Lean body mass increased in the high-protein group (+1.2 ± 1.0 kg, p<0.05) and was significantly greater than the lower-protein group (+0.1 ± 1.0 kg, p<0.05)** [14].
- **The high-protein group lost more fat mass (−4.8 ± 1.6 kg) than the lower-protein group (−3.5 ± 1.4 kg, p<0.05)** [14].
- This study demonstrates that during a marked energy deficit, 2.4 g/kg/day protein combined with high-volume training can produce simultaneous lean mass gain and fat mass loss [14].

**Refalo, Trexler & Helms (2025)**, the most recent systematic review with Bayesian meta-regression (*Strength & Conditioning Journal*), analyzed 29 studies in energy-restricted, resistance-trained individuals [18][19][20]:

- There was a **>97% probability of a linear dose-response relationship between daily protein intake and favorable FFM changes** [18][19][20].
- Protein per kg of FFM (β = 0.06, 95% HDI: 0.01–0.12) showed a stronger relationship than protein per kg of body mass (β = 0.07, 95% HDI: −0.01 to 0.14) [19][20].
- The relationship was stronger in interventions longer than 4 weeks, in men, and when baseline body fat percentage was lower [18][19][20].
- **Practical recommendation: protein intake up to 1.9 g/kg of body mass per day, or 2.5 g/kg of FFM per day, is associated with less FFM loss during energy restriction. Intakes above these values, up to 3.2 g/kg of body mass or 4.2 g/kg of FFM, are linearly associated with larger FFM gain** [18].

The **Helms et al. (2014)** systematic review recommended **2.3–3.1 g/kg of fat-free mass** for energy-restricted, resistance-trained athletes, scaled with the severity of caloric restriction and leanness—corresponding to approximately **1.8–2.7 g/kg body mass per day** [11][17][18][21]. The **Hector & Phillips (2018)** review in the *International Journal of Sports Nutrition and Exercise Metabolism* summarized the field with the practical recommendation of **1.6–2.4 g protein/kg/day during weight loss in athletes**, with the severity of caloric deficit and training type determining where in this range athletes should target [17].

### 2.3 Very High Protein Intakes (2.4–4.4 g/kg/day)

Research on very high protein intakes suggests diminishing returns for muscle gain but potential benefits for fat loss. **Antonio et al. (2015)** randomized 73 healthy resistance-trained subjects to ~2.3 g/kg/day or 3.4 g/kg/day for 8 weeks of heavy periodized resistance training [22]:

- **Fat mass change: −0.3 kg (normal protein) versus −1.7 kg (high protein)**; % body fat change: −0.7% versus −2.4%—significant time-by-group effects favored the high-protein group [22].
- Both groups gained ~1.5 kg FFM (no significant between-group difference) [22].
- The high-protein group lost more fat despite consuming ~400 more kcal/day, potentially due to increased thermic effect of food and/or greater training compliance [22].
- **No deleterious effects on blood parameters were observed**, consistent with the broader literature that high protein intakes are safe in healthy, exercising individuals [22][24].

The authors' earlier 2014 study comparing 1.8 g/kg versus 4.4 g/kg found no body composition differences without training alterations [23][24]. The 2020 narrative review by Antonio, Candow et al. in *Nutrients* concluded: "For individuals who exercise regularly, the current body of evidence supports dietary protein needs 1.5- to 2.0-fold higher than the RDA of 0.8 g/kg/d," with no deleterious effects reported in exercising individuals [24].

### 2.4 Protein Timing and Distribution

**The peri-workout "anabolic window" has been largely refuted as a critical factor.** The seminal meta-analysis by **Schoenfeld, Aragon & Krieger (2013)**, analyzing 23 studies, found that when controlling for total daily protein intake, **there were no significant differences between protein timing conditions for strength or hypertrophy** [10][35]. Total protein intake was the strongest predictor of hypertrophy effect size (estimate = 0.41 ± 0.14, p=0.004), with approximately a 0.2 increase in effect size for every 0.5 g/kg increase in protein ingestion. The authors concluded: "These results refute the commonly held belief that the timing of protein intake in and around a training session is critical to muscular adaptations" [10][35].

A 2025 systematic review with meta-analysis by **Casuso & Goossens** (*Nutrients*) directly compared protein supplementation before versus after training sessions (6 reports, 5 studies, 25–27.1 g protein doses, 6–16 weeks): **lean body mass was not affected by protein timing (SMD: −0.08; 95% CI: −0.398 to 0.244; p=0.641)** [37]. Similarly, **Lak et al. (2024)**, an 8-week RCT in 40 resistance-trained males (24 ± 4 years), found that consuming 50 g whey protein split around training (3 hours before/after versus immediately before/after) produced no significant between-group differences in skeletal muscle mass (+1.07 kg vs +1.18 kg, both p<0.0001) [35].

**Per-meal distribution** does appear to matter for maximizing the muscle protein synthesis (MPS) response. **Schoenfeld & Aragon (2018)** recommend: "To maximize anabolism one should consume protein at a target intake of **0.4 g/kg/meal across a minimum of four meals** in order to reach a minimum of 1.6 g/kg/day. Using the upper daily intake of 2.2 g/kg/day... would necessitate a maximum of 0.55 g/kg/meal" [42][43]. The per-meal MPS threshold in young adults is approximately 20–25 g of high-quality protein following leg-only resistance exercise, but up to 40 g following whole-body exercise (Macnaughton et al., 2016) [46].

**Pre-sleep protein ingestion** is a well-supported strategy, particularly during energy restriction. **Trommelen & Van Loon (2016)** and **Snijders et al. (2019)** reviews document that:

- **Pre-sleep ingestion of 40 g casein increases overnight MPS by approximately 22% versus placebo** [36][37].
- A dose of at least 40 g is required to elicit a robust increase in overnight MPS in young adults, whereas 30 g did not significantly increase overnight MPS [36].
- **Resistance exercise earlier that evening augments the overnight MPS response by 37%** and increases incorporation of pre-sleep protein-derived amino acids into myofibrillar protein by 76% [37].
- In a 12-week resistance training study in young men, ingesting **27.5 g protein prior to sleep resulted in greater gains in muscle mass and strength versus a non-caloric placebo** [36][37].
- **Trommelen et al. (2023)** demonstrated that pre-sleep protein (45 g casein or whey) also increases mitochondrial protein synthesis during overnight recovery from endurance exercise [40].

The **Hudson, Bergia & Campbell (2020)** review cautions that the evidence on optimal protein distribution is "limited and inconsistent," and the effect of distribution cannot be sufficiently disentangled from protein quantity [45]. For adults already consuming ≥1.6 g/kg/day, distribution across meals appears less critical than total intake [45][49].

### 2.5 Summary: Protein Recommendations

| Context | Recommended Intake | Key Evidence |
|---------|-------------------|--------------|
| Resistance training, energy balance | 1.6–2.2 g/kg/day | Morton 2018; Nunes 2022; Tagawa 2022 [1][5][7] |
| Caloric restriction (mild-moderate deficit) | 1.6–2.4 g/kg/day | Hector & Phillips 2018; Refalo 2025 [17][18] |
| Caloric restriction (aggressive deficit, lean athletes) | 2.3–3.1 g/kg FFM | Helms 2014; Witard 2019 [17][21] |
| Body recomposition in trained individuals | 2.6–3.5 g/kg FFM | Barakat 2020 [29] |
| Per-meal dose (young adults) | 0.4 g/kg/meal (up to 0.55 g/kg) | Schoenfeld & Aragon 2018 [42][43] |
| Pre-sleep (optional) | 27.5–40 g casein | Trommelen & Van Loon 2016; Snijders 2019 [36][37] |

## 3. Resistance Training Periodization for Lean Mass Preservation and Gain

### 3.1 Periodized Versus Non-Periodized Training

The meta-analytic evidence supports the superiority of periodized resistance training over non-periodized training for maximal strength, but not necessarily for hypertrophy. **Williams et al. (2017)**, a meta-analysis of 81 effect sizes from 18 studies and over 600 participants, found periodized training outperformed non-periodized approaches for maximal strength (effect size 0.43, p<0.001), with undulating models showing the strongest advantage for trained individuals [8]. **Moesgaard et al. (2022)**, a systematic review and meta-analysis in *Sports Medicine* specifically designed to address volume-equation issues, found that [2][3]:

- When training volume is equated, **periodized resistance training has a greater effect on 1RM strength compared to non-periodized training**.
- **Undulating periodization resulted in greater strength increases than linear periodization for trained individuals**, but this advantage was not observed in untrained individuals.
- **Periodization did not affect muscle hypertrophy when training volume was equated between conditions** [2][3].

This hypertrophy finding is important for body recomposition: it suggests that **the specific periodization model matters little for lean mass preservation during caloric restriction—what matters is total training volume and effort**. The "Stronger by Science" quantitative review (60 studies) similarly concluded that periodized training produces approximately 22% faster strength gains but does not meaningfully affect hypertrophy [7].

### 3.2 Linear Versus Undulating Periodization

**Harries, Lubans & Callister (2015)**, a systematic review and meta-analysis of 17 studies (510 participants), determined there were **no significant differences in effectiveness between linear and undulating periodization on upper-body or lower-body strength** [3][6]. However, several other analyses suggest undulating models may offer advantages for trained individuals:

- **Rhea et al. (2002)**, a 12-week study in recreationally trained men with identical total volumes, found the daily undulating periodization (DUP) group improved bench press 1RM by +28.8% versus +14.4% for linear, and leg press 1RM by +55.8% versus +25.7% (p<0.05)—nearly double the strength gain with equal workloads [8].
- **Stronger by Science** analysis found undulating periodization produced 24.75–27.44% strength gains versus 20.33–21.65% for linear (17% faster for undulating), with the largest difference among trained lifters (effect sizes 0.56–0.76, medium effects) [7].
- **Moesgaard et al. (2022)** found undulating models produce slightly superior 1RM improvements (3–5% higher) in trained athletes compared to linear models, with equivalent hypertrophy [8].

The most recent meta-analysis, **Liu & Bu et al. (2026)** (*Frontiers in Public Health*), analyzed 29 studies with 704 participants (ages 14–80) and found [5]:

- **No significant differences between linear and undulating periodization for upper limb strength (SMD 0.08), lower limb squat strength (SMD 0.08), sprint speed, or explosive power** [5].
- **Subgroup analysis showed undulating periodization was superior to linear periodization in increasing fat-free body weight in obese individuals (SMD 1.17, 95% CI 0.23–2.10)**, while linear periodization was more effective for long-term weight loss (>14 weeks; MD 8.99) and short-term BMI reduction (<8 weeks; MD −1.30) [5].
- Undulating periodization showed superior reduction in insulin resistance among men (SMD −3.04, 95% CI −4.44 to −1.64) [5].

### 3.3 Periodization During Caloric Restriction

Direct randomized controlled trials specifically testing periodization models under controlled hypocaloric conditions in recreationally active adults are scarce. However, several relevant lines of evidence inform this question:

First, since periodization does not affect hypertrophy when volume is equated (Moesgaard 2022) [2][3], the choice of periodization model during a cut should prioritize **maintenance of training volume** over the specific progression scheme. **Roth, Schoenfeld & Behringer (2022)**, a systematic review of 15 studies (129 resistance-trained participants, average training experience 6.02 years, mean age 25.9 years), examined lean mass sparing during caloric restriction [31]:

- On average, **24% of dietary-only and 11% of diet × exercise weight loss was lean tissue** [31].
- **Studies with high resistance training volume (≥10 weekly sets per muscle group) showed low-to-no lean mass loss**, mostly in female subjects; studies increasing RT volume during caloric restriction showed no-to-low lean mass loss versus studies reducing volume [31].
- On average, females gained 0.2 kg lean tissue during dieting, while males lost −2.81 kg (28.7% of total body mass loss); 5 of 7 male studies reported lean tissue loss versus only 2 of 7 female studies [31].
- The evidence is insufficient to definitively conclude higher RT volume spares lean mass during caloric restriction, but **data favor higher volumes, particularly in female athletes**, and increasing RT volume over time may be more effective [31].

Second, **Binmahfoz et al. (2025)**, a systematic review and meta-analysis of 25 RCTs (1,608 participants) examining resistance exercise during dietary weight loss, found that resistance exercise significantly protected against fat-free mass loss (SMD: 0.40, p=0.0003, moderate certainty), with the effect significant for short-duration (≤5 months; SMD: 0.52, p=0.0001) but not long-duration studies (SMD: 0.20, p=0.17) [9]. Most studies used supervised resistance training (typically 3x/week, 30–60 min, 8–10 exercises) [9].

Third, the 2026 network meta-analysis by **Xie et al.** (62 RCTs, 4,429 participants) ranked exercise modalities during caloric restriction for lean body mass preservation: **moderate-intensity mixed exercise and all resistance training intensities were most effective**; moderate-intensity mixed exercise combined with caloric restriction was best overall for lean mass preservation [15][53].

### 3.4 Advanced Training Techniques

**Tsartsapakis et al. (2026)**, a systematic review and meta-analysis of advanced resistance training systems (rest-pause, drop sets, cluster sets, tempo-controlled lifting, velocity-based training, eccentric overload) in recreationally trained adults aged 18–45 (24 studies, 20 in meta-analysis, 5–12 weeks duration), found [4]:

- Overall pooled advantage for advanced systems was small but significant (g = 0.159); strength outcomes showed moderate benefit (g = 0.351).
- **Hypertrophy effects were small and non-significant (g = 0.046)**—advanced techniques do not provide a hypertrophic advantage over traditional multiple-set training when volume and effort are matched [4].

### 3.5 Practical Implications for Body Recomposition

For a recreationally active adult in a caloric deficit aiming to preserve or gain lean mass, the evidence supports:

1. **Prioritize total training volume (≥10 weekly sets per muscle group)** over the specific periodization model [31].
2. **Use either linear or undulating periodization**—both are effective; undulating models may offer slight strength advantages in trained individuals [2][3][8].
3. **Consider increasing training volume during the cut** rather than reducing it, where recovery permits [31].
4. **Maintain heavy loading** (e.g., 70–85% 1RM) alongside higher-rep work, as maximal strength benefits from heavy loads while hypertrophy can be achieved across loading ranges [1][2].

## 4. HIIT Versus MICT for Fat Loss, Lean Mass Preservation, and Adherence

### 4.1 Fat Loss Outcomes

The meta-analytic evidence on HIIT versus MICT for fat loss is substantial but characterized by important methodological caveats and some conflicting findings.

**Keating et al. (2017)**, published in *Obesity Reviews*, concluded that **"HIIT/SIT appears to provide similar benefits to MICT for body fat reduction, although not necessarily in a more time-efficient manner"**—and notably, neither short-term HIIT/SIT nor MICT produced clinically meaningful reductions in body fat in many studies [17].

**Wewege et al. (2017)**, also in *Obesity Reviews*, analyzed 13 trials comparing HIIT and MICT in overweight and obese adults (averaging 10 weeks × 3 sessions/week). Both modalities elicited significant reductions in whole-body fat mass and waist circumference with **no significant differences between HIIT and MICT for any body composition measure—but HIIT required ~40% less training time** [16]. Notably, running training displayed large effects on fat mass for both HIIT and MICT, while cycling training did not induce fat loss [16].

**Guo et al. (2023)**, a meta-analysis of 29 RCTs (807 participants, mean age 33.8 ± 11.6 years) specifically in young and middle-aged adults (18–60 years), found [14]:

- Both HIIT and MICT significantly improved body composition and cardiorespiratory fitness (except fat-free mass).
- **HIIT was significantly superior to MICT for waist circumference (MD = −0.96 cm), percent fat mass (MD = −0.48%), and VO2peak (SMD = 0.19)** [14].
- **HIIT's advantages were significant in younger adults (18–45 years) but not middle-aged adults (45–60 years)** [14].
- HIIT was more effective for obese participants in reducing waist circumference and percent fat mass; HIIT >3 times/week and >6 weeks produced greater benefits [14].
- HIIT was more time-saving (9–54 min versus 15–60 min per session) and enjoyable [14].

**Deng et al. (2026)**, a Bayesian network meta-analysis of 18 RCTs (952 participants, 8–16 weeks), stratified by weekly volume: **HIIT ≥75 min/week was associated with significant reductions in body fat percentage (MD=−3.05, 95% CrI: −4.69 to −1.42), waist-to-hip ratio (MD=−0.05), and waist circumference (MD=−4.82 cm) versus control**, and was **superior to MICT ≥150 min/week for body fat percentage (MD=−0.89, 95% CrI: −1.71 to −0.07) and waist circumference (MD=−1.98, 95% CrI: −3.31 to −0.66)** [10].

**Khodadadi et al. (2023)**, a meta-analysis of 36 RCTs (1,130 participants, ages 18–57) examining HIIT modality effects, found [13]:

- **HIIT significantly reduced body fat percentage (WMD: −1.53%, 95% CI: −2.13 to −0.92)** and fat mass (WMD: −1.86 kg, 95% CI: −2.55 to −1.18) [13].
- **HIIT improved fat-free mass overall (WMD: 0.51 kg, 95% CI: 0.06 to 0.95, p=0.025)**, with cycling-based HIIT significantly increasing FFM (WMD: 0.63 kg, 95% CI: 0.17 to 1.09, p=0.007) [13].
- Overground running produced the largest body fat percentage reduction (WMD: −2.80%) and fat mass reduction (WMD: −4.25 kg); treadmill running did not significantly reduce fat mass [13].
- Training >8 weeks, ≥3 sessions/week, work intervals ≤60 s separated by ≤90 s active recovery were most effective [13].

### 4.2 The Retracted Viana (2019) Analysis

A note on evidence quality: the widely cited **Viana et al. (2019)** paper in the *British Journal of Sports Medicine*, which claimed interval training provided 28.5% greater reductions in total absolute fat mass than MICT, **was retracted on 15 December 2020** [22][27]. Researchers and practitioners should avoid citing the retracted findings. The remaining literature suggests HIIT is modestly superior or equivalent to MICT for fat loss depending on population, modality, and outcome measure.

### 4.3 Lean Mass Preservation

A common concern is whether HIIT/SIT impairs lean mass gains or preservation. The retrieved evidence does not support this concern:

- **Khodadadi et al. (2023)**: HIIT improved FFM overall (WMD: 0.51 kg, p=0.025, I²=0%), though this was sensitive to a single study [13].
- **Guo et al. (2023)**: No significant differences between HIIT and MICT for fat-free mass [14].
- **Scoubeau et al. (2022)**, a meta-analysis of whole-body HIIT (22 studies, 657 participants), found compared to no exercise, whole-body HIIT improved fat-free mass (SMD: 0.38, 95% CI: 0.11–0.65, p<0.001) and fat mass (SMD: 0.40, 95% CI: 0.09–0.72, p<0.001), with similar effects to other aerobic training but larger effects on musculoskeletal fitness (SMD: 0.42, p=0.003) [20].
- **Themistocleous et al. (2024)** systematic review (26 RCTs, 986 participants) of short-burst (≤30s) HIIT/SIT found inconclusive evidence for body composition improvements in overweight/obese adults, with mixed outcomes across studies—but no evidence of lean mass impairment [21].

For the specific population of recreationally active adults aged 18–35, the evidence indicates that **HIIT can be incorporated alongside resistance training without compromising lean mass**, and may modestly contribute to FFM preservation during energy deficit. However, **resistance training remains the more potent stimulus for lean mass accrual and preservation** [9][15].

### 4.4 Adherence and Enjoyment

Adherence is a critical determinant of long-term outcomes. The evidence favors HIIT on enjoyment metrics:

- **Thum et al. (2017)** (PLOS ONE): Twelve recreationally active adults (age 29.5 ± 10.7 yr) reported **significantly higher enjoyment (p=0.013, d=1.54) for HIIT (103.8 ± 9.4) versus MICT (84.2 ± 19.1)**; 11 of 12 participants (92%) preferred HIIT [23].
- **Guo et al. (2023)**: Pooled attendance was 90.24%; HIIT was described as more time-saving and enjoyable [14].
- **Stefanovic et al. (2026)**: Real-world HIIT studies (31 studies, 1,119 participants, mean age 34) reported a mean dropout rate of 14.9% [18].
- **Cassidy et al. (2017)** (Diabetologia review): HIIT was well-tolerated and often preferred over MICT by patients; enjoyment decreases with longer intervals (30–60 second intervals preferred over 120 seconds) [19].
- Real-world adherence statistics show that 63% of new gym members quit before the third month, and fewer than 4% still train continuously after 12 months; supervised HIIT dropout is ~13%, and supervision improves adherence (~72–81% during supervision falling to ~43–67% once unsupervised) [24].

### 4.5 Metabolic Adaptations and Cardiorespiratory Fitness

HIIT demonstrates consistent advantages for cardiorespiratory fitness:

- **Milanović, Sporiš & Weston (2015)**, meta-analysis of 28 controlled trials (723 participants, mean age 25.1 ± 5 years): HIT produced a likely large beneficial effect on VO2max (+5.5 mL/kg/min, 95% CI ±1.2) versus controls, with a possibly small beneficial effect over endurance training (+1.2 mL/kg/min, 95% CI ±0.9) [30].
- **Wen et al. (2019)**: To maximize VO2max improvements, **long-interval (≥2 min), high-volume (≤15 min) and moderate-to-long-term (4–12 weeks) HIIT** are recommended [28].
- **Atakan et al. (2022)** (BJSM): HIIT/SIT significantly increased fat oxidation during exercise (MD = 0.08 g/min, 95% CI 0.04–0.12, p<0.001), with a small advantage over MICT (MD = 0.03 g/min, p=0.005), and larger effects among individuals with overweight/obesity [31].
- **Cassidy et al. (2017)**: HIIT improves VO2peak by ~19.4% versus 10.3% for MICT in cardiometabolic risk patients; a single HIIT session reduces same-day postprandial glucose AUC by ~35%; HIIT increases GLUT-4 content by up to 369% after six sessions [19].
- **Stefanovic et al. (2026)**: Real-world HIIT increased VO2max/peak by 3.9 mL/kg/min (p<0.001) and was superior to active controls (e.g., MICT) for VO2max/peak (1.1 mL/kg/min) [18].

### 4.6 Summary: HIIT vs. MICT

| Outcome | HIIT | MICT | Evidence Strength |
|---------|------|------|-------------------|
| Body fat percentage | Modest reduction; superior in some meta-analyses (younger adults) | Modest reduction | Moderate |
| Absolute fat mass | Reductions 1.6–2.3 kg typical; modality-dependent | Reductions 1.1–1.4 kg typical | Moderate |
| Lean mass | Preserves or modestly increases FFM; not a substitute for RT | Preserves FFM | Moderate |
| VO2max | Consistently superior | Good | High |
| Time efficiency | 40% less time for similar results | — | High |
| Enjoyment/adherence | Higher enjoyment; similar or lower dropout | Lower enjoyment in some studies | Moderate |

## 5. Caloric Deficit Magnitude: Efficacy and Safety

### 5.1 The Composition of Weight Loss

Understanding how weight loss is composed is foundational to choosing a deficit magnitude. The classic "Quarter FFM Rule" (Heymsfield et al., 2014) states that approximately one-fourth of weight loss is fat-free mass during dieting, but this fraction varies substantially by baseline adiposity, age, diet composition, and exercise status [1]. The Forbes curve demonstrates that **the fraction of weight lost as FFM is larger when baseline fat mass is smaller**—meaning leaner individuals are at greater risk of losing lean tissue during aggressive deficits [1].

**Anyiam et al. (2024)**, a systematic review and meta-analysis of 49 studies (4,785 participants) on caloric restriction up to 900 kcal/day, found that **skeletal muscle constituted approximately 25.5% of total weight loss in individuals with type 2 diabetes and 27.5% in non-diabetic individuals** [3]. **McCarthy & Berg (2021)** report typical FFM losses of 20–40% of total weight lost, with FFM losses ranging from 1.0–1.5 kg and skeletal muscle mass losses around 3.2% (0.9–1.7 kg) [12].

### 5.2 The Energy Deficit Threshold for Lean Mass Gains

The most directly relevant study for prescribing deficit magnitude is **Murphy & Koehler (2022)**, a meta-analysis and meta-regression published in the *Scandinavian Journal of Medicine & Science in Sports* [13]:

- Analysis A (7 RCTs, 282 participants): Energy deficit significantly impaired lean mass gains (ES = −0.58, p=0.02) but did not significantly affect strength (ES = −0.31, p=0.28) [13].
- Analysis B (52 matched studies, 1,213 participants): Resistance training with energy deficit had a negative overall effect on lean mass (ES = −0.11, p=0.03), while resistance training in energy balance had a positive effect (ES = 0.20, p<0.001); both groups showed large, positive strength gains (ES ≈ 0.82) [13].
- **Meta-regression: An energy deficit of ~500 kcal/day completely ablated lean mass gains (ES = 0 at that deficit level). Each additional 1000 kcal/day deficit reduced the effect size by 0.31** [13].
- Conclusion: "Individuals looking to gain LM from RT should avoid prolonged energy deficits while individuals trying to lose weight should practice RT and maintain an energy deficit ≤500 kcal day⁻¹ to maintain LM" [13].

This finding aligns with the practical recommendation from **Refalo et al. (2025)** that people seeking to gain FFM should avoid deficits >500 kcal/day, and recommended weight loss rates of ≤0.5% of body mass per week are preferable [18].

### 5.3 Comparing Deficit Magnitudes

**Mild deficits (~10–15% below maintenance, ~300–400 kcal/day):**

- A 10-week RCT by **Vargas-Molina et al. (2026)** (*European Journal of Applied Physiology*) randomized 30 resistance-trained participants (23.0 ± 3.4 years) to an isocaloric diet, a −250 kcal/day deficit diet (both at 2.5 g/kg/day protein), or control [32]:
  - The deficit group reduced fat mass (Δ = −2.94 kg, p<0.001); the isocaloric group showed smaller non-significant decreases (Δ = −1.41 kg, p=0.051) [32].
  - **FFM increased in both the isocaloric group (Δ = 0.97 kg, p<0.001) and the deficit group (Δ = 1.04 kg, p=0.009)**; control showed no meaningful changes [32].
  - Conclusion: "Both a moderate energy deficit and a maintenance-calorie high-protein diet can elicit body recomposition when compared to habitual practice, suggesting that elevated protein intake (2.5 g·kg⁻¹·d⁻¹) may facilitate simultaneous improvements in fat mass and FFM" [32].

**Moderate deficits (~20–25%, ~500–750 kcal/day):**

- This range is the most commonly recommended and best supported. **Carbone & Pasiakos (2019)** note that during moderate energy deficits (500–750 kcal/day), protein intakes of ~1.6 g/kg/day preserve muscle mass, but this protective effect is compromised when energy deficit exceeds 40% of daily needs [51].
- **Lahav, Yavetz & Gepner (2026)**, a retrospective cohort study of 304 adults (aged 20–74) following a structured weight-loss program (~500 kcal/day deficit, 1.5 g/kg/day protein, ~5 months), found that **resistance training was the only exercise modality associated with FFM increase** (men: +0.8 kg; women: +0.90 kg), while diet-only groups lost FFM (men: −2.8 kg; women: −2.94 kg). The fat-mass-to-weight-loss ratio (quality of weight loss) was 1.1 for resistance training versus 0.7 for diet-only (p=0.0002) [10].

**Aggressive deficits (>25%, >750 kcal/day, or VLCD <800–1200 kcal/day):**

- **Longland et al. (2016)** demonstrated that body recomposition is possible even at a ~40% deficit when protein is elevated to 2.4 g/kg/day and high-volume training is performed—but this requires careful programming [14].
- **Janssen, Van Every & Phillips (2023)** note that the extent of muscle loss on VLCDs depends on baseline body composition, magnitude and duration of the deficit, protein intake, resistance exercise status, and other lifestyle factors. Individuals with higher body fat can tolerate larger deficits with lower atrophy risk; trained individuals with low body fat are at greater risk [16].
- **Weiss et al. (2017)** (52 overweight adults, 45–65 years) found that ~7% weight loss via 20% caloric restriction decreased whole-body lean mass ~2% and lower extremity lean mass ~4%; exercise protected against these effects [2].
- **Anyiam et al. (2024)** found a paradoxical result: less restrictive caloric restriction (400–600 kcal/day) produced smaller fat mass and body weight reductions than more restrictive interventions (600–900 kcal/day), likely due to improved compliance and sustainability [3].
- **Huovinen et al.** (cited in Janssen 2023) found that athletes with body fat below 10% could not preserve fat-free mass even with 2 g/kg protein during severe energy restriction [16].
- Postmenopausal women on severe energy restriction (65–75% deficit) showed 2.5-fold greater loss in hip bone mineral density than those on moderate deficits (25–35%) [16].

### 5.4 Resistance Training as the Primary Countermeasure

Regardless of deficit magnitude, resistance training is the most potent nonpharmacological stimulus for lean mass preservation [9][15][16][51]. The **Binmahfoz et al. (2025)** meta-analysis (25 RCTs, 1,608 participants) found that adding resistance exercise to dietary weight loss:

- Produced significantly greater fat mass reduction (SMD: −0.36, p<0.00001, high certainty) [9].
- Significantly protected against fat-free mass loss (SMD: 0.40, p=0.0003, moderate certainty) [9].
- Significantly increased muscle strength (SMD: 2.36, p=0.00001, low certainty) [9].

The **Xie et al. (2025)** network meta-analysis (62 RCTs, 4,429 participants, 10–25% caloric restriction) found that for lean body mass preservation, moderate-intensity mixed exercise combined with caloric restriction was best, followed by all resistance training intensities [15][53].

### 5.5 Summary: Caloric Deficit Magnitude

| Deficit Magnitude | Approximate kcal/day | Lean Mass Effects | Key Considerations |
|-------------------|---------------------|-------------------|---------------------|
| Mild (10–15%) | ~300–400 | Recomposition possible with adequate protein | Slow progress; best adherence; sustainable [32] |
| Moderate (20–25%) | ~500–750 | LM gains ablated at ~500 kcal/day; preservation requires RT + high protein | The evidence-supported sweet spot [13][51] |
| Aggressive (>25%) | >750 or VLCD | High risk of LM loss, especially in lean individuals; recomposition possible only with 2.4+ g/kg protein + RT | Requires careful programming; medical monitoring for VLCD [14][16] |

## 6. Meal-Timing Protocols: Intermittent Fasting, Time-Restricted Feeding, and Meal Frequency

### 6.1 Intermittent Fasting Versus Continuous Energy Restriction

The head-to-head evidence comparing intermittent energy restriction (IER) with continuous energy restriction (CER) in the context of body recomposition is now substantial.

**Cioffi et al. (2018)**, a systematic review and meta-analysis of 11 RCTs (630 participants, 8–24 weeks), found weight loss was comparable between IER and CER (WMD: −0.61 kg; 95% CI −1.70 to 0.47; p=0.27), with no significant between-arm differences in fat mass, fat-free mass, waist circumference, or most metabolic markers [26]. Fasting insulin was significantly reduced with IER (WMD=−0.89 µU/mL, p=0.009) [26].

**Schroor et al. (2024)**, a systematic review and meta-analysis of 28 trials comparing three IER protocols (time-restricted eating, alternate-day fasting, and 5:2 diet) with CER, found [29]:

- Changes in body weight (WMD: −0.42 kg, p=0.132) and fat mass (WMD: −0.31 kg, p=0.362) were comparable between IER and CER.
- **IER diets combined reduced fat-free mass (WMD: −0.20 kg; 95% CI: −0.39 to −0.01; p=0.044) and waist circumference (WMD: −0.91 cm; 95% CI: −1.76 to −0.06; p=0.036) more than CER** [29].
- Subgroup analysis showed time-restricted eating reduced body weight, fat mass, and fat-free mass more than CER [29].

**Templeman et al. (2021)** (*Science Translational Medicine*) conducted a highly controlled 3-week RCT in healthy lean adults (n=12 per group) isolating the effects of fasting from energy restriction [28]:

- Daily energy restriction (75:75 group) reduced body mass by −1.91 ± 0.99 kg, almost entirely from fat loss (−1.75 ± 0.79 kg).
- **Alternate-day fasting with matched energy restriction (0:150 group) reduced body fat significantly less (−0.74 ± 1.32 kg, p=0.01 versus daily restriction)** [28].
- Fasting without energy restriction (0:200) did not significantly reduce body mass or fat mass [28].
- **Conclusion: Alternate-day fasting less effectively reduces body fat than a matched degree of daily energy restriction, with no evidence of fasting-specific metabolic benefits in lean individuals** [28].

**Huang et al. (2024)**, a network meta-analysis of 47 RCTs (3,363 participants), found alternate-day fasting produced the most significant body weight loss (MD: −3.42 kg), followed by time-restricted eating (MD: −2.25 kg), short-term fasting (MD: −1.87 kg), and continuous energy restriction (MD: −1.59 kg) [20]. However, **short-term fasting led to a decline in lean mass (MD: −1.26; 95% CI: −2.16 to −0.47)**, and by 4–6 months, varying degrees of weight regain occurred, particularly with CER; interventions lasting 7–12 months achieved greater weight loss overall, with time-restricted eating potentially ranking first [20].

### 6.2 Time-Restricted Eating and Lean Mass

The effect of time-restricted eating (TRE) on lean mass is a critical question for body recomposition. Recent meta-analyses report somewhat conflicting results:

- **Sun et al. (2025)**, meta-analysis of 20 RCTs (1,242 participants): TRE decreased fat-free mass by **0.58 kg** (95% CI: −0.81 to −0.36) alone and 0.56 kg combined with energy restriction; **combining TRE with energy restriction provided no additional beneficial effects on weight loss** [23].
- **Chen et al. (2026)** (*BMJ Medicine*), network meta-analysis of 41 RCTs (2,287 participants): Overall TRE significantly improved body weight (−2.15 kg), fat mass (−1.32 kg), and waist circumference (−1.63 cm) **but also reduced fat-free mass/lean mass (−0.84 kg)** [25].
- **Xing et al. (2026)**, age-specific meta-analysis of 28 RCTs (1,833 participants): **Significant fat-free mass (lean muscle) loss occurred in both young adults (MD = −0.98 kg, p=0.020) and middle-aged/older adults (MD = −0.98 kg, p<0.001)**; LDL cholesterol also significantly increased in young adults (MD = 6.75 mg/dL) [30].
- **Chen et al. (2025)**, meta-analysis of 13 RCTs (612 participants) specifically in overweight/obese women: TRE significantly reduced body weight (WMD = −1.93 kg) and fasting insulin, **but found no significant effects on fat mass, fat-free mass, lean mass, or visceral fat mass—and no lean mass loss was observed** [22].
- **Xie et al. (2024)**, meta-analysis of 20 studies (1,288 participants) in overweight/obese adults: TRE significantly reduced body fat percentage, fat mass, lean mass, body mass, BMI, and waist circumference, but increased LDL cholesterol (MD = 2.70 mg/dL, p=0.037); an eating window of 6–8 hours appeared most appropriate for fat loss [24].

The discrepancy between studies showing FFM loss and those not may relate to population (overweight/obese versus lean), sex, protein intake adequacy, and whether energy intake is matched. **For lean, recreationally active adults seeking recomposition, the FFM loss signal (0.58–0.98 kg) warrants caution**—particularly given that TRE may reduce the number of opportunities to consume adequate protein (and the per-meal MPS stimulus) within the eating window [23][25][30].

**Early versus late TRE**: The Chen et al. (2026) network meta-analysis found **early TRE (last meal before 17:00) was superior to late TRE, significantly reducing body weight (−1.15 kg, high certainty) and fasting insulin (−3.32 µIU/mL, high certainty)** [25].

### 6.3 Intermittent Fasting Combined with Resistance Training

The most relevant study for body recomposition is a 12-week RCT (*European Journal of Nutrition*, 2022) comparing 5:2 intermittent fasting (IFT) with continuous energy restriction (CERT), both combined with resistance training (2 supervised + 1 unsupervised sessions/week, ≥1.4 g/kg/day protein) in 34 untrained participants (ages 18–45) [27]:

- **LBM significantly increased (+3.7%, p<0.001), body weight decreased (−4.6%, p<0.001), and fat decreased (−24.1%, p<0.001) with no significant difference between groups** [27].
- Sex differences: greater weight and BMI reductions in males, but greater LBM increases in females [27].
- The hypothesis that 5:2 IF would result in greater LBM maintenance or accrual was **not supported**; if anything, the continuous restriction group showed a significant increase in muscle surface area compared to the IF group [27].

### 6.4 Meal Frequency

The evidence on meal frequency (grazing versus 3 meals) does not support a meaningful effect on body composition when total intake is controlled.

**Blazey et al. (2023)**, a systematic review with meta-analysis of 16 trials (*International Journal of Behavioral Nutrition and Physical Activity*), found **very low certainty evidence of no difference between high- and low-frequency eating for weight change (MD: −0.62 kg, 95% CI: −2.76 to 1.52 kg, p=0.57, I²=63%)**, with similar null findings for BMI, fat mass, blood glucose, insulin, and lipids [31]. The authors concluded: "We cannot advocate for either restricted- or unrestricted eating frequency to change markers of cardiometabolic health in healthy young to middle-aged adults" [31].

**Canuto et al. (2017)**, a systematic review of 31 observational studies, found insufficient evidence confirming an association between eating frequency and body weight or body composition when misreporting bias was taken into account; however, in men, a potential protective effect of high eating frequency was observed on BMI and visceral obesity [32]. Observational data from the SONAR-Brazil survey (2,050 adults, mean age 34) found that consuming more than 3 meals/day was associated with lower BMI (−0.14 kg/m²) and 32% lower odds of obesity, while having the largest meal earlier in the day was protective [34].

### 6.5 Peri-Workout Nutrition

The peri-workout nutrition evidence is summarized in Section 2.4: protein timing around training is not critical for lean mass outcomes when total daily protein is adequate [10][35][37]. Pre-sleep protein (27.5–40 g casein) is the one timing strategy with consistent evidence for enhancing overnight MPS and supporting training adaptations [36][37].

## 7. Maintenance Strategies for Sustaining Body Composition Outcomes

### 7.1 Structured Diet Breaks and Refeeds

Emerging evidence suggests that planned diet breaks may improve fat-free mass retention during weight loss. **Johnson et al. (2026)**, a 12-week RCT in 30 adult females (ages 18–65, BMI 30–45), compared continuous caloric restriction (CCR, 25% deficit) with an intermittent approach incorporating structured diet refeeds and planned diet breaks (DRF), both at 1.2 g/kg/day protein [19]:

- Body fat percentage decreased 6.7 ± 2.1% (CCR) and 6.0 ± 1.9% (DRF), with no significant group × time interaction [19].
- **Fat-free mass increased in both groups, but the DRF group showed a larger increase (+2.40 ± 1.32 kg versus +1.22 ± 1.09 kg); the interaction was not significant (p=0.08) but the effect size was moderate (Cohen's d = 0.98), favoring diet breaks** [19].
- The DRF group had significantly greater dietary adherence (91.36% versus 88.43%) and a smaller actual energy deficit (−690 vs −1005 kcal/day), suggesting diet breaks may improve sustainability [19].

### 7.2 Preventing Weight Regain

The network meta-analysis by **Huang et al. (2024)** found that by 4–6 months, varying degrees of weight regain occur with all caloric restriction regimens, particularly with continuous energy restriction; interventions lasting 7–12 months achieved greater weight loss overall, with time-restricted eating potentially ranking first during the 4–6 and 7–12 month periods [20]. This suggests that **cycling between dietary approaches or using time-restricted eating as a maintenance tool** may help sustain outcomes.

### 7.3 Ongoing Training Prescription

Maintenance of resistance training volume is critical. The **Roth, Schoenfeld & Behringer (2022)** review found that studies increasing RT volume during caloric restriction showed no-to-low lean mass loss versus studies reducing volume [31]. The **Murphy & Koehler (2022)** meta-analysis emphasizes that resistance training in energy balance produces positive lean mass gains (ES = 0.20, p<0.001), suggesting that after the cut, transitioning to energy balance or a slight surplus while maintaining RT allows for lean mass recovery [13].

### 7.4 Behavioral Adherence Strategies

The evidence on exercise adherence provides important context for maintenance:

- **63% of new gym members quit before the third month; fewer than 4% still train continuously after 12 months** [24].
- Habit formation takes a median of 66 days (range 18–254 days); **missing a single day does not meaningfully derail habit formation** [24].
- **Supervision improves adherence**: ~72–81% adherence during supervision, falling to ~43–67% once unsupervised; supervised HIIT dropout is ~13% [24].
- **Extrinsic goals (especially weight loss) predict dropout**; people driven by weight-loss motivation had higher dropout risk than those training for enjoyment or health [24].
- Gamification produces a moderate, significant increase in physical activity (Hedges' g = 0.42) across 16 RCTs with 2,407 participants [24].

### 7.5 Protein Intake Maintenance Levels

The evidence suggests that protein intakes of **1.6–2.2 g/kg/day** are appropriate for maintaining lean mass during the maintenance phase, consistent with the Morton (2018) plateau of 1.62 g/kg/day (upper 95% CI 2.2 g/kg/day) [1][2][3]. The **Xing et al. (2026)** review recommends a combined "IF+" strategy—adequate protein and resistance training—for those using intermittent fasting long-term, particularly to mitigate lean mass loss [30].

### 7.6 Reverse Dieting

The retrieved literature did not yield direct randomized controlled trials on reverse dieting (gradual increase in caloric intake post-diet). The closest evidence comes from the structured refeed/diet break literature (Section 7.1) [19], and from the principle established by Murphy & Koehler (2022) that transitioning from deficit to energy balance restores the anabolic response to resistance training [13]. Practitioners commonly recommend a gradual increase of ~50–100 kcal/day per week post-diet, prioritizing protein intake, but this specific protocol lacks direct RCT evidence in the 2015–2026 window.

## 8. Consensus Guidelines from Major Organizations (2015–2026)

The following consensus positions and guidelines were identified in the retrieved literature:

### 8.1 Protein Intake Recommendations

- **Academy of Nutrition and Dietetics, Dietitians of Canada, and ACSM**: Recommend **1.2–2.0 g/kg/day** for physically active individuals (as cited in Carbone & Pasiakos 2019) [51].
- **International Society of Sports Nutrition (ISSN)**: Recommends **1.4–2.0 g/kg/day** for most exercising individuals (Jäger et al., 2017, as cited in ExRx.net and Carbone & Pasiakos 2019) [50][51]. The ISSN defines "high protein" as anything over **2.2 g/kg/day** [33].
- **WHO/FAO/EFSA**: The RDA for protein is **0.83 g/kg/day** (WHO) and 0.8 g/kg/day (US/Canada); the evidence reviewed here consistently indicates this is insufficient for active individuals seeking to gain or preserve lean mass [5][51].
- **Morton et al. (2018)**: The US/Canadian RDA of 0.8 g/kg/day appears insufficient for those seeking to gain strength and muscle mass with resistance training; ~1.6 g/kg/day is the recommended target, with ~2.2 g/kg/day as a defensible upper bound [2][3][16].
- **Hector & Phillips (2018)**: Current recommendations for protein intakes during weight loss in athletes are set at **1.6–2.4 g/kg/day**, with the severity of caloric deficit and training type determining where in this range athletes should target [17].

### 8.2 Resistance Training Guidelines

The NSCA defines periodization as "a method for employing sequential or phasic alterations in the workload, training focus, and training tasks contained within the microcycle, mesocycle, and annual training plan" [7]. The Brookbush Institute systematic review advises that for novice exercisers, program complexity should not be increased with periodized strategies for at least the initial 12 weeks; for experienced exercisers, linear periodization or daily undulation is recommended for endurance, strength, and hypertrophy goals [9].

### 8.3 Weight Loss and Body Composition Consensus

- **Kim (2021)** (*Journal of Obesity & Metabolic Syndrome*): Low-calorie diets (1,000–1,500 calories/day with 500–750 calorie deficits) are recommended as the initial strategy; very-low-calorie diets (<800 kcal/day) should only be used short-term with medical monitoring; high-protein diets (30% of daily calories or 1–1.2 g/kg ideal body weight) improve satiety, preserve lean body mass, and prevent weight regain [17].
- **Janssen, Van Every & Phillips (2023)** (*Current Opinion in Clinical Nutrition and Metabolic Care*): Practical recommendations for VLCDs include minimum protein intake of 1.2–1.5 g/kg/day, limiting the magnitude of energy deficit, and spreading protein intake evenly across multiple daily feedings; per-meal protein feedings of 0.30–0.59 g/kg (younger to older adults), targeting 1.2–1.6 g/kg/day total [16].
- **Mesinovic et al. (2025)** (*Reviews in Endocrine and Metabolic Disorders*): Practical guide emphasizing that 10–58% of weight lost can be fat-free mass, and that resistance training and adequate protein are the primary countermeasures [6].
- **Carbone & Pasiakos (2019)** (*Nutrients*): During moderate energy deficit (500–750 kcal/day), protein intakes of ~1.6 g/kg/day preserve muscle mass; this protective effect is compromised when energy deficit exceeds 40% of daily needs [51].

### 8.4 Evidence Quality Assessment

- **High-quality evidence**: Protein supplementation enhances resistance-training-induced lean mass and strength gains (Morton 2018, high-quality meta-analysis of 49 RCTs) [1][2][3]; protein timing around training is not critical (Schoenfeld 2013) [10][35]; periodized versus non-periodized training for strength (Williams 2017; Moesgaard 2022) [2][3][8].
- **Moderate-quality evidence**: HIIT produces similar or modestly superior fat loss versus MICT with less time commitment [10][14][16]; protein intake ≥1.6 g/kg/day enhances LBM gains during resistance training (Nunes 2022) [5][6]; resistance exercise preserves FFM during dietary weight loss (Binmahfoz 2025, moderate certainty) [9].
- **Conflicting/inconsistent findings**: (1) Whether HIIT is superior to MICT for fat loss (Keating 2017 found similar benefits; Guo 2023 found HIIT superior for WC and %FM) [14][17]; (2) whether TRE causes meaningful lean mass loss (Chen 2025 found no lean loss in women; Sun 2025 and Chen 2026 found 0.58–0.84 kg FFM loss) [22][23][25]; (3) whether periodization model affects strength gains (Harries 2015 found no differences; Rhea 2002 and Moesgaard 2022 favor DUP in trained individuals) [3][6][8].
- **Retracted evidence**: Viana et al. (2019) was retracted on 15/12/2020; the claim that interval training provides 28.5% greater fat mass reduction should not be cited [22][27].
- **Evidence gaps**: No retrieved RCTs specifically tested periodization models under controlled hypocaloric conditions in recreationally active 18–35-year-olds; reverse dieting lacks direct RCT evidence; the specific population of interest (recreationally active 18–35) is underrepresented relative to overweight/obese and older adult populations.

## 9. Integrated Recommendations for Body Recomposition (18–35, Recreationally Active)

Based on the synthesized evidence, the following integrated protocol represents the best-supported approach:

1. **Energy deficit**: Target a moderate deficit of **~20–25% (approximately 400–500 kcal/day below maintenance)**, corresponding to weight loss of ~0.5–0.7% body mass per week. Avoid deficits exceeding 500 kcal/day if the goal includes lean mass gain; larger deficits (up to 40%) can produce recomposition only when protein is elevated to 2.4 g/kg/day and training volume is high [13][14][18].

2. **Protein intake**: Consume **1.6–2.4 g/kg/day** during the cut, scaling toward the higher end with more aggressive deficits. For leaner individuals (<15% body fat for men, <25% for women), intakes up to **2.3–3.1 g/kg FFM** (approximately 2.0–2.7 g/kg body mass) are supported [17][18][21]. Distribute protein at **0.4 g/kg per meal across 4+ meals**; consider 27.5–40 g pre-sleep casein [36][37][42][43].

3. **Resistance training**: Perform **≥10 weekly sets per muscle group**, 3–4 days/week, using either linear or undulating periodization—the model matters less than maintaining or increasing volume [2][3][31]. Heavy loads (70–85% 1RM) combined with moderate loads optimize both strength and hypertrophy [1][2].

4. **Cardiovascular training**: Incorporate **HIIT (≥75 min/week) or MICT (≥150 min/week)** based on preference and time availability. HIIT offers superior VO2max improvements and comparable fat loss with less time; MICT may be preferable for those who find HIIT aversive [10][14][16][30]. Both can be performed alongside resistance training without compromising lean mass [13][14][20].

5. **Meal timing**: Total daily intake matters more than timing. If using TRE or intermittent fasting, restrict the eating window to **8–10 hours with the last meal before ~17:00** where possible, ensure protein intake is adequate within the window, and be aware of the small but consistent FFM loss signal associated with these protocols [23][25][29][30]. The 5:2 approach combined with resistance training produces equivalent body composition outcomes to continuous restriction [27].

6. **Maintenance**: After the cut, transition gradually to energy balance while maintaining protein at ≥1.6 g/kg/day and training volume. Consider structured diet breaks during the cut to improve adherence and potentially enhance FFM retention [19][20]. Prioritize intrinsic motivation and enjoyment in exercise selection to sustain adherence long-term [24].

## 10. Conclusion

The international literature published between 2015 and 2026 supports the feasibility of body recomposition in recreationally active adults aged 18–35. The most robust findings are: (1) protein intake of ~1.6–2.2 g/kg/day maximizes resistance-training-induced lean mass gains, with higher intakes (2.4 g/kg/day) required to support lean mass gain during aggressive caloric restriction; (2) resistance training volume is the primary training variable for lean mass preservation, with the specific periodization model being of secondary importance; (3) HIIT and MICT produce broadly similar fat loss outcomes, with HIIT offering superior time efficiency and VO2max improvements; (4) moderate deficits (~500 kcal/day) represent the upper limit for concurrent lean mass gain, though recomposition is possible in more aggressive deficits with high protein and training volume; (5) meal-timing protocols such as TRE and intermittent fasting produce equivalent weight loss to continuous restriction but carry a small risk of lean mass loss that may be mitigated by adequate protein and resistance training; and (6) long-term maintenance is supported by gradual dietary transitions, sustained training volume, and behavioral strategies that prioritize enjoyment and supervision. The evidence quality is highest for protein intake and training modality questions, moderate for caloric deficit magnitude and meal timing, and limited for specific maintenance protocols such as reverse dieting.
