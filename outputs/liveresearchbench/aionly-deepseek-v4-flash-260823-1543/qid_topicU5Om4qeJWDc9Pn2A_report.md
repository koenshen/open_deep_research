# Ultra-Processed Foods and Modern Health Problems: A Comprehensive Scientific and Scholarly Report

## Executive Summary

Ultra-processed foods (UPFs) now constitute a dominant portion of global dietary intake, accounting for approximately 57–58% of total energy consumption among US adults, nearly 70% of American children's calories, and rapidly increasing proportions in low- and middle-income countries. This report synthesizes the latest worldwide research across four critical dimensions: biological mechanisms, industry influence on behavior, individual susceptibility factors, and environmental/structural determinants. The evidence demonstrates that UPFs harm health through multiple interconnected pathways—disrupting gut microbiota, altering satiety hormone signaling, promoting endocrine disruption via food contact chemicals, triggering neuroinflammation, and hijacking reward pathways—while simultaneously being engineered for hyper-palatability, aggressively marketed to vulnerable populations, and made structurally unavoidable through food environment inequities, economic pressures, and policy failures. A landmark 2024 umbrella review in *The BMJ* analyzing 45 pooled meta-analyses from nearly 10 million participants found convincing evidence linking UPF exposure to higher risks of cardiovascular mortality (50% increase), type 2 diabetes, anxiety outcomes, and combined common mental disorders. The report concludes that addressing the UPF crisis requires coordinated action across clinical practice, public health policy, food system reform, and regulatory frameworks.

## 1. Biological Mechanisms: How Ultra-Processed Foods Disrupt Human Physiology and Neurology

### 1.1 Gut-Brain Axis Disruption

The microbiota-gut-brain axis represents a bidirectional communication network between gut microorganisms, gut cells, and the brain. UPFs disrupt this axis through multiple mechanisms that are only now being fully elucidated.

#### 1.1.1 The Microbiota-Gut-Brain Axis Framework

The Quadram Institute describes the microbiota-gut-brain axis as "a web of connections that allows communication both ways between the gut microorganisms, our gut cells, and the brain" [1]. A varied diet rich in fibers promotes beneficial gut microbes, while a diet high in refined sugars and low in fiber reduces microbial diversity. Research from the University of East Anglia and Quadram Institute (2024) found that mice fed a low-fat processed diet had changes in their gut that affected brain functions through altered bile acid concentrations, with the high-sugar, fiber-poor diet disrupting gut microbiota and affecting how the body processed bile acid, linked to changes in inflammation-related genes in the brain and decline in brain function [1].

#### 1.1.2 Food Additives and Gut-Brain Signaling

A review by Song et al. (2023) published in *Food Research International* examines the effects of UPFs on the microbiota-gut-brain (MGB) axis. The review highlights that UPFs—containing high levels of saturated/trans fats, added sugar, salt, and food additives—negatively impact gut microbiota composition and function, potentially leading to intestinal inflammation, metabolic dysbiosis, and adverse effects on brain health [2]. Specific findings include:

- Emulsifiers such as polysorbate 80 (P80) and carboxymethylcellulose (CMC) can directly affect microbiota, drive intestinal inflammation, and alter anxiety-like and social-related behaviors
- Artificial sweeteners including saccharin, sucralose, aspartame, and acesulfame may be etiologic factors related to the onset of inflammatory bowel disease
- A prospective cohort study cited in the review shows that every 10% increase in UPF intake per day increased dementia risk by 25%; replacing 10% UPF intake with unprocessed or minimally processed foods decreased dementia risk by about 20%

#### 1.1.3 Gut Hormone Secretion Alterations

**The Landmark Hall et al. (2019) Randomized Controlled Trial**

The landmark randomized controlled trial from the NIH (Hall et al., 2019, *Cell Metabolism*) demonstrated that ultra-processed food causes overeating and weight gain through eating speed—not taste preference [3][4]. The study involved 20 adults who lived in an NIH metabolic ward for 28 days, spending 2 weeks on an ultra-processed diet and 2 weeks on an unprocessed diet in random order. Both diets were matched for calories offered, macronutrients, fiber, sugar, and sodium. Key results included:

- 508 extra calories per day consumed on ultra-processed food (p=0.0001)
- Weight change of 0.9 kg gained on ultra-processed food and 0.9 kg lost on unprocessed food in just 14 days each
- Eating rate was 17 calories/minute faster on ultra-processed food
- The appetite-suppressing hormone PYY was significantly higher after unprocessed food, indicating that the ultra-processed diet suppressed satiety hormone signaling
- The weekly cost for ingredients was estimated at $106 for ultra-processed meals vs. $151 for unprocessed meals

The study concluded that "limiting consumption of ultra-processed foods may be an effective strategy for obesity prevention and treatment" [3][4].

**The Food Matrix Collapse Hypothesis**

A review article published in *Frontiers in Nutrition* on May 5, 2026, by Wang et al. proposes a conceptual framework arguing that the primary health threat of UPFs stems not merely from poor nutritional composition but from the industrial collapse of their physical "food matrix" [5]. This structural disintegration triggers a top-down cascade of dysregulation:

1. **Oral processing**: The soft matrix of UPFs accelerates eating rates and weakens early satiety signals
2. **Gastrointestinal tract**: Rapid nutrient absorption suppresses secretion of satiety hormones GLP-1 and PYY (the "ileal brake" mechanism)
3. **Metabolic dysregulation**: Supraphysiological nutrient flux drives insulin resistance, de novo lipogenesis, and mitochondrial stress
4. **Gut microbiome imbalance**: The impoverished matrix combined with chemical additives causes microbiome imbalance, compromised intestinal barrier function, and systemic low-grade chronic inflammation via metabolic endotoxemia

The authors state: "The core threat of UPFs to health may originate profoundly from the industrial collapse of their physical 'food matrix'" [5].

**Artificial Sweeteners and Gut Hormone Disruption**

A review article published in the *International Journal of Molecular Sciences* (2025) by Roberto Coccurello examines how non-caloric sweeteners (NCSs) disrupt the gut-brain axis [6]. Key findings include:

- NCS-induced dysbiosis reduces beneficial taxa such as *Akkermansia muciniphila*, *Faecalibacterium prausnitzii*, *Bifidobacterium*, and *Lactobacillus*
- Diminishes short-chain fatty acid (SCFA) production (butyrate, propionate)
- Impairs gut barrier integrity and promotes systemic inflammation via elevated lipopolysaccharide (LPS) levels
- Blunts satiety signaling and favors appetite-promoting pathways
- A landmark 120-participant RCT showed NCS-induced glycemic responses are microbiota-mediated and transmissible via fecal transplant

Regarding neural reward processing, NCSs decouple sweet taste from caloric content, creating a "reward prediction error": sweet taste signals reward, but no calories follow. NCSs produce weaker dopaminergic responses in the ventral tegmental area (VTA) and dorsal striatum compared to sugar, which may drive compensatory food-seeking and overeating [6]. The review concludes that "both top-down (neural) and bottom-up (microbial) mechanisms converge to foster maladaptive food seeking, metabolic dysregulation, and increased vulnerability to overeating" [6].

#### 1.1.4 Eating Rate and Energy Intake

A randomized controlled trial from Wageningen University (Netherlands) found that texture-derived differences in meal eating rate significantly influence energy intake from UPFs [7]. Over 14 days, 41 participants who consumed a UPF diet with textures that slowed eating rate averaged 369 fewer kcal per day (a cumulative difference of over 5,000 kcal over the 14-day period) compared to when they consumed a UPF diet with textures promoting faster eating. Both diets were matched for energy density, portion size, total energy served, and Nova 4 classification. Despite consuming fewer calories, participants reported no differences in appetite or diet satisfaction. The research, led by Prof. Ciarán Forde, found "the consistency of the effect of meal texture on eating rate and intake was striking" [7].

A separate analysis of 327 foods across 5 countries confirmed the gradient: unprocessed foods at 36 kcal/min, processed at 54, ultra-processed at 69 [3].

### 1.2 Microbiome Alterations

#### 1.2.1 Overview of UPF-Driven Gut Dysbiosis

A review published in *Nutrients* (2025) reports that "UPF accounts for up to 58% of daily caloric intake in high-income countries and approximately 30% in emerging economies" [8]. Key findings include:

- UPFs are associated with intestinal dysbiosis, reduced microbial diversity, decreased short-chain fatty acid production, impaired barrier integrity, and mucus layer disruption
- "Consuming 10% of calories from ultra-processed foods per day is associated with a 19% increased risk of developing Crohn's disease"
- "Serum LPS levels are reportedly six times higher in patients with active Crohn's disease compared to healthy controls"

A comprehensive review published February 28, 2025, in *Nutrients* by researchers from Università Cattolica del Sacro Cuore and Fondazione Policlinico Universitario Gemelli IRCCS in Rome, Italy, examines the detrimental impact of UPFs on the human gut microbiome and gut barrier [9]:

- **Healthy gut microbiome**: Dominated by Firmicutes and Bacteroidetes phyla, with key beneficial bacteria including *Akkermansia muciniphila*, *Faecalibacterium prausnitzii*, and *Bifidobacteria*. The microbiome produces essential SCFAs (acetate, propionate, butyrate) which maintain gut barrier integrity, modulate immunity, and regulate metabolism
- **UPF-driven gut alterations**: UPF consumption is associated with decreased microbial α-diversity, reduced beneficial bacteria, and increased pro-inflammatory microorganisms including *Granulicatella*, *Blautia*, *Carnobacteriaceae*, *Bacteroidaceae*, *Peptostreptococcaceae*, *Bacteroidia*, and *Bacteroidetes*
- "Commonly utilized emulsifiers, including carboxymethylcellulose (CMC), polysorbate 80 (P80), carrageenan, and gums, are known to alter gut microbiota composition, thereby promoting a pro-inflammatory microbial environment that may contribute to the development of metabolic disorders such as obesity and T2DM"

#### 1.2.2 Emulsifiers and Microbiome Disruption

BBC Future reports that emulsifiers, which help mix oils/water and extend shelf life, are found in about half of UK supermarket products (6,640 products analyzed) [10]. Animal studies led by Benoit Chassaing at Institut Pasteur show emulsifiers cause gut bacteria to move closer to the gut wall, eroding the protective mucus layer and triggering inflammation. Chassaing notes: "They are only tested for the toxicity effect or for the ability to induce DNA damage. And for those two aspects, they are perfectly fine. But they were never tested in the past for the direct effect on the microbiome" [10].

Human correlational studies link emulsifier exposure to increased risk of type 2 diabetes (over 100,000 adults, 2024 French study) and breast/prostate cancers (over 90,000 adults) [10]. The PEN (Practice-based Evidence in Nutrition) article reports that one small RCT (n=16) found that 15g/day of CMC for 14 days reduced microbiota diversity, altered fecal metabolome, and caused abdominal discomfort [11].

#### 1.2.3 Artificial Sweeteners and Microbiome Alterations

A review article published in *Cureus* on September 23, 2024, explores the long-term effects of artificial sweeteners on metabolic health [12]. High consumption of ASs is linked to a 30% increased risk of metabolic problems, cardiovascular disease, and certain cancers. The Women's Health Initiative found artificially sweetened beverages associated with elevated risks of stroke, coronary heart disease, and mortality. Specific sweetener risks include:

- Aspartame associated with neurotoxicity and cancer risks
- Sucralose alters gut flora and glucose metabolism
- Saccharin linked to IBD and gut microbiota disruption
- Acesulfame-K raises concerns about metabolic disruption and cancer risk

An original research article published in *Frontiers in Microbiology* on May 15, 2025, by Kidangathazhe et al. examined the effects of five common artificial sweeteners on gut microbiome diversity using minibioreactor arrays [13]. Synthetic sweeteners (Sucralose and Saccharin) significantly reduced microbial diversity. Sucralose enriched potentially pathogenic families like Enterobacteriaceae. Non-synthetic sweeteners (Rebaudioside A and Xylitol) were less disruptive, promoting beneficial taxa such as Lachnospiraceae and Ruminococcaceae. Acesulfame K increased diversity but disrupted network structure and was the only sweetener that did not recover structural integrity after withdrawal [13].

A study published in *iScience* (December 19, 2023) by Cedars-Sinai investigators compared duodenal (small bowel) and stool microbiomes in adults consuming non-aspartame non-sugar sweeteners, aspartame only, and controls [14]. Artificial sweeteners significantly altered microbial diversity and composition in both the small bowel and stool, with effects differing between the small and large bowel. In aspartame consumers, the bacterial pathway for cylindrospermopsin—a toxin harmful to the liver and nervous system—was enriched. Dr. Ruchi Mathur noted: "Artificial sweeteners are not benign for the microbiome of the gut" [14].

### 1.3 Endocrine Disruption

#### 1.3.1 Food Contact Chemicals as Endocrine Disruptors

A review article published in *Cancers* (2025) by Fajkić et al. titled "Ultra-Processed Diets and Endocrine Disruption, Explanation of Missing Link in Rising Cancer Incidence Among Young Adults" presents a narrative review examining the link between UPFs and rising cancer rates among adolescents and young adults (AYAs, defined as 15–39 years old) [15]. UPFs act as "Trojan horses"—seemingly harmless, convenient foods that deliver endocrine-disrupting chemicals (EDCs) such as bisphenols, phthalates, and nitrates that migrate from packaging into food. A 10% increase in UPF proportion in diet is associated with higher incidence and mortality from ovarian cancer, brain cancer, and lymphomas (UK Biobank data). EDCs exhibit non-monotonic dose-response relationships, meaning "low dietary exposures through UPFs may have disproportionately significant biological effects, especially if they occur during hormonal-sensitive developmental stages, such as adolescence" [15].

#### 1.3.2 Specific Additives with EDC Properties

A review article published in the *Journal of Xenobiotics* on November 4, 2024, by Paramasivam et al. examines how food additives in processed foods act as endocrine-disrupting chemicals [16]. Six major food additives with EDC properties are identified:

- **Erythrosine (Red No. 3)**: Interferes with thyroid iodine uptake, decreases T3/T4 levels, increases TSH, exhibits estrogenic activity
- **Tartrazine (Yellow No. 5)**: Binds to estrogen receptors, alters hormone synthesis and metabolism enzyme activity
- **Bisphenol A (BPA)**: Mimics estrogen by binding to ERα and ERβ, disrupts thyroid hormone signaling, interacts with androgen receptors
- **Artificial sweeteners**: Influence hormone release (insulin, leptin, ghrelin), alter gut microbiota composition
- **Phthalates**: Bind to estrogen, androgen, and PPAR receptors; disrupt hormone synthesis enzymes
- **Parabens**: Exhibit weak estrogenic activity, inhibit aromatase, interfere with thyroid peroxidase function

The review concludes that EDCs "potentially increasing the risk of obesity, type-2 diabetes, cardiometabolic diseases and may also trigger allergic reactions" [16].

#### 1.3.3 Phthalates, Bisphenols, and UPF Consumption

An article from the Food Packaging Forum (January 22, 2024) reports on two studies linking ultra-processed food consumption to increased exposure to phthalates and microplastics [17]:

**Study 1 (Baker et al., *Environment International*, Jan 2024)**: Examined 1,031 pregnant women from the CANDLE study. Found that "each 10% higher dietary proportion of ultra-processed foods was associated with 13.1% higher urinary levels of the molar sum of five di(2-ethylhexyl) phthalate metabolites (ΣDEHP)." Lower income and education levels were associated with 1.9% and 1.4% higher ΣDEHP, respectively, mediated via increased ultra-processed food consumption.

**Study 2 (Milne et al., *Environmental Pollution*, Dec 2023)**: Analyzed 111 US protein-rich food samples across 13 protein types. Found microplastics (>50 µm) in 88% of samples. Highly-processed products contained significantly more microplastics than minimally-processed ones, with levels ranging from 0.01 particles/g (chicken breast) to 1.3 particles/g (breaded shrimp).

#### 1.3.4 The Plastics-UPF Nexus

A commentary published in *Globalization and Health* (Oct 24, 2024) by Yates et al. from the London School of Hygiene & Tropical Medicine explores the toxic relationship between UPFs and plastics [18]. The authors argue that these two industries are co-dependent, generating substantial profits for agri-food and petrochemical companies at high costs for human and planetary health. The plastics industry is worth over $700 billion annually, with production projected to triple from 460 million metric tons (2019) to 1.2 billion metric tons by 2060. Thousands of plastic food contact chemicals (FCCs) migrate into foodstuffs, including known hazardous substances like bisphenol A (BPA), ortho-phthalates, and PFAS. A recent report identified over 16,000 plastic chemicals, with almost 60% (over 9,000) "lack specific information on their use or have not been analyzed in plastics, based on industry or scientific sources" [18].

UPF consumption is associated with higher levels of urinary phthalates, bisphenols, and PFAS, including in children and pregnant women. Major UPF producers (Coca Cola, PepsiCo, Unilever, Nestlé) are among the leading plastic polluters worldwide, selling around 5.5 billion products daily. The FAO calculates that the annual public health costs and productivity losses of consuming unhealthy diets, in which UPFs play a significant role, exceed $9 trillion globally [18].

#### 1.3.5 Early-Life Exposure and Obesogens

A review article published on January 8, 2025, in *Current Nutrition Reports* by Celik and Yesildemir reviews the latest evidence on how early-life exposure to endocrine disruptors contributes to obesity and metabolic disorders in children [19]. According to the "environmental obesogen hypothesis," exposure to EDs early in life (including in utero) may impair adipogenesis or energy storage-related mechanisms by increasing the number/size of adipocytes, thereby increasing susceptibility to overweight/obesity. Bisphenol A (BPA) is found in blood, urine, breast milk, and amniotic fluid; BPA can mimic estrogen and activate PPARγ to promote adipogenesis. Phthalates affect energy homeostasis through nuclear receptors (ERα, ERβ, PPARγ, TRs, ARs), exhibit weak estrogenic and strong anti-androgenic effects, and impact adipokine production. The authors recommend "setting national goals, global standards, and policies to reduce environmental exposure to pregnant and lactating women, and babies, considered sensitive populations" [19].

### 1.4 Neuroinflammation and Neurodegeneration

#### 1.4.1 Epidemiological Evidence Linking UPFs to Cognitive Decline

**The Brazilian Study (Gonçalves et al., 2022, *JAMA Neurology*)**

A study analyzing data from 10,775 Brazilian public servants (average age 52) from the Brazilian Longitudinal Study of Adult Health, across three waves from 2008-2019, found that individuals consuming more than 20% of daily calories from ultraprocessed foods had a 28% faster rate of global cognitive decline and a 25% faster rate of decline in executive function compared to those with lower intake [20].

**The Framingham Heart Study (2025, *Journal of Prevention of Alzheimer's Disease*)**

A study published in *The Journal of Prevention of Alzheimer's Disease* (February 2025) examined the association between UPF consumption and risk of all-cause dementia and Alzheimer's disease among 1,375 participants from the Framingham Heart Study Offspring cohort [21]. During a mean follow-up of 12.7 years, 224 individuals were diagnosed with all-cause dementia and 172 with Alzheimer's disease. Among participants <68 years at baseline: each serving per day of UPF was associated with a 13% increased risk for Alzheimer's disease (HR = 1.13, 95% CI: 1.03-1.25); consumption of ≥10 servings/day vs. <10 servings/day was associated with a 2.7-fold increase in Alzheimer's disease risk (HR = 2.71, 95% CI: 1.18-6.24). The study concludes that "consumption of ultra-processed food in middle-age may be linked with an increased risk for Alzheimer's disease" [21].

**Harvard Study (Leung et al., *American Journal of Public Health*)**

A study analyzing data from over 5,300 adults aged 50+ from the University of Michigan's Health and Retirement Study over an average of nine years found that those who ate the highest amount of ultra-processed food daily had a 58% increased risk of developing dementia and a 46% increased risk for cognitive impairment compared to those who ate the least [22]. Processed meats like bacon carried the highest risk. Senior author Cindy Leung noted that "there may not be a safe level" of moderate consumption [22].

**Contrasting Evidence**

A study published in the *European Journal of Nutrition* (February 19, 2026) by Buis et al. examined 1,371 participants from the Longitudinal Aging Study Amsterdam (LASA) and found "no associations... between UPF intake and cognitive function or decline with age for any of the cognitive domains" tested [23]. The authors note that "previous studies on UPF and cognition have shown mixed results; some found faster cognitive decline with higher UPF intake, while others found no association" [23].

#### 1.4.2 Advanced Glycation End Products (AGEs) and Neurodegeneration

A narrative review article published in *Antioxidants* (2025) by Boccardi, Mancinetti, and Mecocci from the University of Perugia and Karolinska Institutet examines the metabolic interplay between oxidative stress and advanced glycation end products (AGEs) in Alzheimer's disease [24]. AGEs form via the Maillard reaction and are accelerated by oxidative stress. Dietary AGEs from ultra-processed foods (UPFs constitute ~55% of US adult caloric intake) and high-temperature cooking contribute to systemic burden. AGE-RAGE signaling activates NADPH oxidase, NF-κB, and pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-1β, TNF-α, IL-6), promoting Aβ aggregation, tau hyperphosphorylation, mitochondrial dysfunction, autophagic impairment, and BBB breakdown. This creates a feed-forward loop of chronic inflammation and oxidative stress [24].

The review states: "The AGE–RAGE interaction acts as a central node linking oxidative stress and inflammation in AD... the AGE–RAGE axis contributes to the increased amyloidogenic processing of APP, Tau hyperphosphorylation, mitochondrial dysfunction, autophagic impairment and cerebrovascular injury, and blood–brain barrier (BBB) compromise" [24].

#### 1.4.3 Systemic Inflammation and Neuroinflammation

A cross-sectional study published in *Frontiers in Nutrition* (June 2026) by Italian researchers examined the relationship between UPF consumption and mild cognitive impairment (MCI) in 92 elderly participants (mean age 73.2 years) [25]. Higher UPF consumption was significantly associated with lower cognitive scores. In adjusted models, high UPF intake was associated with significantly increased odds of MCI (Model 2 OR = 4.24, 95% CI: 1.05–17.13). After adjusting for inflammatory biomarkers (TGF-β1 and TNF-α), the association was attenuated and no longer statistically significant, though the direction remained positive. The study concludes that "higher consumption of UPFs may be associated with increased likelihood of MCI, and that this relationship may be partly explained by systemic inflammation" [25].

A scoping review published in *Frontiers in Nutrition* (January 21, 2026) by Poon et al. examined neurobiological insights into the effects of UPF on lipid metabolism and associated mental health conditions [26]. Proposed mechanisms include: systemic low-grade inflammation (saturated fatty acids activate TLR4 receptors on macrophages and microglia, triggering NF-κB signaling and pro-inflammatory cytokine production); altered neuronal signaling, particularly dopamine and serotonin pathways; gut-brain axis disruption (high UPF intake decreases SCFA production, compromising BBB integrity); neuroinflammation (inflammatory cytokines cross BBB, leading to microglial activation and impaired neurogenesis); HPA axis dysregulation; advanced glycation end products (AGEs) formed during high-temperature processing; and hormonal dysregulation (BPA and contaminants from food packaging alter gut microbiome) [26].

#### 1.4.4 UPFs and Brain Development

A review article published in *Frontiers in Public Health* (2025) titled "The consequences of ultra-processed foods on brain development during prenatal, adolescent and adult stages" reports that UPFs constitute 17.2% of the total food intake among women in their third trimester of pregnancy [27]. The period from 24 to 42 weeks of gestation represents a critical window for child neurodevelopment, during which processes such as synapse formation and myelination are actively taking place. Adolescence is a critical stage of brain maturation, particularly susceptible to the effects of micronutrient deficiencies, often exacerbated by diets high in UPFs. The Developmental Origins of Health and Disease (DOHaD) hypothesis posits that adverse early-life exposures can permanently alter structure and function, predisposing to behavioral and cognitive disorders. Early UPF exposure shapes long-term food preferences, with preferences for sweet/salty foods persisting into adulthood [27].

New research from Children's Hospital Los Angeles (published in the *American Journal of Clinical Nutrition*, 2026) found that "higher ultra-processed food intake had smaller volumes in brain regions tied to reward and emotion by age six" [28].

### 1.5 Reward Pathway Dysregulation and Addiction Neurobiology

#### 1.5.1 The Food Addiction Framework

A review article published in *Pharmacological Research* (Volume 224, February 2026) by Hough, Friuli, Avena, and Romano (Icahn School of Medicine at Mount Sinai) explores the hypothesis that food addiction shares common clinical and neurochemical mechanisms with traditional substance use disorders [29]. "Emerging evidence suggests that ultra-processed foods (UPFs), particularly those high in refined sugars and saturated fats, may elicit neurobiological responses akin to those observed in substance use disorders." Animal and human studies demonstrate that "excessive consumption of palatable foods can induce behaviors characteristic of addiction—bingeing, craving, tolerance, and withdrawal—accompanied by significant dopaminergic alterations within the mesolimbic reward circuitry." "Chronic overconsumption of UPFs alters dopaminergic tone, disrupts prefrontal control, and activates stress pathways, thereby reinforcing compulsive intake" [29].

The Yale Food Addiction Scale (YFAS) and its pediatric adaptations provide structured tools for identifying food addiction phenotypes in clinical and research settings. The review "underscores the need to reframe UPFs as potentially addictive agents and calls for integrative therapeutic strategies and policy-driven reforms aimed at mitigating their impact on public health" [29].

#### 1.5.2 Neuroimaging Studies

**PET Study on Dopamine Responses (Darcey et al., 2025, *Cell Metabolism*)**

A study published in *Cell Metabolism* (2025) investigated whether ultra-processed milkshakes high in fat and sugar elicit postingestive brain dopamine responses similar to drugs of abuse [30]. Researchers used [11C]raclopride PET displacement methods to measure striatal dopamine responses in 50 young, healthy adults across a wide BMI range. Contrary to the hypothesis, "milkshake consumption did not result in significant postingestive dopamine response in the striatum (p=0.62) nor any striatal subregion (p>0.33)." The authors conclude that "the narrative that ultra-processed foods high in fat and sugar can be as addictive as drugs of abuse based on their potential to elicit an outsized dopamine response in brain reward regions was not supported by our data" [30]. However, exploratory analyses revealed that participants who showed dopamine responses in the expected direction ("Responders") perceived the milkshake as more pleasant, wanted more of it, and tended to be hungrier in the fasted state [30].

**Systematic Review of fMRI Studies (Pursey et al., 2024, *Current Addiction Reports*)**

A systematic review published in *Current Addiction Reports* by Pursey, Yokum, Brain, and Burrows synthesizes studies using fMRI to assess neural responses associated with food addiction as measured by the YFAS [31]. The review included 11 studies involving 675 predominantly female participants. Brain areas involved in reward, craving, and appetitive responses (left ACC, left medial OFC, left amygdala, basolateral amygdala, right SFG) were associated with YFAS-assessed food addiction in response to high-calorie processed foods. Effect sizes were large for food-related tasks (d = 1.00–2.40) and medium to large for non-food tasks (d = 0.46–1.39). The prevalence of food addiction has been reported to be 15–20% across published studies and is higher in females, those of higher weight status, and clinical populations [31].

#### 1.5.3 Mechanisms of Hyperpalatability and Addictive Properties

The Veri article explains how Big Food designs products to be addictive through three mechanisms [32]:

1. **Bliss points**: Optimal combinations of salt, sugar, and fat engineered to maximize pleasure
2. **Palatability**: Foods high in salt, sugar, and fat that target the brain's reward system via dopamine release, creating addiction-like deficits
3. **Vanishing caloric density**: Foods that melt quickly in the mouth, tricking the brain into thinking they have no calories, encouraging overconsumption

The article notes that "foods that quickly 'vanish' in your mouth are more rewarding to the brain, reduce gastric satiety, and encourage overconsumption" [32].

The Center for Nutritional Psychology reports on a conceptual paper published in the *Journal of Metabolic Health* by Vera I. Tarman, which proposes a five-stage model of ultra-processed food addiction [33]. Approximately 14% of people worldwide display symptoms of food addiction. The five stages are: (1) Pre-addiction—increased reward activity in the brain with heightened dopamine and opioid signaling, though control is maintained; (2) Early addiction—receptors become less sensitive, opioid release decreases, excessive eating and cravings begin; (3) Mid-addiction—significantly reduced dopamine and opioid release, loss of control, frequent binge eating; (4) Late-stage—further reduced sensitivity, tolerance, severe withdrawal, metabolic disturbances; (5) End-stage—severe reward system deficits, dopamine/opioid depletion, compulsive eating with minimal pleasure, complete loss of control [33].

### 1.6 Cross-Cutting Evidence: Major Systematic Reviews

#### 1.6.1 Umbrella Review in *The BMJ* (February 29, 2024)

A comprehensive umbrella review published in *The BMJ* analyzed 45 pooled meta-analyses from 14 review articles (involving nearly 10 million participants) and found consistent evidence linking UPF exposure to 32 damaging health outcomes [34]. None of the reviewed studies were funded by UPF manufacturers.

**Convincing evidence (Class I)** supported direct associations between greater UPF exposure and higher risks of:
- Cardiovascular disease-related mortality (risk ratio 1.50, 95% CI: 1.37 to 1.63)
- Type 2 diabetes (dose-response risk ratio 1.12, 1.11 to 1.13)
- Prevalent anxiety outcomes (odds ratio 1.48, 1.37 to 1.59)
- Combined common mental disorder outcomes (odds ratio 1.53, 1.43 to 1.63)

**Highly suggestive evidence (Class II)** indicated direct associations with:
- All-cause mortality (risk ratio 1.21, 1.15 to 1.27)
- Heart disease-related mortality (hazard ratio 1.66, 1.51 to 1.84)
- Type 2 diabetes (odds ratio 1.40, 1.23 to 1.59)
- Depressive outcomes (hazard ratio 1.22, 1.16 to 1.28)
- Obesity (odds ratio 1.55, 1.36 to 1.77)

Using GRADE, 22 pooled analyses were rated as low quality, 19 as very low quality, and 4 as moderate quality. 93% of pooled analyses showed point estimates in the same direction (greater than one), indicating consistent increased risk with greater UPF exposure. The accompanying editorial calls for "a UN framework convention on ultra-processed foods, modeled after the tobacco framework" [34].

## 2. Industry Influence on Behavior: Product Design, Marketing, and Formulation

### 2.1 Product Design and Formulation

#### 2.1.1 Hyper-palatability and the "Bliss Point"

The combination of fats, sugars, and salt in ultra-processed foods is designed to generate the "Bliss effect" and addiction in consumers [35]. The "bliss point" is the ideal amount of sugar, salt, and fat that is impossible to resist. Investigative journalist Michael Moss, in his book "Salt Sugar Fat: How the Food Giants Hooked Us" (2013), revealed how food corporations like Kraft, Coca-Cola, Frito-Lay, and Nestlé deliberately engineer products to hit the "bliss point" — the precise combination of salt, sugar, and fat that triggers maximum dopamine release and craving. Research by Fazzino et al. (2019) identified three hyper-palatable food clusters: fat + sodium, fat + simple sugars, and carbohydrates + sodium, with consumption increases of up to 30% [35].

According to the Stanford Medicine article (July 15, 2025), featuring research dietitian Dalia Perelman from the Stanford Prevention Research Center, UPFs "are basically industrial formulations" containing "ingredients you wouldn't find in a typical kitchen — things like emulsifiers, colorings, flavor enhancers, bulking agents, gels" [36]. UPFs are formulated to hit the "bliss point" — the precise combination of sugar, salt, and fat maximizing palatability [36].

A 2024 perspective paper in *PLOS Medicine* by Professor David Ludwig of Harvard Medical School argues that "hyperpalatability" may actually be the wrong explanation. The more compelling mechanism, Ludwig argues, is metabolic: when processed carbohydrates are rapidly digested, blood glucose spikes then crashes — and it's at that crash point, roughly three to four hours after eating, that a brain region linked to craving and reward (the nucleus accumbens) becomes significantly more active [37].

#### 2.1.2 Food Matrix Collapse

As detailed in Section 1.1.3, the collapse of the food matrix is central to satiety disruption. The cascade begins with oral processing, where the soft matrix of UPFs accelerates eating rates and weakens early satiety signals. The review critiques "nutritionism" and fortified UPFs, arguing that adding isolated nutrients to a collapsed matrix cannot restore health value. The authors conclude that the integrity of the food matrix is an indispensable dimension for evaluating the health value of food, and call for a fundamental shift in perspective from focusing solely on "what is in our food" to equally considering "what has been done to our food" [5].

#### 2.1.3 Vanishing Caloric Density and Mouthfeel Optimization

Chris van Tulleken explains the term "vanishing caloric density" in relation to foods such as chips, cookies, and other soft foods that are irresistible [38]. Michael Moss revealed how food scientists calculate the "bliss point" of sugary beverages and enhance the "mouthfeel" of fat by manipulating its chemical structure, exposing marketing techniques borrowed directly from tobacco company playbooks [35].

A Canadian patent (CA2657130C) assigned to Frito Lay Trading Co GmbH describes a method for producing low-fat snack foods that have the appearance and taste of traditional fried snacks without using an oil-frying process [39]. The patent background notes that raw potato slices have 75-85% moisture, and conventional frying causes water to boil, bursting cell walls and allowing oil absorption, yielding oil contents ranging from 30% to 45% by weight. The goal of the patent is to produce a shelf-stable, ready-to-eat snack with low oil content while maintaining the texture and taste of fried products [39].

#### 2.1.4 The NOVA Classification System

The NOVA food classification system, developed by researchers at the University of São Paulo (Carlos Monteiro et al.), categorizes foods based on the nature, extent, and purpose of industrial processing [40][41]. The four groups are: (1) Unprocessed or minimally processed foods; (2) Processed culinary ingredients; (3) Processed foods; and (4) Ultra-processed foods — formulations made mostly from industrial ingredients and additives with little intact food, such as soft drinks, packaged snacks, reconstituted meat products, and pre-prepared frozen dishes. The overall purpose of ultra-processing is to create branded, convenient (durable, ready to consume), attractive (hyper-palatable) and highly profitable (low-cost ingredients) food products designed to displace all other food groups [40][41].

#### 2.1.5 Current Consumption Levels

UPFs now account for nearly 60% of U.S. adults' calorie consumption and close to 70% of American children's intake [36]. 73% of the U.S. food supply is ultra-processed, according to estimation from a machine-learning algorithm that accurately predicted the degree of processing of any food [42]. The average US adult's diet has a majority of daily calories (~57%) from UPFs, a number that has increased throughout the years, up from 53.5% in 2001-2002, with annual per capita consumption increasing ~142% since 1960 [43].

Over half of the adults in the United States have at least one chronic disease, and roughly a quarter of adults have two or more [43]. Over 40% of the roughly 73 million children (aged 0-17) in the United States have at least one chronic health condition, according to the CDC [44]. The U.S. obesity rate is, on average, more than double that of its G7 peers [44].

#### 2.1.6 Not All UPFs Are Equal

The NIH Office of Disease Prevention webinar (July 8, 2026) notes that not all UPFs are the same or associated in the same way with disease [42]. Sugary beverages, animal-based products, and artificial sweeteners showed the strongest adverse associations, while cereals, yogurt, and savory snacks showed inverse associations with disease risk. Some ultra-processed foods (like Cheerios) can be more nutrient-dense than others [36]. Perelman advocates for balance and moderation, not elimination, and suggests prioritizing fresh fruits, vegetables, beans, whole grains, nuts, and seeds while reading ingredient lists carefully [36].

### 2.2 Satiety Overshoot: How UPFs Bypass Satiety Signals

The collapse of the food matrix in UPFs is central to satiety disruption. The cascade begins with oral processing, where the soft matrix of UPFs accelerates eating rates and weakens early satiety signals. In the gastrointestinal tract, rapid nutrient absorption suppresses secretion of satiety hormones GLP-1 and PYY (the "ileal brake" mechanism) [5].

UPFs promote overconsumption through hyperpalatability, altered nutrient profiles (high saturated fat, added sugar, sodium), low water content, high energy density, faster eating rates (especially soft-textured and liquid UPFs), and disruption of evolved nutrient-flavor associations [43]. Over 10,000 food additives have been approved over the last 60 years, with concerns about the "cocktail effect" of cumulative exposures [43].

Appetite regulation refers to the complex physiological processes that control hunger, satiety, and energy balance through coordinated central and peripheral signals [45]. Key hormones include: ghrelin (the "hunger hormone"), leptin (the "satiety hormone"), orexin, MCH, and INSL5 that drive appetite, as well as CCK, PYY, and GLP-1 that promote fullness [46]. GLP-1 delays gastric emptying, controls blood sugar levels, and reduces food intake by acting on appetite-regulating centers in the hypothalamus and brainstem [45]. High-protein meals facilitate the release of GLP-1 and PYY, which reduce hunger. High glycemic index foods cause rapid glucose/insulin spikes and earlier hunger onset.

#### 2.2.1 GLP-1 Medications and UPF Consumption

A notable section of the FoodTimes article discusses how GLP-1 medications like Ozempic and Wegovy may disrupt Big Food's bliss point strategies by altering taste preferences and neurological reward pathways, with users reporting reduced food spending (11% decrease) and developing disgust for industrial foods [35].

### 2.3 Marketing Strategies

#### 2.3.1 Scale of Unhealthy Food Marketing

Food, beverage, and restaurant companies spend nearly $14 billion annually on advertising in the U.S., with 80% promoting fast food, sugary drinks, candy, and unhealthy snacks [47]. Fast-food companies spent approximately $5 billion on U.S. advertising in 2019, with disproportionate targeting of Black and Hispanic youth compared to white youth [47].

#### 2.3.2 Targeted Marketing to Communities of Color

U.S. food companies disproportionately target Black and Hispanic consumers with marketing for high-calorie, low-nutrient products including candy, sugary drinks, snacks, and fast food. The more than $1 billion spent on this targeted marketing exacerbates inequities in poor diet and diet-related diseases in communities of color, including heart disease, obesity, and diabetes [48].

A 2022 Rudd Center report found that U.S. food companies spend over $1 billion on targeted marketing to Black and Hispanic consumers, exacerbating inequities in diet and diet-related diseases [48]. African American children and teens see more than twice as many ads for sugary drinks as their white peers [49]. A 2022 study in the *American Journal of Preventive Medicine* found that "Overall greater health-adverse and fewer health-beneficial advertisements are broadcasted on Spanish-language than on English-language TV" [48].

The Rudd Center's Director of Marketing Initiatives Frances Fleming-Milici stated: "Companies often put their money behind their least healthy products and target those to Black and Latino young consumers. We have evidence that there are disproportionate effects of diet-related disease among those groups. It's higher. So when you target those groups with your least healthy products, you are making the problem worse" [47].

#### 2.3.3 Child-Directed Marketing

Food advertising has played a significant role in driving dietary shifts and maintaining poor dietary intake among children [50]. UPFs account for 49.1% of the total caloric intake among Canadian children aged 2-5 years old and 54.2% among Canadian children aged 6-12 years old [50]. In Canada, children (2-11 years old) are exposed to almost 2,000 advertisements for unhealthy foods annually on television and more than 4,000 on digital platforms [50].

The average Australian 5 to 8-year-old is exposed to at least 827 unhealthy food advertisements on television each year [51]. Australian adolescents are exposed to almost 100 promotions per week from online sources. Children aged 4 to 6 years believe a product tastes better if it has a cartoon character on the pack. For every hour online, children aged 13-17 are exposed to 17 unhealthy food ads [51].

Digital marketing targets children via data-driven personalized ads, influencer endorsements ('kidfluencers'), TikTok campaigns turning users into 'unofficial brand ambassadors', advergames, and in-game marketing [51]. Adolescents are especially vulnerable due to strong engagement with social media, gaming, and streaming services. Marketers build extensive profiles using browsing history, geo-location, and social network data to deliver highly targeted ads [51].

#### 2.3.4 Social Media and Digital Marketing

A Cancer Research UK study found that more than half of the 4,000 young people aged 11 to 21 who took part in a survey had seen posts from businesses and influencers promoting products high in fat, sugar and salt (HFSS) on social media within a month [52]. These posts had a direct impact on young people's behavior, with many reporting feeling tempted, hungry, or craving specific foods after viewing. Influencer marketing is particularly effective because it appears more relatable and trustworthy than corporate advertising, and young people often struggle to recognize when they are being targeted [52].

A study from Children's Hospital Los Angeles (published in the *American Journal of Clinical Nutrition*, 2026) found that higher ultra-processed food intake had smaller volumes in brain regions tied to reward and emotion by age six [28].

#### 2.3.5 Marketing Tactics and Strategies

Corporations use sophisticated tactics including celebrity endorsements (e.g., Bad Bunny), sports sponsorships (FIFA World Cup, Olympics), QR code promotions, school partnerships, social media influencers, and algorithmic micro-targeting based on ZIP codes, ethnicity, and online behavior [47]. The concept of "cradle-to-grave" marketing aims to hook children young for lifelong consumption [47].

Clay Venetis, a policy associate at the Center for Science in the Public Interest: "These unhealthy food companies use cultural moments and important milestones or events in kids' lives as Trojan horses to sneak in their advertising. The goal is to hit people at the exact moment they're most likely to be moved emotionally" [47].

Marion Nestle, a public health advocate and professor at NYU: "Of course it's a Trojan horse. That's the whole point of community support and funding community activities. If you get kids to like and want a product, they will pester their parents for it and consume it for life" [47].

#### 2.3.6 Food Industry Lobbying and Political Strategies

Food companies spend millions lobbying to block regulations on child-targeted advertising, soda taxes, and warning labels [47]. Federal agencies (HHS, CDC, FTC) classify targeted food marketing as a significant public health concern, with "racialized marketing" linked to higher rates of diet-related diseases (obesity, type 2 diabetes, cardiovascular disease) among Black and Hispanic youth [47].

### 2.4 The Food Addiction Concept

#### 2.4.1 Yale Food Addiction Scale (YFAS)

The Yale Food Addiction Scale (YFAS; Gearhardt, Corbin, & Brownell, 2009) is the first measure designed specifically to assess signs of addictive-like eating behavior, particularly regarding highly processed foods [53]. It includes 25 items based on DSM-IV criteria for substance dependence, with two scoring options: a symptom count (0-7) and a dichotomous diagnosis (3+ symptoms plus clinically significant impairment). It has received psychometric support across various populations and has been translated into multiple languages including Arabic, Chinese, Finnish, French, German, Hindi, Iranian, Italian, Malay, Portuguese, Spanish, and Turkish [53].

The YFAS 2.0 (Gearhardt, Corbin, & Brownell, 2016) was developed to maintain consistency with the current diagnostic understanding of addiction (DSM-5 criteria) and to improve the psychometric properties of the original YFAS [54]. The YFAS 2.0 consists of 35 self-report items that assess eating behaviors and emotional responses associated with food consumption over the past 12 months, using an 8-point Likert scale. It covers symptoms such as cravings, loss of control, continued use despite negative consequences, and withdrawal. The scale provides two scoring options: a symptom count score (0-11) and a dichotomous diagnosis (No Food Addiction, or Mild/Moderate/Severe Food Addiction) [54].

#### 2.4.2 Prevalence of Food Addiction

According to the National Institute on Drug Abuse (2020), about 1 in 5 individuals with obesity meet the criteria for food addiction using this scale [55]. A World Health Organization-backed study using YFAS data found that nearly 15% of adolescents globally show signs of food addiction, with symptoms strongly correlated to increased intake of sugary beverages and snack foods [55]. The Yale Food Addiction Scale indicates approximately 15-20% of adults may meet criteria for food addiction [35]. Recent meta-analytic data cited in a 2025 commentary suggest that 14% of adults and 12% of children globally exhibit this addictive-like eating phenotype, a prevalence rate comparable to that of alcohol use disorders [56]. Higher prevalence is found in females (12% vs 6.4%), older adults (age >35), and obese/overweight individuals (25% vs 11%) [57].

#### 2.4.3 The Food Addiction Debate

The concept of food addiction remains debated in the scientific community, with overlaps with disordered eating and emotional eating [58]. Some researchers propose "ultra-processed food addiction" or "ultra-processed food use disorder" as potential diagnostic terms [56]. There currently remains no definitive consensus on diagnostic validity, as some researchers maintain that food craving, emotional eating, binge eating, body-image distress, and perceived loss of control may overlap with existing eating-disorder constructs rather than proving a distinct addiction diagnosis [56].

A systematic review of 52 studies found that every DSM-5 SUD criterion was empirically supported in the context of food, with brain reward dysfunction and impaired control demonstrating the strongest evidence base [56]. Food addiction is associated with poorer quality of life, worse treatment prognosis, and greater psychopathology [59].

## 3. Individual Susceptibility: Genetic, Epigenetic, Microbiome, Psychological, and Sociodemographic Factors

### 3.1 Genetic Factors

#### 3.1.1 DRD2 (Dopamine D2 Receptor)

The DRD2 gene encodes the dopamine D2 receptor. The primary SNP studied is rs1800497 (Taq1A). The 'A' (also known as the 'A1') allele correlates with a 30% lower number ('density') of D2 receptors [60]. According to a large meta-analysis of 33 trials, there was no strong or consistent correlation between this variant and body mass index (BMI). However, people with the 'A' allele may not respond well to (or comply with) weight-loss interventions. Around 33% of European descendants carry the 'A' allele, but this variant is up to twice as common among East Asian and African populations (64-65%) [60].

People with rs1800497-A crave tasty, energy-dense foods — but the feelings of 'reward' they experience upon consumption may be relatively weaker. In five clinical trials with over 1,300 participants, the 'A' allele correlates with higher preference for carbohydrates, sugar, fast food, and high-fat fried foods. Brain imaging studies show people with the 'A' allele have a blunted reward response to tasty food despite craving it. The variant is associated with uncontrolled eating, emotional eating, high food reinforcement, and consuming more calories [60].

The TaqA1 allele has a 30-40% reduction in D2 receptor density in the dorsal striatum [61]. Carriers (18% Caucasians, 38% Africans, 39% Asians) have reduced dopamine function, requiring more food to achieve the same reward, and have increased risk for addiction disorders and obesity [61].

A study in *Frontiers in Behavioral Neuroscience* (March 31, 2023) evaluated the association between the bilocus genetic profile (rs1799732-rs1800497) of the dopaminergic pathway with food reinforcement and food addiction in Chilean adults [62]. The cross-sectional study of 221 adults (74% female) aged 18-54 found that the genetic variants rs1799732 and rs1800497 were associated with anthropometric measurements but not with food addiction or food reinforcement. The authors suggest that other genotypes, such as rs4680 and rs6277, which affect DA signaling capacity through a multilocus composite score, should be studied [62].

#### 3.1.2 DRD4 (Dopamine D4 Receptor)

The DRD4 7R allele (7-repeat allele) is linked to novelty seeking, ADHD, impulsivity, and exploratory behaviors. Referred to as the "adventure gene," it leads to less responsive dopamine receptors, causing individuals to seek new and stimulating experiences. May contribute to overeating or impulsivity [63].

#### 3.1.3 MC4R (Melanocortin 4 Receptor)

Mutations in the MC4R gene account for 6-8% of obesity cases and represent the most common genetic cause of human obesity [64]. A common variant of the MC4R gene (rs17782313, C-allele), distributed in about 22% of the population, increases the risk for weight gain by causing increased appetite and decreased satiety. Each copy of the C-allele at this SNP correlates to a BMI increase of about 0.22 and an 8% increased risk for being overweight. Carriers experience increased appetite and decreased satiety, leading to larger food intake, more frequent snacking, and preference for fatty foods [64].

To date, 166 MC4R mutations have been documented, with about 0.66% of the general population carrying one. In early-onset severe obesity populations, 6-8% carry MC4R loss-of-function mutations. These individuals typically develop obesity by age 3-4, with meals containing about three times the calories of non-carriers [64].

#### 3.1.4 FTO (Fat Mass and Obesity-Associated Gene)

FTO represents the fat mass and obesity-associated gene which holds the distinction of being the most researched gene related to body weight and obesity [65]. The key SNP rs9939609 is strongly correlated with obesity across age groups and ethnicities. Carriers of the 'A' allele show higher weight gain, obesity prevalence, elevated ghrelin levels, increased food consumption, preference for calorie-rich foods, less meal satisfaction, emotional/binge eating, greater insulin resistance, and higher blood sugar. The 'T' allele carriers typically maintain normal weight with greater satiety and healthier food choices [65].

A healthy diet provides individuals with the 'A' variant protection against its negative health effects. A three-year investigation demonstrated that individuals with the 'AA' genotype experience better weight loss outcomes on a Mediterranean diet than those with the 'TT' genotype [65]. However, a study published in *BMC Nutrition* (2023) investigating the relationship between the FTO rs9939609 (A/T) polymorphism and food preferences found no significant differences between groups in sweet food preference or fatty food preference, concluding that the results do not support the hypothesis that FTO rs9939609 polymorphism influences different food preferences [66].

#### 3.1.5 OPRM1 (Mu-Opioid Receptor)

The OPRM1 gene provides instructions for making a protein called the mu (μ) opioid receptor [67]. The best-studied OPRM1 gene polymorphism, called A118G or rs1799971, changes a protein building block in the μ opioid receptor protein. This is a "gain-of-function" mutation with ~3-times greater affinity for endorphins [61]. Carriers (38% Asians, 16% Caucasians, 1% Africans) show greater preference for sweet and fatty foods but paradoxically are less likely to gain weight due to heightened satisfaction [61].

The combination of the OPRM1 G-allele (118G) with the normal DRD2 A2A2 genotype creates the highest sensitivity toward palatable foods. This combination is found in 11% of Caucasians and 14% of Asians (less than 0.4% of Africans). These individuals have a dramatically increased risk for binge eating disorder when they frequently consume palatable foods [61].

#### 3.1.6 Taste Receptor Polymorphisms (TAS1R, TAS2R, CD36)

A systematic review published in *Frontiers in Genetics* (December 19, 2019) by Diószegi et al. investigated the genetic background of taste perception, taste preferences, and their nutritional implications [68]. Of 949 identified publications, 103 articles were included. Key findings include:

- **Bitter Taste (64 studies)**: TAS2R38 variants (rs713598, rs1726866, rs10246939) were strongly associated with PTC/PROP sensitivity and bitterness perception of foods like vegetables
- **Sweet Taste (28 studies)**: TAS2R38 polymorphisms were also associated with sweet taste preference and sugar intake. TAS1R2 and TAS1R3 variants showed inconclusive or limited associations
- **Fat Taste (22 studies)**: The most convincing results were for CD36 polymorphisms (rs1761667, rs1527483) associated with oleic acid threshold and fat perception

Heritability estimates range from high to moderate for bitter tasting stimuli [0.72, 0.71, 0.34, for PROP, PTC, and quinine hydrochloride, respectively] and moderate for sweet tasting compounds (glucose: h² = 0.31, fructose: h² = 0.34) [68].

The SWEET Project (*Nutrients*, 2025) investigated how SNPs in TAS1R2 and TAS1R3 genes affect receptor activation by 12 different sweeteners [69]. Although certain human TAS1R2/TAS1R3 SNPs showed reduced receptor response, they were not associated with particular phenotypes. The authors concluded that "sweet taste perception and sugar consumption are governed by factors other than the TAS1R2 and TAS1R3 genes" [69].

### 3.2 Epigenetic and Developmental Factors

#### 3.2.1 Developmental Origins of Health and Disease (DOHaD) Paradigm

The DOHaD hypothesis posits that environmental exposures during critical windows of early development—from preconception through infancy—have lasting effects on health and disease risk across the lifespan [70]. Originally conceptualized by Dr. David Barker in the 1980s through observations linking low birth weight to adult cardiovascular disease, the framework has expanded to encompass non-communicable diseases (NCDs) including obesity, diabetes, metabolic syndrome, neurodevelopmental disorders, and mental health conditions [70].

The core premise is that genetic heritage does not define one's destiny; rather, lifestyle choices and environmental factors influence gene expression through epigenetic modifications (methylation and other chemical changes) without altering the DNA sequence itself [71]. Research shows how external factors influence genetic function, resulting in altered longevity and the incidence of cancer and age-related diseases [71].

#### 3.2.2 Key Mechanisms of Developmental Programming

Epigenetic modifications (DNA methylation, histone changes) are influenced by one-carbon metabolism nutrients (folate, choline, methionine, vitamin B12) [70]. Hormonal signaling (cortisol, insulin, leptin) affects fetal HPA axis, metabolic regulation, and appetite. Placental function adapts nutrient transport to maternal nutritional status. The maternal microbiome influences infant microbial colonization and immune development. The "first 1000 days" of life, spanning preconception, pregnancy, and early infancy, represent a uniquely sensitive period of biological plasticity during which biological systems are established and calibrated [70].

#### 3.2.3 Maternal and Paternal Influences

Children of obese mothers are twice as likely to be obese at age 2. Infants of overweight/obese mothers have higher fat mass at birth and increased risk of metabolic disorders [71]. Undernutrition during prenatal life compromises the male germline, contributing to environmentally induced diseases (paternal transgenerational epigenetic inheritance). Men eating high saturated fat had 38% lower sperm concentrations and 41% lower sperm counts [71].

The Hertfordshire study (UK) found low birth weight (<2.5 kg) associated with coronary death, type 2 diabetes, and metabolic syndrome [71]. The Dutch Hunger Winter study showed that famine during pregnancy resulted in elevated rates of adult obesity, hyperlipidemia, and cardiovascular disease [71].

#### 3.2.4 Early-Life Nutrition and Appetite Regulation

Feeding behavior is a complex trait dedicated to the regulation of food intake in order to meet the physiological needs of the body. The early environment plays a major role in the establishment of feeding behavior both at the physiological and psychological levels [72]. Several animal models have clearly shown that either severe undernutrition during pregnancy or placental insufficiency induce intra-uterine growth retardation (IUGR), leading to a low birth weight associated with low plasma leptin levels [72].

Fetal exposure to flavors via amniotic fluid, breastfeeding's positive effect on food acceptance, parental feeding practices, and socioeconomic context all shape feeding behavior from early life [72]. Studies on Dutch famine survivors show conflicting results regarding later food preferences (some show preference for high-fat diets) [72].

#### 3.2.5 Early-Life UPF Exposure

UPFs constitute 17.2% of the total food intake among women in their third trimester of pregnancy [27]. The period from 24 to 42 weeks of gestation represents a critical window for child neurodevelopment, during which processes such as synapse formation and myelination are actively taking place. Adolescence is a critical stage of brain maturation, particularly susceptible to the effects of micronutrient deficiencies, often exacerbated by diets high in UPFs [27].

Prenatal UPF consumption is associated with adverse offspring outcomes including altered neurodevelopment, increased adiposity, and immune-related conditions. Mechanisms include nutritional deficiencies, metabolic dysregulation, inflammation, endocrine disruption, and microbiota alterations [73]. Early UPF exposure shapes long-term food preferences, with preferences for sweet/salty foods persisting into adulthood [27].

In high-income countries, children and adolescents derive 50-60% of daily energy from UPFs (58% in US, 42% in Australia, 25.9% in Italy). In low/middle-income countries, UPFs account for 16-30% of energy intake [73]. High UPF intake is linked to excess weight gain, metabolic syndrome, cardiovascular risk, dental caries (71% increased risk), and allergic diseases (atopic dermatitis, asthma) [73].

### 3.3 Microbiome-Associated Susceptibility

#### 3.3.1 UPF-Induced Gut Dysbiosis

The consumption of UPFs may lead to the proliferation of pro-inflammatory microbial strains and reduced microbial α-diversity in the gut [74]. High UPF consumers show increased levels of potentially harmful bacterial groups including Granulicatella, Blautia, Carnobacteriaceae, Bacteroidaceae, Peptostreptococcaceae, Bacteroidia, and Bacteroidetes. Conversely, high UPF consumers showed decreased levels of Lachnospira and Roseburia, which are known producers of short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) [9].

UPFs, characterized by a high content of synthetic additives and emulsifiers, and low fiber content, are associated with a decrease in microbial diversity, lower levels of beneficial bacteria like Akkermansia muciniphila and Faecalibacterium prausnitzii, and an increase in pro-inflammatory microorganisms [9]. These alterations in the microbial community contribute to persistent inflammation, which is associated with various chronic disorders including metabolic syndrome, irritable bowel syndrome, type 2 diabetes, and colorectal cancer [9].

#### 3.3.2 Metabolic Endotoxemia

Metabolic endotoxemia is a condition in which blood lipopolysaccharide (LPS) levels are elevated, regardless of the presence of obvious infection. It has been suggested to lead to chronic inflammation-related diseases such as obesity, type 2 diabetes mellitus, non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD), pancreatitis, amyotrophic lateral sclerosis, and Alzheimer's disease [75].

In 2007, Cani et al. showed that mice fed with a high-fat diet had higher blood LPS levels than normal chow-fed mice, resulting in inflammation of the liver and adipose tissue, which led to the development of NAFLD and insulin resistance [75]. LPS enters the bloodstream through lipid absorption (via chylomicrons), increased intestinal permeability due to disrupted tight junctions, and potentially through constipation/peristalsis reduction. The LPS concentration in the portal blood is approximately 10 times higher than the LPS concentration in the peripheral blood [75].

#### 3.3.3 UPF-Induced Dysbiosis and Disease Links

- **Diabetes**: Higher Firmicutes-to-Bacteroidetes ratio and lower Akkermansia muciniphila (which improves insulin sensitivity) [74]
- **Inflammatory Bowel Disease**: Reduced α-diversity and low anti-inflammatory bacteria (Roseburia hominis, Akkermansia muciniphila) [74]
- **Cancer**: Colorectal cancer associated with loss of anti-inflammatory bacteria and increased pathobionts (Bacteroides fragilis, Fusobacterium nucleatum, Parvimonas micra) [74]
- **Neurological Disorders**: Gut-brain axis disruption linked to Parkinson's disease, Alzheimer's disease, and major depressive disorder (characterized by reduced microbial richness and diversity) [74]

### 3.4 Psychological and Behavioral Factors

#### 3.4.1 Hedonic Hunger and UPF Consumption

A study published in *Frontiers in Public Health* (February 16, 2026) investigated how food and nutrition literacy (FNL) relates to ultra-processed food (UPF) consumption, hedonic hunger, emotional eating, and sleep quality among 1,400 university students in Ankara, Türkiye [76]. Females scored significantly higher than males in FNL knowledge and attitude sub-dimensions, but no gender difference was found in the behavior sub-dimension. Females had higher hedonic hunger, higher emotional eating, and poorer sleep quality than males. Individuals with lower FNL levels showed higher hedonic hunger, poorer sleep quality, greater UPF consumption, and more pronounced emotional eating. UPF consumption was high overall: 67% of participants were classified as high UPF consumers, and 77.7% had poor sleep quality [76].

#### 3.4.2 Impulsivity and Delay Discounting

A study on hedonic hunger, UPF consumption, and the moderating effects of impulsivity in pregnant individuals with BMI ≥ 25 included 220 participants (mean age 31.6 years, mean pre-pregnancy BMI 32.0) [77]. UPF consumption averaged 62.5% of total energy intake. Self-report impulsivity did not moderate this association, but delay discounting did (p=.01). Among participants with lower delay discounting (less impulsive decision-making), a one-unit increase in hedonic hunger was associated with 7% lower UPF intake (p=.01). Among those with higher delay discounting, the association was not significant. These findings suggest that lower delay discounting may serve as a protective factor during pregnancy [77].

#### 3.4.3 Stress Reactivity and Emotional Eating

Chronic stress, via glucocorticoids, stimulates appetite for palatable foods and contributes to leptin resistance and insulin resistance [78]. Emotional eating is driven by emotional cues (depression, anxiety, happiness, boredom) rather than hunger. It is regulated by the brain's reward system, which involves motivation (wanting), outcome (liking), memory (learning), and habituation (adapting). Palatable foods (high-sugar, high-fat) act like addictive substances in the reward system [61]. About 3.5% of women and 2% of men in the US are diagnosed with binge eating disorder at some point in their lives [61].

#### 3.4.4 Mental Well-Being and UPF Consumption

An analysis of Global Mind Project data from 400,787 adults across 60 countries, published in *Frontiers in Nutrition* (Bala et al., 2025), found that frequent consumption of UPFs is associated with worse overall mental well-being [79]. The study revealed that 28% of participants showed clinical mental distress levels, while 31% reported consuming UPFs at least once a week. Among those who rarely or never consumed UPFs, 20% were classified as clinically distressed, compared to 54% in the highest UPF consumption group. The main contributors to this link were increased symptoms of depression and challenges with emotional and cognitive control. The mental distress burden linked to UPF consumption in the United States and the core anglosphere was higher than the global average [79].

### 3.5 Sociodemographic Factors

#### 3.5.1 Systematic Review of Sociodemographic Determinants

A systematic review published in *Nutrition Research Reviews* (Cambridge University Press, October 31, 2023) examined sociodemographic determinants of UPF consumption using nationally representative samples [80]. The review included 55 papers spanning 32 countries and 36 nationally representative surveys. Thirteen sociodemographic variables were identified, all significantly associated with UPF intake in at least one study. Higher UPF intakes were associated with younger age, urbanization, urban residence, and being unmarried, single, separated, or divorced. UPF intake differences of 10-20% of total energy were seen across age, race/ethnicity, rural/urbanization, food insecurity, income, and region. Education, income, and socioeconomic status showed varying associations depending on the country [80].

#### 3.5.2 Country-Specific Patterns

- **Australia**: UPF = 38.8% of energy; younger age, lower education, and middle income associated with higher intake
- **Brazil**: UPF = 20-30% of energy; younger age, higher income, higher education, and urban residence associated with higher intake
- **Canada**: UPF ≈ 45-47% of energy; younger age, male gender, lower education, food insecurity, and non-immigrant status associated with higher intake
- **France**: UPF ≈ 31%; younger age, higher education, urban residence associated with higher intake
- **Italy**: UPF = 17.3% in adults; younger age, female gender, urban residence, unmarried status associated with higher intake
- **Korea**: UPF = 24.9%; younger age, male gender, higher education, urban residence associated with higher intake
- **Mexico**: UPF = 29.8%; younger age, higher education, higher socioeconomic status, urban residence associated with higher intake
- **Netherlands**: UPF = 61% of energy; younger age, middle education, urban residence associated with higher intake

The review concludes that "significant sociodemographic variation exists in UPF consumption, highlighting public health inequalities and the urgent need for policy action to minimize social injustice-related health inequalities" [80].

#### 3.5.3 U.S. Disparities in UPF Purchases

A study from researchers at the Global Food Research Program at UNC-Chapel Hill and The George Institute for Global Health, published in *Public Health Nutrition*, examined over 33 million packaged food and beverage purchases from 2020 across nearly 60,000 U.S. households [81]. Nearly half of all packaged food purchases and over one-third of beverage purchases were ultra-processed. Specific categories were overwhelmingly ultra-processed: 90% of carbonated soft drinks, 81% of mixed dishes and soups, and 71% of sweets and snacks fell into the UPF category. Only 12% of purchases were minimally processed items like fruits, vegetables, nuts, and oils. Disparities were observed across socioeconomic groups: lower-income and lower-education households purchased more UPFs, while non-Hispanic White households purchased significantly more UPFs than Black, Hispanic, or other race/ethnic groups [81].

#### 3.5.4 Acculturation and UPF Consumption

A study published in *Frontiers in Public Health* (May 15, 2025) investigated the relationship between acculturation and UPF consumption among foreign-born adults in the United States using data from 3,639 non-US born adults (aged 19-70) from NHANES 2011-2018 [82]. Overall, 43.3% of dietary energy came from UPFs among foreign-born adults. As acculturation increased, UPF consumption rose linearly: each unit increase in AcI score corresponded to a 3% increase in mean UPF consumption. Non-Hispanic White individuals had the highest UPF intake (47.9%), while NH-Asians had the lowest (35.5%). The authors recommend targeted interventions promoting healthier dietary choices while preserving culturally relevant eating habits [82].

#### 3.5.5 UK Adolescents

A study published in 2024 examined UPF consumption among UK adolescents using data from the National Diet and Nutrition Survey (NDNS) spanning 2008/09 to 2018/19, analyzing 2,991 adolescents aged 11-18 years [83]. Mean UPF consumption was 861 g/day, accounting for 65.9% of total energy intake (TEI). Between 2008 and 2019, mean UPF consumption decreased from 996 to 776 g/day and from 67.7% to 62.8% of TEI. Higher %TEI was consumed by adolescents with lower socioeconomic status, white ethnicity, and living in England North. The study found significant social and regional patterning of UPF consumption, suggesting inequalities associated with UPF intake and factors beyond individual choice [83].

## 4. Environmental and Structural Factors: Food Deserts, Food Swamps, Economic Inequality, and Policy Approaches

### 4.1 Food Deserts and Food Swamps

#### 4.1.1 Definitions and Terminology

**Food Deserts** are geographic areas where residents have few to no convenient options for securing affordable and healthy foods, especially fresh fruits and vegetables [84]. The term refers to "low-income and low-access areas" where residents face barriers to healthy food due to geography, limited income, transportation, and availability. The term is being phased out at the federal level in favor of "low-income and low-access areas" [85]. It is criticized for implying scarcity is natural, masking deliberate policy decisions (e.g., redlining, suburbanization), ignoring racial disparities, and not reflecting community vibrancy. Baltimore, Maryland has shifted to using "Healthy Food Priority Areas" [85].

**Food Swamps** are "areas in which large relative amounts of energy-dense snack foods, inundate healthy food options" (Donald Rose et al.) [85]. Food swamps are neighborhoods characterized by an abundance of fast-food restaurants and convenience stores that primarily offer ultra-processed, high-calorie foods, while healthy options remain scarce [84]. The term describes areas where unhealthy food outlets (fast food, convenience stores) outnumber healthy options [85].

**Food Security** is defined as consistent access to sufficient, safe, nutritious, and culturally appropriate food, according to the United Nations' Committee on World Food Security [85]. Food security includes the dimensions of (1) Availability; (2) Access; (3) Use; and (4) Stability of these three dimensions.

#### 4.1.2 Prevalence and Geographic Distribution

Nearly 39.5 million people (13% of the U.S. population) live in low-income, low-access areas, more than 1 mile (urban) or 10 miles (rural) from the nearest supermarket. Almost 19 million people (6% of the population) have limited access to a supermarket [84]. A report from the USDA Economic Research Service estimated that 7.3 million Americans live in low-income census tracts that are distant from a fully stocked grocery store or supermarket [86].

Among states with the greatest share of residents living in low-income, low-food-access areas, nine of the top 10 are located in the South. Mississippi ranks first with 30% of residents in food deserts, followed by New Mexico (29%) and Arkansas (26%) [84]. Among large metro areas, Memphis has the highest share (32%), followed by San Antonio and Riverside, CA (26% each) [84].

#### 4.1.3 Food Swamps vs. Food Deserts: Health Outcomes

Research increasingly demonstrates that food swamps are stronger predictors of obesity and diet-related disease than food deserts alone [84]. A seminal study published in the *International Journal of Environmental Research and Public Health* (2017) found that food swamps are a stronger predictor of obesity rates than food deserts across U.S. counties. Even after controlling for food desert effects, food swamps had a positive, statistically significant effect on adult obesity rates. The traditional RFEI (ratio of fast food restaurants and convenience stores to grocery stores/supermarkets) had the strongest relationship with obesity rates [87]. The food swamp effect was stronger in counties with greater income inequality (p<0.05) and where residents are less mobile (p<0.01) [87].

A 2023 study found that counties with the highest food swamp scores had a 77% increase in obesity-related cancer mortality compared with counties with lower food swamp scores [84].

#### 4.1.4 Recent Trends (2003-2023)

A new study published in the *American Journal of Public Health* (June 2026) reveals that between 2003 and 2023, nearly five million Americans lived in food deserts (areas with no grocery stores), predominantly in poor, rural areas and communities reliant on public transit [88]. Food swamps increased from 80.2% to 88.5% of census tracts (2003 to 2023). Food deserts declined only slightly, from 6.1% to 5.5% over the same period. Food deserts were substantially higher in rural, economically disadvantaged (persistent poverty) areas and among public transit users. If only relying on public transit, over 7.4 million people would be living in food deserts [88].

Lead author Dr. Daniel Wiese (principal scientist, cancer disparity research at ACS) stated: "Everyone in the United States should be living in a food oasis rather than a food desert. Unfortunately, our findings show many neighborhoods where healthy food options, especially fresh fruits and vegetables, are almost nonexistent, and fast-food restaurants and corner stores with only a few healthy options are dominating the landscape in these same neighborhoods. This needs to change" [88].

#### 4.1.5 Causes of Food Deserts

Causes include food distribution systems favoring convenience stores in low-income areas, supermarket disinvestment in lower-income neighborhoods, transportation challenges, and income inequality (healthier diets cost ~$1.50 more per day) [84]. Root causes of food swamps include redlining, white flight, and zoning policies [85]. Critics note the term "food swamp" still does not adequately address root causes, with some advocating for "food apartheid" as a better descriptor [85].

### 4.2 Economic Inequality and Food Pricing

#### 4.2.1 Cost Differentials Between UPFs and Whole Foods

Americans pay 40% more for fruits and vegetables due to supply chain costs [89]. A 2022 survey found 46% cited high cost of healthy food as the biggest barrier to a healthier diet [89]. 18 million U.S. households experienced food insecurity in 2023 [89]. Americans scored an average of just 58 out of 100 on the USDA's Healthy Eating Index [89].

Processed foods are cheaper, more shelf-stable, and aggressively marketed, but contribute to chronic diseases like diabetes, heart disease, and obesity [89]. There's evidence that inflation continues to shape food choices, particularly for low-income Americans who prioritize price over healthfulness. Higher food prices often increase food insecurity. "These shifts increase the risk of chronic diseases such as diabetes, heart disease and obesity" [89].

#### 4.2.2 Cost and Affordability of Healthy Diets Globally

The Food Prices for Nutrition DataHub, a World Bank-hosted platform, provides global statistics on the cost and affordability of healthy diets across over 170 countries [90]. Key findings from the SOFI 2025 report indicate that approximately 2.6 billion people (about one-third of the global population) could not afford a healthy diet in 2024, representing a reduction from the COVID-19 pandemic peak in 2020, though recovery has been uneven with worsening trends in low-income countries and sub-Saharan Africa [90].

#### 4.2.3 Price Elasticity of Demand and Fiscal Policies

A study published in the *American Journal of Preventive Medicine* (July 2024) by Pourya Valizadeh and Shu Wen Ng examined the impact of nationwide taxes on unhealthy ultraprocessed foods (UPFs) and subsidies for minimally processed foods (MPFs) for low-income households [91]. Using 2017–2018 Nielsen Homescan Panel data (11,152 low-income and 47,518 higher-income U.S. households), the study found that a sugar-based tax on sugar-sweetened beverages (SSBs) would lower both volume and calories purchased, with the largest impact on low-income households without children. Targeted subsidies would increase fruit, vegetable, and healthier drink purchases without substantially increasing calories. The authors concluded: "Levying national taxes on unhealthy ultraprocessed foods/beverages and offering targeted subsidies for minimally processed foods/beverages could promote healthier food choices among low-income households" [91].

#### 4.2.4 Agricultural Subsidies and the US Farm Bill

The US Farm Bill is a massive piece of legislation that addresses agricultural commodities, conservation, trade, nutrition, rural development, energy, forestry, and more [92]. The last comprehensive Farm Bill passed in 2018; its provisions expired in 2023 and have been renewed only year-to-year since. The July 2025 "One Big Beautiful Bill Act" fundamentally disrupted the historic bipartisan compromise that paired farm subsidies with nutrition assistance (SNAP). It slashed SNAP spending by $186 billion over ten years (with about one-third of cuts shifted to state budgets), while boosting farm subsidies for key crops like corn, soybeans, and wheat by $60 billion, plus an additional $12 billion in December 2025 to offset tariff-related export losses [92].

Of the $1.4 trillion in the Congressional Budget Office's baseline projected outlays (2027–36) for farm and nutrition programs, nutrition programs account for more than 70 percent—with most of the remaining spending directed to crop insurance, commodity programs, and conservation [93]. Mandatory nutrition-program spending was projected to account for 72 percent ($985 billion) of the 10-year projected outlays from 2027–2036 [93].

#### 4.2.5 European Union Common Agricultural Policy (CAP)

The common agricultural policy (CAP) is a partnership between agriculture and society, and between Europe and its farmers [94]. Launched in 1962, the CAP aims to support farmers and ensure Europe's food security. The CAP 2023-27 entered into force on 1 January 2023, representing a modernised policy with emphasis on results and performance. The CAP is financed through two funds: the European Agricultural Guarantee Fund (EAGF) at €291.1 billion, and the European Agricultural Rural Development Fund (EAFRD) at €95.5 billion, totalling €387 billion for the 2021-27 period [94]. The CAP has been criticized for alleged anti-development effects, funding of animal farming, oversupply and redistribution issues, artificially high food prices, negative impacts on public health, harming smaller farms, and environmental problems [95].

### 4.3 Built Environment and Food Access

#### 4.3.1 Supermarket Availability and Food Purchasing

A study published in *Public Health Nutrition* (2019) by Peng and Kaza examined whether neighborhood supermarket and convenience store availability and broader built environment context are associated with food purchasing behavior [96]. Using data from the Nielsen Homescan Consumer Panel for 2010 (22,448 households across 378 US metropolitan statistical areas), the study found that when controlling for broader built environment context, there was NO significant association between neighborhood supermarket availability and fruit/vegetable expenditures. An INVERSE association was found between convenience store count and fruit expenditures. Higher regional destination accessibility was associated with higher fruit expenditures. The researchers concluded that policy interventions focusing exclusively on increasing neighborhood supermarket availability likely will not promote fruit and vegetable consumption [96].

#### 4.3.2 Walkability and Dietary Outcomes

Walkability is a core urban design element because of being advantageous onto three fronts: health, livability, and sustainability [97]. Walking is associated with lower rates of obesity, diabetes, cardiovascular diseases, and improved mental health. Key physical environment factors for walkability include connectivity/accessibility, mixed land use, greenness and parks, transit-oriented development (TOD), pedestrian thermal safety/comfort, and road safety. There is no unified universal standardized walkability theory despite the need for rigorous evaluation tools for policymakers and developers [97].

#### 4.3.3 Neighborhood Deprivation and UPF Availability

Disadvantaged areas are more likely to lack quality food retailers and infrastructure that supports healthy eating, such as public transit [98]. High-poverty, non-white (particularly Black) neighborhoods have the least supermarket access [84]. A 2022 study examined U.S. census tracts by race and ethnicity, poverty level, and access to quality food stores. It found that high-poverty, non-white — particularly Black — neighborhoods continue to have the least access to supermarkets [84].

A systematic review of 54 studies (published 2009) found that residents with better supermarket access and limited convenience store access tend to have healthier diets and lower obesity levels. Low-income, minority, and rural neighborhoods have the poorest access to supermarkets and healthful foods. Fast-food restaurants are more concentrated in lower-income and minority neighborhoods. Lower-income ZIP codes had 1.3 times more fast-food restaurants than high-income areas. Fast-food restaurants also cluster near schools in low-income and racially mixed areas [99].

### 4.4 Policy and Regulatory Approaches

#### 4.4.1 Front-of-Pack Nutrition Labeling (FOPNL) in the Americas

A PAHO policy study examined the development, adoption, and implementation of front-of-pack nutrition labelling (FOPNL) systems across the WHO Region of the Americas (AMRO) [100]. In AMRO, all 35 countries have discussed FOPNL, 30 countries have formally introduced FOPNL, 11 have adopted FOPNL, and 7 countries (Argentina, Chile, Ecuador, Mexico, Peru, Uruguay, and Venezuela) have implemented FOPNL as of August 2022. FOPNL has gradually spread and evolved to better protect health by increasingly adopting larger warning labels, contrasting background devices for better salience, using "excess" instead of "high in" to improve efficacy, and adopting the Pan American Health Organization's (PAHO) Nutrient Profile Model to better define nutrient thresholds. Early evidence shows successful compliance (75-80%+ in Chile and Peru), decreased purchases of unhealthy products, and product reformulation [100].

#### 4.4.2 Chile's Warning Label Law

Chile was the first country to implement a mandatory, national front-of-package nutrient warning label policy (Law of Food Labeling and Advertising, passed in 2016, implemented in three progressively stricter phases: 2016, 2018, 2019) [101]. The law introduced mandatory black octagonal front-of-package nutrient warning labels for products high in sugar, salt, saturated fat, and calories; marketing restrictions protecting children; and bans on ultra-processed product sales in schools.

A study published in *The Lancet Planetary Health* (2021) examined changes in household food and beverage purchases following Chile's Phase 1 implementation. Compared to the counterfactual, overall calories purchased declined 16.4 kcal/capita/day (4%), overall sugar declined 11.5 kcal/capita/day (10%), saturated fat declined 2.2 kcal/capita/day (4%), and sodium content of overall purchases declined 27.7 mg/capita/day (5%) [102].

In Phase 2 of the warning label law, Chilean households purchased 37% less sugar, 22% less sodium, 16% less saturated fat, and 23% fewer total calories from products with warning labels. These changes were equitable across socioeconomic groups [101]. In the final and most nutritionally strict phase of the law, a remarkable 94% of products required to display these labels had the appropriate labels—a stark contrast to low uptake of voluntary labeling programs in Australia and New Zealand [101].

Chile's policy has inspired similar "stop sign" warning label policies across eight countries in the Americas. The evaluations of Chile's policy clearly demonstrate the necessity of mandatory front-of-package labeling systems featuring nutrient warning labels, which have been shown to be the most effective in helping consumers identify unhealthy products [101].

#### 4.4.3 Mexico's Warning Labels

In Mexico, a modelling study projected that the warning labels could reduce the prevalence of obesity by 14.7% between 2018 and 2023, and save the country US$ 1.8 billion in obesity-related costs [100].

A study published in *Preventing Chronic Disease* (CDC, June 2026) compared changes in sugar-sweetened beverage (SSB) consumption among adults in Mexico and the US before and after Mexico implemented mandatory front-of-package warning labels (FoPWLs) in 2020, while the US updated Nutrition Facts labels during the same period [103]. From 2018–2019 to 2020–2023, any SSB consumption and SSB consumption frequency increased in the US. In Mexico, any SSB consumption also increased, but increases were smaller than in the US. In sex-stratified models, Mexican women had a greater relative reduction in SSB volume than US women. The study concluded: "FoPWLs may help curb whether and how often adults consume SSBs. Additional measures may be needed to reduce the amount of SSBs consumed" [103].

#### 4.4.4 UK HFSS Regulations

The UK's HFSS (High in Fat, Sugar, and Salt) regulations mark one of the most sweeping changes in food marketing legislation in the UK [104]. The regulations restrict how products high in saturated fat, sugar, and salt can be marketed, advertised, and promoted.

**Timeline of Regulations:**
- **October 1, 2022**: Placement restrictions took effect in England, banning HFSS products from prominent retail positions (aisle ends, checkouts) in larger retailers
- **October 2025**: Volume promotions ban (e.g., "Buy One, Get One Free") took effect in England
- **January 5, 2026**: Advertising restrictions (TV pre-9 PM watershed ban and paid online advertising limitations) came into force UK-wide
- **Spring 2026**: Wales intends to implement placement and promotion restrictions
- **Autumn 2026**: Scotland expected to implement its own measures

**How HFSS Classification Works**: Products are scored using the Government Nutrient Profile Model (NPM). Points are added for calories, fats, sugars, and sodium (Category A). Points are subtracted for beneficial nutrients like fiber, protein, and fruit/vegetable content (Category C). A score of 4+ for foods or 1+ for drinks earns HFSS designation [104].

**Impact Data**: End-of-aisle promotions increased sugary drink sales by 51.7%. Early restrictions have shown healthier product sales up 5.6% and non-compliant product sales down 5.1% [104].

#### 4.4.5 Sugar-Sweetened Beverage (SSB) Taxes

Over 130 jurisdictions across nearly 120 countries and territories have implemented taxes on sugar-sweetened beverages (SSBs) to date [105]. Data from the UK, Mexico, South Africa and other countries indicate that SSB taxes successfully reduce sugar consumption from SSBs, and SSB taxes have led to reformulation of products in the UK and other countries.

**Mexico** (2014): Introduced a 1 peso/L volumetric tax, leading to ~11% price rise on soft drinks. By 2016, there was a 37% reduction in SSB purchases. Over 10 years, the SSB tax is predicted to prevent 239,900 cases of obesity; of these, 39% would be in children. The tax is predicted to save almost $4 per $1 spent on implementation [105].

**United Kingdom** (2018): The Soft Drinks Industry Levy (SDIL) is a two-tiered tax based on sugar concentration. It led to reformulation removing 45 million kg of sugar from soft drinks each year. The proportion of high-sugar drinks in supermarkets fell from 49% to 15% (2015-2019). Sugar purchased from taxed drinks decreased by 35%. The tax is predicted to reduce dental caries by 3,600 cases and overweight/obesity by over 64,000 cases among under-18s [105].

**South Africa** (2018): The Health Promotion Levy (10% tax on sugary drinks) led to a 29% reduction in carbonated drink purchases in urban areas and 51% reduction in sugar purchased. Lower socioeconomic groups showed a 57% drop in sugar from taxed drinks [105].

**Berkeley, California**: The first US SSB tax, enacted in 2014 and implemented in 2015 at $0.01 per ounce, received 76% voter support. Consumption of SSBs decreased 21% in Berkeley and increased 4% in comparison cities. Water consumption increased more in Berkeley (+63%) than in comparison cities (+19%). The 21% reduction in SSB consumption represents a price elasticity of -2.6, larger than the average elasticity of -1.2 found in other studies [106].

#### 4.4.6 School Food Policies

The USDA Food and Nutrition Administration published a final rule titled "Child Nutrition Programs: Meal Patterns Consistent With the 2020-2025 Dietary Guidelines for Americans," effective July 1, 2024, with required changes phased in starting school year 2025-26 at the earliest [107]. The rule updates school nutrition standards including: first-time limits on added sugars in school breakfasts and lunches, reducing sodium gradually, maintaining whole grain-rich requirements, and allowing flavored milk with no more than 10 grams of added sugars per 8 ounces by 2025-26. Research shows that school meals are the most nutritious food source for American schoolchildren, but currently about 17% of calories in school breakfasts and 11% in school lunches come from added sugars, and over 90% of schoolchildren consume more sodium than recommended [107].

The 2025-2030 Dietary Guidelines for Americans (DGAs) released in January 2026 by USDA and HHS include, for the first time, urging Americans to limit consumption of "highly processed" foods (recommending avoidance of chips, cookies, candy with added sugars and sodium, and sugar-sweetened beverages) [108]. CDC survey data shows youth ages 18 and under consume more than 60% of their daily calories from ultraprocessed foods.

California became the first state to ban ultra-processed foods in K-12 through a recently signed law that will phase those products out of school meals starting in 2029 [109].

However, school nutrition directors face major barriers. A School Nutrition Association (SNA) survey found that 95% of school nutrition directors are seriously or moderately concerned about financial sustainability three years from now. More than 93% cited the need for more staff, culinary training, equipment, and infrastructure to reduce reliance on UPFs. 79% of respondents reported an "extreme need" for increased funding to expand scratch cooking and reduce ultraprocessed foods [109]. 98% of operators are challenged by food costs, 70% say federal reimbursement rates (max $4.60/meal) are insufficient to cover costs, and over 90% need more staff, training, equipment, and infrastructure [109].

### 4.5 Global Trade and Food Systems

#### 4.5.1 Global UPF Consumption Trends

Ultra-processed products are not modified foods, but formulations mostly of cheap industrial sources of dietary energy and nutrients plus additives [40]. The most striking change in food systems of high-income countries, and now of low- and middle-income countries, is displacement of dietary patterns based on meals and dishes prepared from unprocessed or minimally processed foods by those that are increasingly based on ultra-processed food and drink products [40].

In 2000, sales of ultra-processed foods and beverages in the upper-middle-income countries were one third of those in the high-income countries. Fifteen years later, they were more than half [40]. UPFs now account for over half of total calorie intake in countries like the US (≈58%) and the UK (≈57%) [110]. Annual per capita consumption of UPFs is estimated at 100 kg in Western Europe and 120 kg in North America and Australasia, with ultra-processed drinks accounting for an additional 180 kg and 120 kg, respectively [111]. In the US, over 70% of packaged foods are ultra-processed, and these foods make up 70-80% of the diet for the top consumers in the US, UK, and Australia [111].

#### 4.5.2 Global Trade Liberalization and UPF Imports

**New Zealand Case Study**: A study published in *Global Health* (2026) examined New Zealand's food and beverage imports from 1990–2023, categorized by the Nova classification system, alongside progressive trade liberalization [112]. The share of ultra-processed foods in total food and beverage imports increased over the study period, from 15.7 kg per capita (8.7% of food imports) in 1990 to 103.8 kg per capita (21.8%) in 2023. The per capita import volume of food-derivatives/additives (UPF inputs like industrial sweeteners, modified oils, flavorings, and texture enhancers) increased dramatically since 1990, surpassing all other UPF subgroups since 2011. By 2023, NZ imported 28 kg per capita of these UPF inputs, with industrial sweeteners alone accounting for 20.7 kg per capita. Tariff rates were reduced to zero or low levels for all food products between 1990–2023. The authors conclude that 34 years of progressive trade liberalization in NZ were characterized by an increasing proportion of imported products being ultra-processed foods/beverages and their inputs [112].

#### 4.5.3 Market Strategies of Multinational Food Corporations

A systematic review and document analysis published in *Globalization and Health* (2021) by Wood, Williams, Nagarajan, and Sacks examined the market strategies used by dominant processed food manufacturers to increase and consolidate their power [113]. The study identified 21 market strategies categorized into six interconnected strategic objectives: (i) reduce intense competition with equivalent-sized rivals and maintain dominance over smaller rivals; (ii) raise barriers to market entry by new competitors; (iii) counter the threat of market disruptors and drive dietary displacement in favor of their products; (iv) increase firm buyer power over suppliers; (v) increase firm seller power over retailers and distributors; and (vi) leverage informational power asymmetries in relations with consumers [113].

The authors highlight that the concentrated market power of dominant food companies is a public health concern because it allows them to shape food value chains toward production of unhealthy products and generate excess profits that fund lobbying, marketing, and other practices undermining public health [113].

#### 4.5.4 Environmental Impacts of UPFs

UPFs account for 17-39% of total diet-related energy use, 36-45% of total diet-related biodiversity loss, up to a third of total diet-related greenhouse gas emissions, land use and food waste, and up to a quarter of total diet-related water-use by adults in a number of high-income countries [114]. UPF production relies on commodity ingredients like palm oil, soya, wheat, maize, milk, eggs, and meat, which have driven ecosystem destruction (e.g., rainforest clearing for palm oil in Indonesia, Cerrado conversion for soya in Brazil). The authors conclude that UPFs are "potentially counterproductive" to sustainable food system objectives and call on policymakers to regulate UPF production, availability, and consumption [114].

In wealthy countries, UPFs can be responsible for up to one-third of all the climate emissions that come from what people eat [111]. In Belgium, UPFs drive ~50% of dietary climate and land impacts; in the Netherlands, 45% of food-related emissions; in France, ~25% of dietary climate, energy, water, and land impacts [111].

## 5. Conclusions and Recommendations

The evidence synthesized in this report demonstrates that ultra-processed foods represent a profound threat to human health through multiple interconnected biological, behavioral, and environmental pathways. The convergence of findings across diverse research traditions—from molecular neuroscience to public health epidemiology to political economy—reveals a system that systematically undermines health at every level.

### 5.1 Key Findings

1. **Biological mechanisms**: UPFs disrupt gut-brain axis signaling, alter gut microbiome composition, interfere with endocrine function through food contact chemicals, promote neuroinflammation via AGEs and inflammatory cascades, and dysregulate reward pathways in ways that parallel substance use disorders. These mechanisms operate synergistically, creating cascading effects that extend beyond simple nutrient composition.

2. **Industry influence**: UPFs are deliberately engineered to maximize consumption through hyper-palatability, vanishing caloric density, optimized mouthfeel, and precise bliss point formulation. Marketing strategies target vulnerable populations—particularly children and communities of color—with sophisticated, data-driven campaigns that exploit psychological vulnerabilities and create lifelong consumption patterns.

3. **Individual susceptibility**: Genetic variants in dopamine receptors (DRD2, DRD4), opioid receptors (OPRM1), melanocortin signaling (MC4R), and taste receptors (TAS2R38, CD36) create differential susceptibility to UPF addiction and metabolic dysfunction. Early-life exposures through the DOHaD mechanism program lifelong vulnerability, while psychological factors (impulsivity, emotional eating, hedonic hunger) moderate the relationship between food environment and consumption.

4. **Environmental and structural factors**: Food deserts, food swamps, economic inequality, targeted marketing, and built environment characteristics create structural barriers to healthy eating that cannot be overcome by individual choice alone. The current policy landscape—including agricultural subsidies that favor commodity crops, trade liberalization that facilitates UPF importation, and insufficient regulatory frameworks—systematically reinforces the dominance of ultra-processed foods in the global food supply.

### 5.2 Policy Recommendations

Based on the evidence reviewed, the following policy approaches are recommended:

1. **Mandatory front-of-package warning labels**: Modeled on Chile's successful system, with black octagonal warning labels for products high in sugar, salt, saturated fat, and calories, accompanied by marketing restrictions and school sales bans.

2. **Fiscal policies**: Sugar-sweetened beverage taxes, combined with subsidies for minimally processed foods, to shift price incentives toward healthier options. Evidence from Mexico, the UK, South Africa, and Berkeley demonstrates that such policies reduce consumption and generate revenue for public health investments.

3. **Marketing restrictions**: Comprehensive limitations on marketing of UPFs to children across all media platforms, including digital marketing, influencer promotions, and sports sponsorships. The UK's HFSS regulations provide a model for restricting both placement and promotion.

4. **School food reforms**: Increased funding for school nutrition programs to support scratch cooking, reduced reliance on UPFs, and implementation of nutrition standards that limit added sugars, sodium, and ultra-processed ingredients.

5. **Agricultural policy reform**: Restructuring of farm subsidies away from commodity crops (corn, soy, wheat) that serve as UPF ingredients toward support for fruits, vegetables, and sustainably produced whole foods.

6. **Trade policy**: Integration of nutrition goals into trade agreements, including mechanisms to protect domestic food environments from the influx of ultra-processed imports.

7. **Regulation of food contact chemicals**: Stronger oversight of the thousands of chemicals that migrate from packaging into food, including bisphenols, phthalates, and PFAS, with particular attention to vulnerable developmental windows.

8. **Clinical integration**: Recognition of ultra-processed food addiction as a clinical entity, with development of screening tools (YFAS), treatment protocols, and integration into medical education and practice.

### 5.3 Research Priorities

Future research should prioritize:
- Long-term interventional studies to establish causality between UPF consumption and health outcomes
- Investigation of the "cocktail effect" of combined additive exposures
- Development of biomarkers for early detection of UPF-related metabolic dysfunction
- Studies on the effectiveness of GLP-1 agonists in addressing UPF addiction
- Research on transgenerational epigenetic effects of UPF exposure
- Evaluation of natural experiments as countries implement varying policy approaches

The global burden of diet-related disease demands urgent action. The evidence is clear: ultra-processed foods are not merely a matter of poor nutritional choices but represent a systemic failure of food systems, regulatory frameworks, and public health policy. Addressing this crisis requires coordinated action across all levels—from clinical practice to global governance—guided by the principle that the right to healthy food is a fundamental human right.
