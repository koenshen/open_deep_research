# Comprehensive Evidence-Based Review: Chronic Exercise-Associated Gastrointestinal Distress in Masters (40+) Endurance Runners

## Introduction and Scope

Exercise-associated gastrointestinal symptoms (Ex-GIS) and the broader syndrome of exercise-induced gastrointestinal syndrome (EIGS) are among the most common medical complaints in endurance sport. Depending on the study, **30–90% of endurance athletes report GI symptoms during exercise**, with prevalence rising to **≥93% in ultra-endurance events** and 96% in one 161 km ultramarathon study [1][2]. For a masters runner (40+) who is both recreational and competitive, chronic stomach pain and GI distress during runs from 10K to ultramarathon distances are not only performance-limiting but may indicate underlying pathophysiology requiring targeted diagnosis and management.

This review synthesizes the current evidence base across five domains: (1) the most common causes of chronic exercise-associated GI distress in masters endurance runners; (2) how similar cases have been characterized in the sports-medicine literature and official guidelines; (3) the efficacy of medical and behavior-based treatments; (4) the most reliable prevention strategies for fueling, hydration, and training; and (5) evidence-quality weighting and gaps in the literature. Where age-specific (40+) data are absent—which is common—findings are extrapolated from the broader endurance-athlete literature and clearly noted as such.

## Part 1: Most Common Causes and Pathophysiology

### 1.1 Splanchnic Hypoperfusion and Gastrointestinal Ischemia

The **circulatory–gastrointestinal pathway** is the most extensively documented mechanism of exercise-associated GI distress. During strenuous exercise, sympathetic activation causes profound splanchnic vasoconstriction, redistributing blood flow to working muscle, heart, lungs, and skin. **Splanchnic blood flow can be reduced by up to 80%** during maximal exercise [1][3][4]. At 70% VO₂max, gut blood flow drops by nearly 80%, which can cause nausea, cramping, and diarrhea [27][37].

The landmark experimental study by **van Wijck et al. (2011)** in *PLOS ONE* demonstrated that 60 minutes of cycling at 70% of maximum workload in healthy young men caused rapid splanchnic hypoperfusion (gastric tonometry gap increased from −0.85 to +0.85 kPa within the first 10 minutes), quantifiable small intestinal injury (plasma I-FABP increased from 309 to 615 pg/mL; I-BABP from 5.06 to 14.3 ng/mL), and increased small intestinal permeability [3][9]. Importantly, hypoperfusion correlated significantly with intestinal damage (r = 0.59, p < 0.001), and the authors concluded that "the healthy human small intestine can withstand transient hypoperfusion," but that prolonged or repeated episodes may cause clinically significant symptoms [3][9].

For masters runners, this mechanism is particularly relevant because aging is associated with reduced cardiovascular reserve, reduced maximal cardiac output, and less efficient thermoregulatory blood flow, all of which may amplify the splanchnic hypoperfusion response to a given absolute workload [22][23][25][26]. However, direct age-stratified data in athletes over 40 are lacking.

### 1.2 Motility Disturbances and Delayed Gastric Emptying

The **neuroendocrine–gastrointestinal pathway** involves sympathetic activation and stress-hormone responses that impair GI motility, transit, digestion, and absorption [1][2]. Exercise above ~60–70% VO₂max slows gastric emptying, and heavy exercise decreases peristalsis and lower esophageal sphincter tone, increasing reflux risk [4][11]. Exercise stress of **≥2 hours at ≥60% VO₂max** is considered the threshold for significant GI perturbations [1].

In the **GastroAxEx case series (Gaskell et al., 2021)**, nine endurance athletes with EIGS underwent individualized laboratory exercise simulations. Breath-hydrogen testing revealed that **5 of 9 athletes had delayed orocecal transit time (90–150 min post-exercise), and 4 of 9 showed no H₂ turning point by 180 minutes**, suggestive of exercise-associated gastroparesis or paralytic ileus [5]. A subsequent 2025 case series by Scrivin et al. found slow or delayed orocecal transit in 2 of 9 athletes [7].

Delayed gastric emptying and slowed small-intestinal transit impair delivery of fluid and carbohydrate to the absorptive surface, exacerbate bloating and nausea, and can create a self-perpetuating cycle of feeding intolerance during long runs [1][5][11].

### 1.3 Mechanical Factors: Jostling, Posture, and Intra-Abdominal Pressure

Repetitive impact and mechanical jostling during running cause trauma to the gut, which is why **runners experience more lower-GI symptoms than cyclists** [1][4]. Long-distance running is predominantly associated with lower-GI symptoms (prevalence up to 71–79%), while cycling is associated with both upper and lower symptoms [4][38].

Mechanical factors include:
- **Bouncing impact** of running causing intestinal trauma and occasional bleeding [1][4].
- **Posture changes** (e.g., aero position in cycling, forward-leaning running posture) increasing intra-abdominal pressure [1][4].
- **Aerophagia** (swallowing air) during high respiratory rates and drinking from bottles [1][4].
- **Increased intra-abdominal pressure** from high-intensity effort, which has been shown in resistance exercise to correlate with GI damage magnitude [27].

For masters runners, age-related loss of core stability and changes in running economy may subtly alter biomechanics and abdominal pressure dynamics, but this has not been directly studied.

### 1.4 Food Intolerances and Malabsorption (FODMAPs, Lactose, Fructose, Gluten)

Food intolerances and malabsorption are among the most modifiable contributors to chronic GI distress in endurance athletes.

**FODMAPs (Fermentable Oligosaccharides, Disaccharides, Monosaccharides, and Polyols)** are short-chain carbohydrates that are poorly absorbed in the small intestine and rapidly fermented by colonic bacteria, producing gas, bloating, and altered motility [21][22]. Survey data from 910 athletes showed that **55% actively avoided at least one high-FODMAP food, 93% reported that food elicited GI symptoms, and 86.5% reported improvement through avoidance** [23]. A seminal intervention study (Lis et al., 2017) found that **82% of runners (9 of 11) with persistent exercise-associated GIS experienced significantly lower daily symptoms during low-FODMAP vs. high-FODMAP periods** [37]. The systematic review by Mlinaric & Mohorko (2025) confirmed significant reductions in gut discomfort, flatulence, urge to defecate, loose stool, and diarrhea with low-FODMAP diets, with **69% of participants reporting positive effects on low-FODMAP vs. 25% on high-FODMAP** [12].

**Lactose intolerance** is the highest single FODMAP trigger (86.5% in one survey), followed by galacto-oligosaccharides (23.9%), fructose (23.0%), fructans (6.2%), and polyols (5.4%) [23]. **Lactase levels decrease with age**, making lactose intolerance more common in older adults—a particularly relevant consideration for masters runners [24].

**Fructose malabsorption** is common; fructose absorption via GLUT5 is rate-limited (~1 g/min), and high-fructose foods or fructose-only drinks are poorly tolerated during exercise [4][11]. However, **fructose combined with glucose is better tolerated** because glucose and fructose use different intestinal transporters (SGLT1 and GLUT5), and co-ingestion enhances total carbohydrate absorption and reduces GI distress [2][4][42].

**Celiac disease and gluten sensitivity**: A gluten-free diet in athletes without celiac disease has **not** been shown to improve GI symptoms, performance, or well-being in randomized crossover trials [37][47]. However, **celiac disease may be underdiagnosed in athletes**—Leone et al. (2020) found NCAA athletes were 3.85 times more likely to be diagnosed with celiac disease and 18.36 times more likely to report high celiac-symptom scores than the general population [36]. For masters runners with chronic unexplained GI symptoms, celiac serology (tissue transglutaminase IgA) is a reasonable screening test [49]. Importantly, fructans (a FODMAP) trigger IBS-like symptoms in twice as many individuals with self-reported non-celiac gluten sensitivity as gluten itself, indicating that FODMAP reduction—not gluten avoidance—is the key modulator [37].

### 1.5 NSAID Use

**NSAID use is a major, frequently overlooked cause of chronic GI distress in endurance runners.** The landmark crossover study by **van Wijck et al. (2012)** in *Medicine & Science in Sports & Exercise* demonstrated that **ibuprofen (400 mg twice before cycling) aggravated exercise-induced small intestinal injury and induced gut barrier dysfunction** in healthy trained men. Peak I-FABP levels were 875 pg/mL with cycling + ibuprofen vs. 474 pg/mL with cycling alone and 352 pg/mL at rest (p < 0.002). Small intestinal permeability (lactulose/rhamnose ratio) was 0.08 vs. 0.04 with cycling alone. The authors concluded: "NSAID consumption by athletes is not harmless and should be discouraged" [8][57][58].

Prevalence data are concerning: a UK survey of **806 Parkrun participants (mean age 48)** found that **88% had used NSAIDs in the past 12 months**, 57% before a run, 11% during, and 67.7% after running; one-third experienced suspected adverse reactions including GI discomfort, heartburn, nausea, vomiting, diarrhea, and GI bleeding [47]. NSAID use is associated with a **3–5-fold increased risk of upper GI complications** [4][52]. A 2024 scoping review of NSAIDs in marathon/ultraendurance running found potential links to acute kidney injury and electrolyte disturbances, and although clear GI correlations were not established, the authors called for larger, higher-quality trials [15][46].

For masters runners over 40, NSAID risk is compounded by age-related declines in gastric mucosal resistance and renal function, and NSAIDs are a known risk factor for peptic ulcer disease in older adults [22][24][33][34].

### 1.6 Dehydration and Hyperthermia

**Dehydration** reduces splanchnic blood flow, slows gastric emptying, and exacerbates exercise-induced GI damage [2][3][11]. In the Scrivin et al. (2025) case series, most athletes (6 of 9) consumed <400 mL/hr of fluid ad libitum, resulting in **2–4% body mass loss**, and 8 of 9 consumed <40 g/hr of exogenous carbohydrate during exercise [7]. Hypohydration of ~3.1% body weight is among the most influential factors increasing GI damage [39][62].

**Hyperthermia** is directly linked to intestinal permeability. Core temperatures exceeding **39°C are universally associated with augmented GI permeability** (Pires et al., 2017; Snipe et al., 2018) [7][24][59]. A Monash University study (2023) identified core temperature as a major risk factor for gut issues during exercise in hot weather, with a rise to 39°C predicting gut damage and pathogen translocation [60]. Costa et al. (2019) found intestinal injury (I-FABP >1000 pg/mL) at ambient temperatures ≥35°C, and total-GIS incidence rose from ≤70% in temperate conditions to ≥88% in hot conditions (≥35°C) [61].

For masters athletes, **thermoregulatory capacity declines with age**: reduced sweat rate per gland, impaired heat-induced skin vasodilation, lower cardiac output, and reduced thirst perception [25][26][52]. This makes masters runners more vulnerable to hyperthermia-induced GI damage during long runs in warm conditions, although fit older athletes can maintain adequate thermoregulation via training adaptations [26][53][56].

### 1.7 Age-Related Changes in GI Physiology Relevant to Runners Over 40

Direct data on GI function in masters runners (40+) are extremely limited, but age-related GI changes in the general older adult population are well documented [22][23][24]:

- **Stomach**: Reduced lining resistance increases peptic ulcer risk (especially with NSAIDs); decreased elasticity slows emptying; acid secretion largely unchanged but **atrophic gastritis** becomes more common, increasing risk of vitamin B12 deficiency and **small intestinal bacterial overgrowth (SIBO)** [24][33][34].
- **Small intestine**: Structure and nutrient absorption largely preserved; **lactase levels decline**; **SIBO prevalence increases** (15.6% in elderly vs. 5.9% in younger) [24][34].
- **Large intestine**: Transit slows, increasing constipation risk [24].
- **Gut microbiome**: Composition shifts with age, influenced by diet and exercise; moderate exercise promotes a healthier microbiome, while intense prolonged exercise can increase permeability and systemic inflammation [29][30][68].
- **Mucosal blood flow**: Reduced gastric blood flow and impaired mucosal repair mechanisms occur with aging, potentially lowering the threshold for exercise-induced ischemic injury [33].
- **Cardiovascular aging**: Reduced maximal heart rate and cardiac output with age may amplify splanchnic hypoperfusion during exercise [55].

It is critical to note that **most of these data come from adults over 65**, not specifically masters athletes aged 40–65. The Merck Manual notes that aging has less effect on digestive function than on other organ systems due to reserve capacity [24]. Importantly, **the small intestine's ability to absorb carbohydrates stays largely intact with age**—the SGLT1 (glucose) and GLUT5 (fructose) transporters remain functional in healthy older adults, and masters athletes can achieve the same fueling targets as younger athletes with appropriate gut training [27][37].

## Part 2: How Similar Cases Have Been Described in the Literature

### 2.1 The EIGS Conceptual Framework

The modern literature describes exercise-associated GI disturbances under the umbrella term **"exercise-induced gastrointestinal syndrome (EIGS)"**, which encompasses both the pathophysiological perturbations and the clinical symptoms (Ex-GIS) [2][43]. The **Sports Dietitians Australia (SDA) and Ultra Sports Science Foundation (USSF) Joint Position Statement (2025)**, published in *Sports Medicine*, defines two primary causal pathways [2][30]:

1. **Circulatory–gastrointestinal pathway**: Splanchnic hypoperfusion → intestinal ischemia → epithelial injury → increased permeability → bacterial/endotoxin translocation → systemic inflammation.
2. **Neuroendocrine–gastrointestinal pathway**: Sympathetic activation → altered gut peptides (CCK, GLP-1, PYY, ghrelin) and catecholamines → impaired motility, transit, digestion, and absorption.

Two additional pathways—mechanical strain (jostling) and exaggerated metabolic responses (acidosis, hypoglycemia)—warrant further investigation [2][30][43].

### 2.2 Case Series and Clinical Descriptions

**GastroAxEx Case Series (Gaskell, Rauch & Costa, 2021, *Frontiers in Physiology*)**: Nine endurance athletes with EIGS and two controls underwent a four-phase protocol (clinical assessment, tailored laboratory exercise simulation, individualized therapeutic intervention, and field-based monitoring). Most common symptoms were nausea and vomiting (8/9), negatively affecting feeding tolerance; all cases (9/9) reduced event workload and 5/9 withdrew from competition. Key findings included elevated cortisol in 6/7, significant enterocyte injury (I-FABP Δ>1000 pg/mL) in 2/8, delayed orocecal transit in 5/9, and severe thermoregulatory strain (core >40°C) in 2 athletes. All 9 athletes were identified as having the **neuroendocrine–gastrointestinal pathway** as the proposed causal factor. After individualized intervention (lead-in diet, gut training, hydration, thermoregulation, and ondansetron in 7/9), **8/9 reported improved symptoms** and only 1/9 withdrew from competition (vs. 8/9 previously) [5].

**Scrivin, Slater & Costa (2025, *International Journal of Sport Nutrition and Exercise Metabolism*)**: A four-phase management approach using a **48-hour high-carbohydrate low-FODMAP diet** in 9 recreational-to-elite endurance athletes with recurrent severe Ex-GIS. Eight of 9 athletes successfully implemented the diet, which improved intake tolerance, reduced incidence/severity of Ex-GIS, and improved perceived work output during targeted events. The authors concluded that a "48-hr high carbohydrate low FODMAP before endurance exercise appears to be a valuable addition to suggested therapeutic interventions used to mitigate Ex-GIS" [7].

**Peters et al. (1999, *American Journal of Gastroenterology*)**: A foundational study of 2,076 long-distance runners, cyclists, and triathletes found that 45% had at least one GI complaint, 11% reported serious complaints, and 2.7% had complaints in the first 24 hours post-run. Runners experienced more lower (71%) than upper (36%) GI symptoms. Exercise-related GI symptoms were significantly related to GI symptoms during nonexercise periods, age, gender, diet, and years of training [4][38].

**Endoscopy and bleeding studies**: Endoscopy after maximal distance runs (18–50 km) has shown pathological features in all runners, from vascular congestion to hemorrhage [26]. In the 1986 Comrades Marathon (89.4 km), **81% of 89 exhausted runners were endotoxemic**, significantly correlated with nausea, vomiting, and diarrhea [26]. Schwartz et al. (1990) studied 41 Chicago Marathon runners; 9 experienced GI bleeding with gastric antral erosions, and **60% of the bleeding group used NSAIDs** [26].

### 2.3 Official Guidelines and Position Statements

Several authoritative bodies have published guidelines or position statements relevant to exercise-associated GI distress:

1. **SDA/USSF Joint Position Statement (2025, *Sports Medicine*)**: This is the most comprehensive and current guidance. It grades evidence and reports that macronutrient intake before/during exercise, maintenance of euhydration, dietary FODMAP manipulation, and gut training/feeding tolerance adjustments have **consistent beneficial outcomes**; heat-mitigating strategies and certain supplements (prebiotics, phenols) are **promising but need further exploration**; and low-carbohydrate high-fat diets, probiotic supplementation, and pharmaceutical administration have **negative or unclear outcomes**. The core recommendation is individualized GI assessment during exercise to identify causal factors, with abstention from strategies with unclear or adverse outcomes [2][30][43].

2. **Academy of Nutrition and Dietetics, Dietitians of Canada, and ACSM Position: "Nutrition and Athletic Performance" (2016)**: Recommends 1–4 g/kg carbohydrate in the 1–4 hours before exercise, up to 90 g/h of multiple transportable carbohydrates for exercise >2.5 hours, and individualized hydration to avoid >2–3% body mass loss [29][32].

3. **ISSN Position Stand: Nutrient Timing (2017)**: Recommends ~30–60 g/h of carbohydrate in a 6–8% carbohydrate-electrolyte solution every 10–15 min for extended (>60 min) high-intensity exercise; notes most carbohydrate recommendations are based on male endurance athletes [30].

4. **ACSM Position Stands**: Developed using an evidence-based protocol (PICOT questions, systematic reviews, risk-of-bias evaluation, evidence grading). The 2007 ACSM position stand "Exercise and Fluid Replacement" and subsequent updates recommend avoiding >2% body mass loss and individualizing fluid intake [17][64].

5. **North American Consensus on Breath Testing (2017)**: Establishes criteria for SIBO/IMO diagnosis via hydrogen/methane breath testing (hydrogen rise ≥20 ppm above baseline by 90 minutes; methane ≥10 ppm at any time) [19][20].

6. **German Nutrition Society (DGE) Position on Carbohydrates in Sports Nutrition (2020)**: Recommends 1–4 g/kg body weight 2–3 hours before competition; 6–12 g/kg/day for prolonged endurance; combining glucose, fructose, and sucrose to increase oxidation to 1.5–1.7 g/min [34].

### 2.4 Prevalence and Symptom Patterns in Masters Runners

Limited age-stratified data exist. The Ribichini et al. (2023) review notes that **younger age is a risk factor for Ex-GIS**—that is, older athletes may be somewhat protected, although the effect is modest [1]. The Scrivin et al. (2022) survey of 137 endurance athletes with Ex-GIS had a mean age of 41.6 ± 11.1 years, making it directly relevant to masters runners; 11% reported a diagnosed GI disorder (most commonly IBS) and 13% used medications (e.g., omeprazole, Buscopan, Gaviscon) [28].

The **VIREF study** of master athletes found the most common GI symptoms during racing were gas (51%), stomach pain/cramp (43%), intestinal problems/pain (42%), reflux/heartburn (39%), and nausea/vomiting (39%); increasing race distance and performance level increased food restriction and GI symptom incidence [37].

## Part 3: Treatment Efficacy

### 3.1 Pharmacological Interventions

#### Proton Pump Inhibitors (PPIs)

The strongest evidence for pharmacological treatment in athletes comes from a **randomized, double-blinded, placebo-controlled trial by Thalmann et al. (2006, *British Journal of Sports Medicine*)** which found that a **3-day prophylactic regimen of oral pantoprazole decreased the incidence of gastrointestinal bleeding in ultramarathon runners** [16][17]. This supports short-course PPI use for athletes with a history of exercise-associated GI bleeding or erosive gastritis.

For GERD/heartburn in athletes, H2-receptor antagonists (e.g., ranitidine 300 mg 1 hour before activity) can reduce exercise-induced reflux markers, and a 2-week PPI trial (daily 30 minutes before breakfast) is considered first-line [6]. However, **chronic PPI use carries risks**, including increased SIBO risk, C. difficile infection, and altered gut microbiota [7][19]. PPIs should be used judiciously and not as a long-term solution without medical supervision.

**Evidence quality**: Moderate (single RCT for bleeding prevention; expert consensus for GERD management).

#### Ondansetron (Antiemetic)

The **TOO FUN Study (Pasternak et al., 2021, *Sports*)** was the first randomized controlled trial of ondansetron (4 mg oral capsules) for ultramarathon-associated nausea/vomiting in 62 runners (average age 45, range 21–78). **Results were negative**: there were no statistically significant differences between ondansetron and placebo in improvement (30% vs. 55%, p = 0.26), and the placebo group tended to show more benefit. The authors concluded: "We found no evidence that ondansetron capsules improve nausea and vomiting during ultramarathons" [13][31][32][33]. Limitations included oral capsule absorption (vs. orally dissolving tablets) and the 4 mg dose (vs. 8 mg commonly used clinically).

**Evidence quality**: High (RCT), but negative—ondansetron is not supported for routine use.

#### Loperamide (Antidiarrheal)

Loperamide (Imodium) is the most commonly discussed antidiarrheal for runners. The IFFGD notes it "has been shown to decrease stool frequency when taken before intense exercise," but cautions that antimotility drugs can inhibit perspiration and impair thermoregulation [9]. **Expert opinion (Dr. Stacy Sims, Triathlete.com, 2022) advises against loperamide before racing, especially in warm conditions**, due to risks of QT prolongation (heart rhythm disruption) with dehydration, reduced nutrient absorption, and interference with fluid/electrolyte balance [12]. Other sources support a single dose about an hour before a major race as a last resort, unless bloody diarrhea is present [15].

**Evidence quality**: Low (no athlete-specific RCTs; expert opinion and consensus).

#### Antispasmodics (Buscopan/Hyoscine Butylbromide)

Buscopan (hyoscine butylbromide) is an antimuscarinic antispasmodic used for relief of lower abdominal pain caused by GI spasm, including IBS. It is available over-the-counter in many countries and works within 15 minutes; maximum OTC duration is 2 weeks [35][36][37]. In the Scrivin et al. (2022) survey, Buscopan was among the medications used by athletes (9% overall medication use) [28]. **No athlete-specific randomized trials exist**, so evidence is extrapolated from IBS literature.

**Evidence quality**: Low (extrapolated from IBS; no athlete-specific trials).

#### Prokinetic Agents

Prokinetics (e.g., metoclopramide) are used for functional heartburn and gastroparesis but **have no athlete-specific evidence** for exercise-associated delayed gastric emptying [6][34]. They are sometimes used off-label but should be medically supervised.

#### NSAID Cessation

The most evidence-based "pharmacological" intervention is **discontinuation of NSAIDs**. The van Wijck et al. (2012) study is unambiguous: "NSAID consumption by athletes is not harmless and should be discouraged" [8]. A 2025 review in the *Journal of Science and Medicine in Sport* emphasizes that NSAIDs are a major cause of adverse drug reactions, increase GI bleeding risk 3–5-fold, and are often used without medical indication [16]. For masters runners over 40, the risk–benefit calculus strongly favors avoiding routine NSAID use around training and racing [15][16][47].

**Evidence quality**: High (experimental crossover study; consensus).

### 3.2 Dietary and Behavioral Therapies

#### Low-FODMAP Diet

The low-FODMAP diet is the **best-supported dietary intervention for Ex-GIS**. Evidence includes:
- Lis et al. (2017): 82% of runners (9/11) with persistent GIS had significantly lower symptoms on low-FODMAP [37].
- Mlinaric & Mohorko (2025) systematic review: 69% positive response on low-FODMAP vs. 25% on high-FODMAP [12].
- Scrivin et al. (2025) case series: 48-hour high-carbohydrate low-FODMAP diet before endurance exercise improved intake tolerance and reduced Ex-GIS [7].
- Survey data: 82.6% of athletes eliminating at least one FODMAP group reported improvement [14][23].

The low-FODMAP diet is not intended for long-term use; a **strict elimination phase of 2–6 weeks, followed by systematic reintroduction and personalization** is recommended [22][23][37]. Risks of long-term restriction include reduced SCFA-producing gut bacteria, inadequate nutrient intake, and increased risk of disordered eating [23][47]. A practical approach for athletes is **2–3 days of low-FODMAP eating before key sessions or races** [23][47].

**Evidence quality**: Moderate-to-high (systematic reviews, RCTs in IBS, multiple athlete intervention studies).

#### Gluten-Free Diet (Without Celiac Disease)

A gluten-free diet in non-celiac athletes **does not improve GI symptoms, performance, or well-being**, based on randomized crossover trials [37][47][51]. However, in athletes with confirmed celiac disease, a strict gluten-free diet is essential [49].

**Evidence quality**: High (RCTs), but negative for non-celiac athletes.

#### Probiotics and Prebiotics

The SDA/USSF position statement **lists probiotic supplementation under "negative outcomes reported"** [2][30]. A systematic review (Gaskell et al., 2026) of 18 studies found **no consistent performance benefits from microbiota-targeted interventions** [40]. However, some evidence suggests specific probiotics (e.g., LGG) may shorten GI symptom episode duration by 33–57%, and a 2022 meta-analysis found probiotics may improve performance in aerobic-trained populations (with likely publication bias) [12][39]. The evidence is mixed, and no strong recommendation can be made.

**Evidence quality**: Low-to-moderate; mixed results; position statement advises against routine use.

#### Cognitive-Behavioral Therapy (CBT) and Gut-Brain Therapies

For athletes with chronic GI symptoms that overlap with IBS (which is underdiagnosed in athletes—9.8% prevalence with only 2.8% diagnosed), **gut-brain behavioral therapies have strong evidence** from the general IBS literature:
- At least 20 published RCTs support CBT for IBS, with durable effects ≥1 year [18][24].
- A network meta-analysis of 67 RCTs (n = 7,441) found minimal-contact CBT (RR 0.55), standard CBT (RR 0.65), and gut-directed hypnotherapy (RR 0.79) significantly improve global IBS symptoms [23].
- A landmark 7-site RCT (n = 436) found **61% of patients in 4-session home-based CBT reported moderate-to-substantial improvement**, with 58% maintaining gains at 6 months [39].
- The American Gastroenterological Association and American College of Gastroenterology recommend referring IBS patients to CBT or other gut-brain behavioral therapy [18][39].

While no athlete-specific CBT trials exist, psychological stress is a recognized factor in Ex-GIS [2][12], and pre-race anxiety is a common trigger [12]. Referral to a psychologist specializing in gut-brain therapies is a reasonable, evidence-informed adjunct for masters runners with chronic, stress-exacerbated GI symptoms.

**Evidence quality**: High for IBS (extrapolated to athletes); no athlete-specific trials.

### 3.3 Diagnostic-Driven Management

For masters runners with **chronic or severe** GI symptoms, the literature supports a structured diagnostic approach:

#### Endoscopy (Upper and Lower)

Papantoniou et al. (2023, *Annals of Gastroenterology*) recommend that **GI bleeding should NOT be immediately attributed to endurance exercise**; upper and lower endoscopy should be performed to rule out IBD, neoplasia, peptic ulcers, Mallory-Weiss tears, angiodysplasias, hemorrhoids, fissures, and other causes [17]. For occult bleeding (positive fecal occult blood, iron-deficiency anemia), endoscopy is recommended in all athletes, with iron supplementation for low ferritin and PPIs/H2-receptor antagonists for exercise-induced gastritis [17].

#### Capsule Endoscopy

For small bowel bleeding (accounting for ~5% of GI bleeding) after negative bidirectional endoscopy, **capsule endoscopy is the diagnostic test of choice**, with a diagnostic yield of ~60% [20][21][40]. Guidelines recommend capsule endoscopy within 48 hours of overt bleeding for maximal yield (81–92%) [20][40]. Angiodysplasias are the most common finding (30–50%), **typically in older patients**—highly relevant for masters runners [20][40].

#### SIBO Breath Testing

Small intestinal bacterial overgrowth (SIBO) is more common with age and with chronic PPI use [7][33][34]. Symptoms overlap significantly with Ex-GIS: bloating, gas, diarrhea, abdominal pain [21]. The North American Consensus criteria are: **hydrogen rise ≥20 ppm above baseline by 90 minutes (SIBO) or methane ≥10 ppm at any time (IMO)** [19][44]. Breath testing can guide targeted antibiotic therapy (e.g., rifaximin for hydrogen-predominant SIBO; rifaximin + neomycin for methane-predominant IMO, which achieves 85% clinical response vs. 56% with rifaximin alone) [19][44]. For masters runners with chronic bloating and diarrhea that does not respond to dietary manipulation, SIBO breath testing is a reasonable diagnostic step.

#### GastroAxEx Protocol

The **GastroAxEx (gastrointestinal assessment during exercise)** protocol is a standardized laboratory assessment used in case series to identify individual causal factors and guide personalized intervention [5][7]. It involves a clinical assessment, tailored exercise simulation (typically 2 hours at 60% VO₂max with controlled feeding and hydration), measurement of biomarkers (I-FABP, cortisol, endotoxin), breath-H₂ testing for orocecal transit, and symptom tracking. This approach is emerging as a gold-standard clinical tool for complex Ex-GIS cases [5][7][30].

## Part 4: Prevention Strategies

### 4.1 Nutrition Timing (Pre-Run Meal)

The consensus recommendation is a **main meal 3–4 hours before exercise**, with 1–4 g/kg of carbohydrate, low in fat and fiber [29][50][53]. If only 1–2 hours are available, choose 1–2 g/kg of mostly simple carbohydrates [50][51]. Specific guidance:

- **4 hours out**: Mixed meal with fat, protein, carbohydrate (including some fiber).
- **1–2 hours out**: Low-fiber, mostly simple carbohydrates (white rice, bagels, banana).
- **15–30 minutes before**: Gels or readily available simple carbs; avoid fructose alone [50].
- **Night before a long run**: 7–12 g/kg carbohydrate in the 24 hours prior (roughly 490–840 g for a 70 kg runner); the plate should be 60–70% carbohydrate, 15–20% protein, minimal fat and fiber [54].
- **Avoid**: high-fiber foods (cruciferous vegetables, whole grains) 1–3 days out; dairy if lactose intolerant; high-fructose foods; sugar alcohols (sorbitol, xylitol); greasy/fried foods [2][15][21].
- **Glycogen loading**: 10–12 g/kg/day for 36–48 hours prior to events >90 minutes [34].

**Masters-specific note**: Age-related decline in taste and smell can affect food choices, and reduced thirst perception increases dehydration risk [36]. A structured pre-run nutrition plan, written down and practiced, is especially important.

### 4.2 Carbohydrate Type and Concentration During Runs

The evidence strongly supports using **multiple transportable carbohydrates (glucose + fructose)** at recommended rates:

- **Absorption physiology**: Glucose is absorbed via SGLT1 (saturates at ~60 g/h); fructose via GLUT5. Using both transporters simultaneously increases total absorptive capacity to **75–90 g/h** [11][41].
- **Recommended intakes**: 60 g/h for exercise up to 2 hours; **up to 90 g/h for exercise >2 hours** using a 2:1 glucose-to-fructose (or maltodextrin-to-fructose) ratio [11][41]. For well-trained athletes, 90 g/h of glucose-fructose mixtures is recommended for prolonged (>2.5 h) moderate-to-high-intensity exercise [12][41].
- **Higher intakes (120 g/h)**: Some elite athletes achieve 120 g/h with personalized gut training (up to 90 g/h at least 2 days weekly for 4 weeks prior), which limited post-race muscle damage without GI distress in one study [11][63]. However, a 2025 review states: "Existing research does not directly support performance-enhancing effects of ingesting carbohydrate at ≥100 g/h versus 60–90 g/h" [55].
- **Concentration and osmolality**: Beverages >500 mOsm/L (e.g., >8–10% carbohydrate drinks) delay gastric emptying and cause GI distress. Sports drinks at **6% concentration are isotonic and empty quickly** [2][11]. Avoid fiber, fat, protein, and concentrated carbohydrate solutions during runs [2][21].
- **Fructose co-ingestion**: Co-ingestion of glucose and fructose can improve endurance performance by ~8–9% vs. glucose alone and accelerates post-exercise liver glycogen repletion [12][42][43]. Peak exogenous carbohydrate oxidation reaches ~1.75 g/min with glucose+fructose mixtures vs. ~1.0 g/min with glucose alone [41][42][43].
- **Product format**: Drink, gel, or chew does not change absorption at a given dose [55]. However, highly concentrated solutions and solid foods are worse tolerated than diluted liquids [2][12].

**Masters-specific note**: The SGLT1 and GLUT5 transporters remain functional with age; masters athletes can target **60–90 g/h** using glucose-fructose blends, and "age isn't the limiting factor—gut training is" [27][37].

### 4.3 Hydration Protocols

- **ACSM guidance**: Athletes should avoid losing >2% of body weight during exercise. Hydration plans must be individualized based on personal sweat rate [64].
- **Pre-exercise**: Drink 5–7 mL/kg of fluid in the 4 hours prior, with modest sodium (3–6 g) [50][54]. Urine should be pale yellow.
- **During exercise**: 0.4–0.8 L/h is a reasonable starting point, customized to sweat rate, heat, and intensity [66]. For events >1 hour or in heat, a sports drink with carbohydrates and electrolytes (sodium 0.5–0.7 g/L) is recommended [15][64].
- **Post-exercise**: 16–24 oz (450–675 mL) fluid per pound (0.5 kg) body weight lost, with sodium to promote retention [15][64].
- **Thirst-driven vs. programmed**: A 2025 narrative review concluded that neither programmed nor thirst-driven fluid intake is universally superior; a **hybrid model integrating individual sweat testing, environmental context, and internal cues** is most practical [65][67]. However, thirst perception declines with age, so masters runners should be more deliberate about fluid intake [36][65].
- **Avoid overhydration**: Hyponatremia (low blood sodium) is a documented risk, especially with high fluid intake and sodium losses. In the 2002 Boston Marathon, hyponatremia was more common than dehydration [54]. Drink to thirst unless sweat losses are high [65][66].
- **Sodium**: 300–800 mg/h of sodium during longer events (up to 1,500 mg/h for heavy salty sweaters) is a reasonable target [55].

**Masters-specific note**: Older athletes have **reduced thirst sensation, reduced kidney concentrating ability, and increased insensible water loss**, putting them at higher dehydration risk [36][52]. One study found older men replaced only 34% of lost fluid vs. 80% in young men after exercise [36].

### 4.4 Gut-Training Adaptations

"Training the gut" is one of the most evidence-based prevention strategies. The GI tract is highly adaptable, and repeated exposure to carbohydrate during exercise improves tolerance and reduces symptoms.

**Mechanisms**:
- Upregulation of **SGLT1 transporters** in response to dietary carbohydrate. Animal studies show 1.9-fold increases in SGLT1 after 2 weeks of high-carbohydrate diet; piglets show changes in 3 days, horses in 1 week [9][13].
- Improved gastric emptying and stomach comfort [9][13].
- Reduced carbohydrate malabsorption (45–54% reduction after 2-week repetitive feeding protocols) [8][57].

**Key studies**:
- **Cox et al. (2010)**: 28 days of high-carbohydrate intake (8.5 g/kg/day) in endurance-trained cyclists improved exogenous carbohydrate oxidation vs. controls (5.3 g/kg/day), attributed to SGLT1 upregulation [9][13].
- **Costa et al. (landmark gut-training study)**: 25 endurance runners completed 10 sessions over 14 days, running 2 hours at 60% VO₂max while consuming 30 g carbohydrate every 20 minutes (90 g/h total), producing **60–63% reductions in GI symptoms and 4.3–5.2% performance improvements** [52].
- **Martinez et al. (2023, *Sports Medicine* systematic review)**: Gut discomfort decreased by an average of 47% with 2-week repetitive carbohydrate feeding; carbohydrate malabsorption reduced 45–54%; but no significant changes in gastric emptying rate or markers of intestinal injury/permeability were found. The authors call for more research using sensitive tools [8][57].

**Practical protocol for masters runners**:
- **Start 8–12 weeks before a key race** during base/build phase [7][55].
- **Begin at 30–40 g/h** during sessions over 90 minutes, using a glucose/fructose product [27][37].
- **Increase by 10–15 g/h every 1–2 weeks** as tolerated, aiming for at least 60 g/h [27][37].
- **Practice at least once weekly** during high-volume workouts that mimic race conditions [27][37].
- **Allow up to 10 weeks** to fully adapt [27][37].
- **Important caveat**: Tolerance does not equal absorption—gut training improves tolerance, but there is no evidence it increases maximum absorption rate [5]. However, it does reduce malabsorption, which is clinically meaningful.

**Masters-specific note**: "Your gut is trainable at any age. With smart, progressive fueling, consistent practice, and heat-specific adjustments, masters athletes can perform strongly well into their later years. Age is context, not a ceiling" [27][37].

### 4.5 Training Intensity, Volume Modifications, and Heat Acclimatization

- **Intensity and duration thresholds**: Exercise stress of ≥2 hours at ≥60% VO₂max is the threshold for significant GI perturbations [1]. High-intensity exercise (≥70% VO₂max) slows gastric emptying [11]. Minimal exertional stress for clinically significant GI disturbance is 2 hours at ≥60% VO₂max in hot conditions (≥35°C), or 3 hours in temperate conditions [44].
- **Modality**: Runners experience more GI symptoms than cyclists due to mechanical jostling [1][4]. This is inherent to running and cannot be eliminated, but can be mitigated with fueling/hydration strategies.
- **Heat acclimatization**: Takes 10–14 days; positive adaptations include increased sweat rate, lower core temperature, reduced heart rate, improved blood flow, and better electrolyte balance [26][27]. Heat acclimatization reduces GI damage risk because core temperature ≥39°C is the key trigger for intestinal permeability [44][60].
- **Pacing**: Adjusting pace to physiological signals (heart rate, perceived effort) rather than target pace is important in heat. Holding marathon pace at 25°C is not the same physiological effort as at 12°C [28].
- **Time of day**: Nocturnal exercise prompts greater GI function disturbance and Ex-GIS than diurnal exercise [30]. Masters runners doing early-morning or late-evening runs should be aware.
- **NSAID avoidance**: NSAIDs are GI irritants; administration prior to exercise markedly increases GI perturbations [30]. The London Marathon now advises runners to avoid NSAIDs within 48 hours of the race [47].

**Masters-specific note**: Reduced cardiovascular cooling and decreased sweating efficiency mean masters athletes should **drop carbohydrate intake by 10–20 g/h until heat-acclimated**, choose liquid calories, eat small amounts every 10–15 minutes, start hydrated and cool, don't rely on thirst, and include sodium in all fluids [27][37].

### 4.6 Summary of Prevention Priorities

Based on the SDA/USSF position statement and supporting evidence, the prevention strategies with **consistent beneficial outcomes** are [2][30]:
1. **Macronutrient intake before and during exercise** (carbohydrate at 60–90 g/h using glucose-fructose blends).
2. **Maintenance of euhydration** (avoid >2% body mass loss; individualized fluid plan).
3. **Dietary FODMAP manipulation** (48-hour high-carb low-FODMAP before key sessions; 2–3 days before races).
4. **Gut training/feeding tolerance adjustments** (progressive carbohydrate exposure over 6–10 weeks).

**Promising strategies needing further exploration**: Heat-mitigating strategies (cooling, heat acclimatization) and certain supplements (prebiotics, phenols) [2][30].

**Strategies with negative outcomes**: Low-carbohydrate high-fat diets, probiotic supplementation, pharmaceutical administration, and feeding intolerances [2][30].

## Part 5: Evidence Quality, Weighting, and Gaps

### 5.1 Evidence-Quality Weighting

This review gives extra weight to recent (last 5–10 years) peer-reviewed research, systematic reviews, meta-analyses, and high-quality RCTs. The highest-quality evidence comes from:

- **Systematic reviews and meta-analyses**: Costa et al. (2017) systematic review of EIGS [43][45]; Martinez et al. (2023) gut-training systematic review [8][57]; Mlinaric & Mohorko (2025) nutritional strategies systematic review [12]; Wallett et al. (2021) heat and GI markers systematic review [25]; Gaskell et al. (2026) microbiota systematic review [40]; Pannone & Abbott (2024) NSAID scoping review [15][46].
- **Randomized controlled trials**: van Wijck et al. (2012) ibuprofen crossover trial [8]; Thalmann et al. (2006) pantoprazole RCT [16][17]; Pasternak et al. (2021) ondansetron RCT (TOO FUN) [13][31]; Lis et al. (2015) gluten-free diet crossover RCT [37].
- **Experimental studies**: van Wijck et al. (2011) splanchnic hypoperfusion study [3][9]; Pugh et al. HIIT study [22].
- **Case series**: Gaskell et al. (2021) GastroAxEx [5]; Scrivin et al. (2025) low-FODMAP case series [7].
- **Position statements**: SDA/USSF (2025) [2][30]; AND/DC/ACSM (2016) [29]; ISSN (2017) [30]; ACSM position stands [17].

### 5.2 Masters-Specific Data: What Is Known and What Is Extrapolated

**Directly studied in masters runners (40+)**:
- The Scrivin et al. (2022) survey of Ex-GIS management strategies (mean age 41.6 ± 11.1) [28].
- The TOO FUN Study ondansetron RCT (average age 45, range 21–78) [13].
- The Parkrun NSAID survey (mean age 48) [47].
- The VIREF study on food restriction and GI symptoms in master athletes [37].
- The Neversecond/Outside Run practical guidance for masters athletes (carbohydrate absorption intact with age; gut trainable at any age) [27][37].
- Aging thermoregulation studies in older athletes [25][26][52][53][54][56].

**Extrapolated from younger athletes (evidence applied with caution)**:
- Splanchnic hypoperfusion mechanisms (van Wijck et al. studied men with mean age ~24) [3][9].
- Gut-training protocols (Martinez et al. systematic review: included studies had participants aged 15–35, 79% male) [8][57].
- Low-FODMAP intervention studies (mostly younger athletes) [12][37].
- Most carbohydrate/hydration recommendations (largely based on male endurance athletes) [30].

**Age-related GI changes extrapolated from general older adult literature (65+, not specifically athletes)**:
- Reduced gastric mucosal resistance, increased SIBO prevalence, reduced lactase, slowed colonic transit [22][23][24][33][34].
- Reduced thirst perception, reduced kidney concentrating ability, impaired thermoregulation [36][52].

### 5.3 Gaps in the Literature and Future Research Directions

1. **Masters-specific randomized trials are absent.** No studies have specifically randomized masters runners (40+) vs. younger runners to GI interventions. This is the single most important gap.

2. **Female athletes are underrepresented.** Many key studies (van Wijck, Pugh, Martinez systematic review) included only male or predominantly male participants, despite GI symptoms being more prevalent in female athletes [22][30].

3. **Gut-training mechanisms are unclear.** The 2023 systematic review found no significant changes in gastric emptying or markers of intestinal injury/permeability with gut training; more sensitive tools are needed to elucidate mechanisms [8][57].

4. **Higher carbohydrate intakes (>90 g/h) require more research.** Whether 120 g/h improves performance over 90 g/h is not established, and individual responses vary widely [55].

5. **NSAID research quality is limited.** The scoping review found only 4 RCTs in 30 studies, with inconsistent NSAID types/doses and confounding factors (heat, hydration, race profile) [15][46].

6. **Hydration strategies in ultra-endurance running need age- and sex-specific data.** The 2025 hydration review explicitly calls for research on "sex-based, age, and experience differences" [65][67].

7. **FODMAP approaches need optimization.** Whether low-FODMAP diets are best applied acutely (48 hours before exercise) or integrated into habitual diets requires further study [7][28].

8. **Gut-brain behavioral therapies have not been tested in athletes.** Despite strong evidence in IBS, no athlete-specific CBT or gut-directed hypnotherapy trials exist [18][24].

9. **Heat adaptation in trained athletes needs investigation.** Whether trained athletes' tolerance to exercise-induced GI disturbance in the heat is an acquired adaptation related to regular training remains to be studied [25].

10. **Methodological quality of Ex-GIS research needs improvement.** The SDA/USSF position statement notes deficiencies in participant screening, dietary control, ambient temperature control, hydration monitoring, biomarker selection, and Ex-GIS assessment tools [30][44].

## Conclusion

Chronic exercise-associated GI distress in masters endurance runners is a multifactorial condition with well-characterized pathophysiology. The most common causes are **splanchnic hypoperfusion/ischemia, motility disturbances, mechanical jostling, FODMAP/lactose intolerances, NSAID use, and dehydration/hyperthermia**, all modulated by age-related changes in GI physiology, thermoregulation, and thirst perception.

The strongest evidence-based management approach combines:
1. **Eliminate NSAIDs** around training and racing.
2. **Implement a 48-hour high-carbohydrate low-FODMAP diet** before key sessions and races.
3. **Train the gut** with progressive carbohydrate feeding (30→60–90 g/h) over 6–10 weeks using glucose-fructose blends.
4. **Maintain euhydration** with an individualized, age-aware fluid plan (avoid >2% body mass loss).
5. **Avoid high-fiber, high-fat, high-fructose, and lactose-containing foods** in the days before and during long runs.
6. **Heat-acclimatize** before racing in warm conditions; reduce carbohydrate intake by 10–20 g/h until acclimated.
7. **Consider diagnostic evaluation** (celiac serology, SIBO breath testing, endoscopy/capsule endoscopy) for chronic or severe symptoms, especially with GI bleeding or iron-deficiency anemia.
8. **Consider gut-brain behavioral therapy** for stress-exacerbated symptoms, extrapolating from strong IBS evidence.

The current literature is robust for the general endurance-athlete population, but **masters-specific randomized trials are urgently needed**. Until then, the prudent approach is to apply the general evidence base with age-related considerations: masters runners can achieve the same carbohydrate absorption and gut-training adaptations as younger athletes, but must pay closer attention to hydration, heat management, NSAID avoidance, and age-related changes in GI mucosal resistance and motility.
