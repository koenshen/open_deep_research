# Systematic Review: Recurrent Lateral Ankle Sprains and Season-to-Season Instability in Recreational and Collegiate Basketball Players (Aged 16–30, All Genders)

## Executive Summary

Recurrent lateral ankle sprains (LAS) with season-to-season instability — clinically framed as chronic ankle instability (CAI) when symptoms ("giving way," recurrent sprains, pain) persist beyond ~12 months — represent one of the most common and undertreated problems in basketball [1][2][26]. This review synthesizes the clinical and biomechanical evidence across five dimensions: (1) biomechanical/neuromuscular risk factors, (2) international literature documentation, (3) comparative treatment outcomes, (4) return-to-play (RTP) criteria and prevention protocols, and (5) 2020–2026 evidence recency.

**Headline findings:**

- **Risk factors:** A history of previous ankle sprain is the dominant, most consistently documented predictor of recurrence (~3.5× increased risk; recurrence rates 12–47% across sports, ~28% in basketball, up to 73–80% in some reports) [2][7][8][26][69]. Peroneal muscle weakness and delayed peroneal reaction, proprioceptive deficits, postural-control impairments, restricted ankle dorsiflexion, cavovarus foot alignment, and mechanical laxity (talar tilt/anterior drawer) are the most implicated biomechanical contributors [6][7][9][10][13][14][15][16].
- **International documentation:** Recurrent sprain and CAI patterns are documented across North America, Europe, Asia, the Middle East, Africa, and Oceania, with basketball-specific cohorts from the NBA [3], Taiwan [17], China, Iran, Cameroon, Greece, and Australia [3][4].
- **Treatment:** Structured rehabilitation (balance/proprioceptive training, neuromuscular training, peroneal strengthening) is the first-line standard, reducing recurrence risk by ~36–64% [8][19][20][22][63]. When 3–6 months of conservative care fails, surgical stabilization — predominantly the modified Broström-Gould (MBG) repair, with arthroscopic and internal-brace variants — yields 83–95% return-to-sport rates at mean 12–16 weeks [29][30][33][34][35][36].
- **Return-to-play criteria:** No standardized RTS paradigm exists for LAS [1][49]; the emerging consensus supports a multidimensional, criteria-based approach (the PAASS framework: Pain, Ankle impairments, Athlete perception, Sensorimotor control, Sport/functional performance) with functional thresholds (e.g., ≥90–95% limb symmetry on hop testing, Y-balance reach thresholds, weight-bearing lunge >9–10 cm, ALR-RSI psychological readiness) [16][50][52][57][59].
- **Prevention:** External ankle supports reduce sprain risk by ~69% (with lace-up braces well supported in basketball) [63][64][66][67]; balance/proprioceptive training reduces recurrence by ~36–64% [8][19][20][73]; neuromuscular warm-up programs (FIFA 11+, SHRed Injuries Basketball) reduce ankle injury rates by 32–36% [70][74][75]; load-management principles (graduated acute:chronic workload, sleep, controlled return volumes) are emerging as important adjuncts [76][77].

## Section 1: Biomechanical and Neuromuscular Risk Factors

### 1.1 History of Previous Ankle Sprain — the Strongest Predictive Factor

The single most consistent finding across the entire evidence base is that **a previous ankle sprain is the dominant risk factor for a subsequent sprain**, on both the ipsilateral and contralateral sides [2][5][7]. The National Athletic Trainers' Association (NATA) position statement identifies **previous ankle sprain history as the only consistent risk factor identified across original research studies** [2]. Individuals with a history of ankle sprain have approximately a **3.5-fold higher injury rate** than those without [7]. Recurrence rates range from **12% to 47%** depending on sport, and CAI develops in an estimated **40–70%** of individuals with repetitive sprains [7][8]. Basketball-specific figures are especially striking: the NBA injury surveillance study (2013–14 through 2016–17) found that a documented ankle sprain in the prior year conferred a **41% increased incidence (IRR 1.41)** of a new sprain [3]. A widely cited basketball-focused resource notes that after one sprain, players are **4.9× more likely to sprain the same ankle again**, and **55% of athletes do not seek medical attention** for the initial injury [26]. Up to **73%** of basketball players report a history of ankle injury, with a mean of 3.5 prior injuries in one Australian cohort [4].

### 1.2 Peroneal Muscle Weakness and Reaction-Time Deficits

Peroneal (evertor) muscle dysfunction is among the most extensively studied neuromuscular contributors to recurrent sprains:

- **Eccentric evertor weakness and impaired end-range evertor strength:** Evidence supports that end-range (15–20°) eccentric evertor / concentric invertor strength ratios and eccentric evertor torque are impaired in chronically unstable ankles [9]. Resistance training targeting evertor strength improves isokinetic eversion strength and dynamic balance in patients with recurrent LAS [7].
- **Delayed peroneal reaction time (PRT):** The foundational meta-analysis by Hoch and McKeon (2014) established that individuals with previous ankle sprains exhibit delayed peroneal reaction time, with deficits more evident in CAI patients than in healthy controls [6, reported in 6]. These latency deficits reduce the peroneals' capacity to reflexively counter sudden inversion moments during landing and cutting — the exact mechanism of most basketball LAS injuries.
- **Muscle strength asymmetries:** Strength asymmetries ≥15% in ankle dorsiflexion/plantarflexion eccentric strength confer substantially elevated risk, and peroneus longus weakness specifically increases risk [5]. Hip and core strength deficits are also recognized as contributors in the kinetic-chain literature [5].

Rehabilitation targeting the peroneals — concentric and eccentric strengthening, isokinetic training — is therefore considered a cornerstone of secondary prevention in basketball players [18][24].

### 1.3 Proprioceptive Deficits

CAI is associated with impaired ankle proprioception due in part to mechanoreceptor damage from repeated ligament injury [6]. Systematic review evidence (Li et al., *Journal of Sport and Health Science*, 2021) demonstrates that **both kinesthesia and joint position sense (JPS) are impaired in CAI patients compared with both the uninjured contralateral limb and healthy controls**, with the largest deficits in inversion and eversion JPS [6, summarized in 6]. More recent work (Li et al., 2026) shows that proprioceptive deficits in CAI are **modality-specific** — primarily affecting kinesthesia (motion detection), force sense, and **landing proprioception** (discrimination of inversion angles at landing), with landing proprioception showing the strongest discriminative ability for CAI [15, described in 6]. Clinically, this supports targeted proprioceptive assessment and training as central to CAI management [6][18].

### 1.4 Postural Control and Dynamic Balance Deficits

Static and dynamic postural control impairments are well-documented risk factors and sequelae of recurrent LAS:

- The **Star Excursion Balance Test (SEBT)/Y-Balance Test (YBT)** is the most widely used dynamic balance assessment. In male collegiate athletes, **decreased YBT anterior reach (≤54.4%) and elevated BMI (≥30.2)** were independently associated with ankle sprain injury, and athletes with both had **4.84× greater odds** of sustaining an ankle sprain [11].
- A systematic review and meta-analysis of the YBT-Lower Quarter found that **anterior reach asymmetry ≥4 cm was associated with a 2.2× increased injury risk (OR 2.20)** and posteromedial asymmetry ≥4 cm with a **3.86× risk** in male soccer players; composite scores below ~89–94% were also predictive, though population-specific cutoffs matter [12][55][56].
- Static postural control deficits (single-leg balance) are also recognized as risk factors for LAS recurrence [5][6]. The NATA position statement recommends balance training as a core component of rehabilitation and follow-up management to reduce re-injury rates [2].

### 1.5 Cavovarus Foot Alignment and Hindfoot Varus

Cavovarus (subtle cavus) foot alignment is a structural risk factor for recurrent LAS and CAI:

- A case-control study comparing the prevalence of subtle cavus foot between CAI patients and controls on standing lateral radiographs found that **subtle cavus foot is more prevalent in CAI patients**, supporting it as a risk factor for chronic lateral ankle instability [13].
- The mechanism is biomechanical: a plantarflexed first ray and hindfoot varus limit the foot's ability to evert and pronate during weight acceptance, concentrating pressure on the lateral foot and impairing peroneal function [13]. Clinical signs include the **"peek-a-boo heel sign"** and a positive Coleman block test [13].
- In a retrospective case series from Yonsei Medical Journal (2020), 15 patients with CAI and cavovarus deformity underwent modified Broström repair combined with corrective osteotomies (calcaneal closing wedge, first metatarsal dorsiflexion osteotomy, gastrocnemius recession). At mean 58.5 months follow-up, **no patient experienced recurrent instability**, with talar tilt improving from 12.3° to 5.6° and anterior translation from 7.9 mm to 4.8 mm [reported in 13].
- Surgical decision-making should therefore include assessment of foot alignment; untreated cavovarus is associated with operative failure in CAI surgery [13][29].

### 1.6 Talar Tilt and Anterior Drawer Laxity (Mechanical Instability)

Mechanical instability — ligamentous laxity of the anterior talofibular ligament (ATFL) and calcaneofibular ligament (CFL) — is the structural substrate of CAI:

- **Anterior drawer test:** A positive test is typically defined as **>8 mm anterior translation** or **>1 cm compared with the contralateral side** with a soft end-feel; diagnostic studies report sensitivity of 86% and specificity of 74% (van Dijk et al.) [15].
- **Talar tilt test:** Pathological talar tilt is generally defined as **>10° absolute** or **>5° side-to-side difference** [14]. A talar tilt ≥15° on the injured side reliably indicates complete ATFL+CFL rupture [14].
- In a cross-sectional study of 106 elite football and basketball players, a **positive talar tilt test was significantly associated with both acute and recurrent left ankle sprains** — evidence that mechanical laxity and recurrence are linked in basketball populations [reported in 14, see also 6].
- The distinction between **mechanical instability (MAI)** and **functional instability (FAI)** is clinically important: functional deficits may respond to conservative neuromuscular training, whereas true mechanical laxity may require external support or surgical stabilization [6][32]. Novel biomechanical work in MAI patients identified **end-range pronation strength deficits** that limit active prevention of excessive supination — a potential indication for mechanical treatment (bracing or ligament repair) [6].

### 1.7 Reduced Ankle Dorsiflexion Range of Motion

Restricted ankle dorsiflexion is an established risk factor for LAS and CAI and is highly relevant to basketball's landing, cutting, and deceleration demands:

- A 2025 clinical perspective defines **clinically relevant dorsiflexion impairments** as **>1.5 cm side-to-side asymmetry on the weight-bearing lunge test (WBLT) or >4.7° tibial-inclination asymmetry**, with normative values of **>9–10 cm toe-to-wall distance and >40–42° tibial inclination** [10].
- Restricted dorsiflexion impairs force absorption during landing and deceleration, increasing load on lateral ankle structures [10][18]. Minimum dorsiflexion requirements are approximately **10° for walking and 20–30° for running**, underscoring the performance relevance in basketball [18].
- Dorsiflexion limitation is also associated with dynamic postural-control deficits: in CAI patients, dorsiflexion ROM had the strongest correlation (r = 0.55) with SEBT anterior reach distance [10, reported in 10].

### 1.8 Additional Contributing Factors

- **Age:** In the NBA, players under 26 years old had a **54% higher incidence** of ankle sprain than older players (IRR 1.54) [3]. Across the broader literature, adolescents and young adults (15–24 years) have the highest ankle sprain incidence [5].
- **BMI:** Elevated BMI is associated with increased ankle sprain risk; in collegiate male athletes, BMI ≥30.2 combined with low YBT anterior reach carried 4.84× odds [11].
- **Sex:** Some meta-analytic data suggest higher LAS incidence in females (13.6 vs. 6.9 per 1,000 exposures), although findings are mixed across studies [5][63]. In basketball, female players have comparable or slightly higher ankle injury rates (7.9 vs. 7.1 per 1,000 athlete exposures) [18].
- **Contralateral risk:** Recurrent instability is not confined to the originally injured ankle; bilateral CAI is common (a Taiwanese basketball cohort found 26% unilateral CAI and ~13% bilateral) [17].
- **Mechanism:** In the NBA, 71.2% of ankle sprains involved contact (most often stepping on an opponent's foot, 33.3% of contact mechanisms), and 61.6% of non-contact sprains occurred via inversion [3]. Activities most associated with injury: defense (18.8%), layups/drives (14.5%), and rebounding (12.2%) [3][4].

### 1.9 Risk-Factor Synthesis

The evidence converges on a **multifactorial model** in which a history of LAS initiates a cycle: ligamentous laxity (mechanical instability) → mechanoreceptor damage and proprioceptive loss → peroneal weakness and delayed reaction → dynamic postural-control deficits → elevated re-injury risk during sport-specific movements (landing, cutting, rebounding). Structural factors (cavovarus, restricted dorsiflexion, elevated BMI) and age modify this cycle. Screening and secondary-prevention programs in basketball should therefore target: (1) athletes with prior sprains, (2) peroneal strength and reaction, (3) dorsiflexion ROM, (4) dynamic balance (YBT/SEBT), and (5) foot alignment.

## Section 2: International Literature Documentation

### 2.1 Global Epidemiology

- Ankle sprains are among the most prevalent musculoskeletal injuries in physically active populations; ~2 million occur annually in the US (estimates range up to 28,000/day) [2][8][23]. Basketball accounts for **~41% of ankle sprains seen in US emergency departments** among athletes, followed by football (9.3%) and soccer (7.9%) [8].
- Ankle sprains represent **15–20% of all sports injuries**, and up to **45% of athletic injuries** in some estimates; they are the most common injury in basketball at all levels [2][18][66].
- An estimated **23,000–27,000 lateral ankle sprains occur daily in the US**; **12–47% of all ankle sprains are recurrent**, and recurrence rates by sport include 46% (volleyball), 43% (American football), **28% (basketball)**, and 19% (soccer) [8][7].
- Up to **74% of LAS patients have persistent symptoms** at follow-up, and nearly **40% develop CAI within one year** of initial injury [1].

### 2.2 Basketball-Specific Injury Patterns (Global)

- **NBA (North America):** The NBA injury surveillance study (796 ankle sprains among 389 players over four seasons) found a **single-season ankle sprain risk of 25.8%** — roughly **1 in 4 NBA players per season** [3]. Game incidence was 4.5 per 1,000 player-games, higher than collegiate (1.4–2.3) and high school (0.8) rates [3]. Lateral sprains comprised **80.2%** of all sprains [3]. Median time loss was 2 games (8 days) for sprains causing missed time; high/syndesmotic sprains caused a median 5 games / 24 days lost [3].
- **Collegiate (US/global):** Ankle injuries are the most common injury in both men's and women's collegiate basketball; men's basketball ranks among the top NCAA sports for LAS incidence (~11.96 per 10,000 athlete-exposures) [4, reported in 4]. Injury rates in basketball are ~7.1–7.9 per 1,000 athlete exposures [18].
- **Australia:** Ankle injuries account for **over half (53.7%) of all playing time missed** due to injury in basketball; 73% of Australian basketball players report a history of ankle injury (mean 3.5 prior injuries) [4].
- **Taiwan:** A cross-sectional study of basketball athletes found **60% experienced recurrent ankle sprains**; 28% perceived instability despite a history of sprain, with ~26% meeting criteria for unilateral CAI [17].
- **Iran:** In a cross-sectional study of 106 elite football and basketball players (Tehran University of Medical Sciences), ankle sprains were significantly more prevalent in basketball players (p = 0.002 for acute, p = 0.018 for recurrent), with recurrence documented in 14.2% and a positive talar tilt test associated with recurrent LAS [6, reported in 14].
- **Cameroon:** A 2024 cross-sectional study of 215 professional and amateur athletes in Bafoussam found a first-sprain prevalence of 72.6%, **recurrence in 61.5% of those with an initial sprain**, and identified **hand-ball sports (basketball/volleyball/handball) as an independent risk factor for recurrence (aOR 4.72)**. Physiotherapy exposure and functional recovery before return to sport were protective [reported in 54, author-year mention].
- **Japan:** Prospective work in collegiate male basketball players has examined spinal reflex excitability changes across a competitive season in those who sprained vs. those who remained injury-free, documenting neurophysiological consequences of in-season sprains [53, author-year mention].

### 2.3 Regional Case Reports and Case Series

- **North America:** A case report from Angelo State University (Texas) documents an adolescent African-American basketball player with extensive recurrent LAS leading to CAI — illustrative of the common clinical trajectory in youth basketball [author-year mention]. The US Military Health System retrospective cohort (30,910 patients with ankle sprains) found **44.1% had at least one recurrence** and 19.6% had three or more care episodes, reinforcing recurrence as the rule rather than the exception even outside sport [reported in 41, author-year mention].
- **Europe:** A 2-year prospective cohort of Greek professional female basketball players recorded 32 ankle sprains among 204 participants (1.12 per 1,000 exposure-hours) [author-year mention]. A prospective single-season European basketball epidemiological study documented ankle sprain incidence comparable to knee overuse injury incidence [author-year mention]. In elite football (as the closest high-volume cutting-sport evidence), the average time-loss after LAS is only 15 days, with recurrence averaging 17% (range 6–41%) — highlighting premature RTS as a driver of recurrence [52, author-year mention within 1].
- **Asia:** The Yonsei Medical Journal case series (South Korea, 2020; n=15, mean age 25.7) demonstrated that combined modified Broström repair plus cavovarus correction eliminated recurrent instability at ~5-year follow-up [reported in 13]. Chinese investigators have contributed leading systematic reviews on CAI proprioception [14, author-year mention].
- **Middle East:** Iranian research has documented basketball/football ankle sprain patterns (2016) and, more recently, an RCT of proprioceptive training with visual feedback in athletes with CAI (Nasirpour et al., 2025) showing significant improvements in static and dynamic balance with feedback-enhanced training [21, report noted in 22].
- **Africa:** The Cameroonian study above represents one of the few published African datasets specifically addressing ankle sprain recurrence and secondary-prevention gaps in basketball/hand-sports athletes [author-year mention].
- **Oceania:** Australian data (field-based studies of recreational/semi-competitive basketball; 3.85 ankle injuries per 1,000 athlete-participations, ~46% causing ≥1 week missed) are frequently cited in the international literature [4].

**Documentation gap:** Higher-level basketball-specific evidence (RCTs) remains sparse outside North American collegiate/NBA surveillance; much of the treatment evidence derives from mixed-sport cohorts (soccer, volleyball, handball, netball) and must be extrapolated to basketball with caution. Case reports and case series remain important contributors to the international evidence base, particularly for surgical indications and rare/combined pathologies [14][31][42].

## Section 3: Comparative Treatment Outcomes

### 3.1 Non-Surgical Rehabilitation Programs

#### Balance/Proprioceptive Training

- A systematic review/meta-analysis of 3,726 participants found proprioceptive training significantly reduced ankle sprain incidence overall (**RR 0.65**), with **RR 0.64** for secondary prevention in those with a history of sprain; **number needed to treat = 13** for those with prior sprain vs. 17 overall [19].
- The foundational Verhagen RCT (2004) demonstrated that a proprioceptive balance-board program effectively prevented ankle sprain **recurrences** [20].
- A meta-analysis of 8 RCTs (7,195 competitive athletes) found **balance training reduced ankle sprain risk by 46% (RR 0.54)** [8].
- In CAI specifically, an 8-week wobble-board program was superior to resistance-tubing strengthening on patient-oriented outcomes (FAAM-ADL, +6.1%; Hedge's g = 0.928), though both improved clinician-oriented tests (SEBT, side-hop, figure-of-8 hop, foot-lift test) [21].
- A 6-week home-based RCT in patients with recurrent LAS found both resistance training and balance training improved isokinetic eversion strength (30°/s and 120°/s) and dynamic balance; **balance training produced significantly greater improvements** in crossover-hop, YBT total/posteromedial/posterolateral reach, and FAOS pain, sports function, and quality-of-life subscales [7].
- A 2025 systematic review of rehabilitation interventions for athletes with CAI (13 RCTs, 490 athletes aged 16–30) concluded that balance training, plyometric training, neuromuscular training, and CrossFit-based programs all improve static/dynamic postural stability and patient-reported outcomes (FAAM, CAIT), with added benefits from stroboscopic vision, vibration, and neurofeedback adjuncts [22].

#### Neuromuscular/Perturbation Training

- Neuromuscular training (NMT) combined with neurofeedback was more effective than NMT alone for postural control and proprioception, and reduced anxiety/depression in athletes with CAI [22].
- Perturbation-based and sport-specific NMT programs (e.g., FIFA 11+) reduce ankle injury rates in team sports by ~32% [70][74].
- Early functional rehabilitation (early weight-bearing, bracing, therapeutic exercise) is supported as effective for reducing recurrence risk [23].

#### Peroneal Strengthening

- Peroneal strengthening (especially eccentric) is a pillar of LAS rehabilitation protocols [18][24]. Isokinetic eversion strength testing (30°/s and 120°/s) is a valid outcome measure for rehab progression [7][57].
- End-range eccentric evertor strength is specifically impaired in CAI and should be targeted [9].

#### Bracing/Taping as Conservative Adjuncts

- Ankle braces reduce first-time and recurrent sprain risk by **~69%** without significantly impairing sprint/jump/change-of-direction performance [63].
- A meta-analysis found bracing reduced ankle sprain risk by **64% (RR 0.36)** vs. controls [8].
- In previously injured athletes, braces reduced recurrence by **69%** and tape by **71%**; no type of support was clearly superior [67].

#### Typical Non-Surgical RTP Timelines

- Grade 1: days to 1–2 weeks; Grade 2: 2–6 weeks; Grade 3: 6–12+ weeks [26].
- An NBA physical therapist's experienced-based timelines: Grade I ~7 days, Grade II ~15 days, Grade IIIA ~31 days, Grade IIIB ~55 days [27].
- Mass General Brigham's non-operative protocol spans 4 phases (~11–16 weeks for full RTS) with explicit functional progression criteria [25].
- A 2025 systematic review found average RTS times of ~3 days for high school athletes and 44.4% of intercollegiate athletes returning in <24 hours — widely considered premature relative to functional recovery [1][49].

### 3.2 Surgical Stabilization Procedures

#### Indications

Surgical stabilization is indicated when **3–6 months of structured non-surgical management fails** to restore stability in patients with clinically and/or radiographically confirmed CLAI — including high-demand athletes with recurrent injuries [29][30][31]. Approximately **20–30% of patients with acute LAS develop persistent instability** requiring surgical consideration [31]. The Chinese Society of Sports Medicine clinical guidelines specify surgery when symptoms persist after 3–6 months of non-surgical treatment with positive anterior drawer/talar tilt and confirmatory stress radiography or MRI (recommendation 2C) [29].

#### Modified Broström-Gould (Open) Outcomes

- In 18 elite athletes (mean age 19.3; including basketball players) undergoing modified Broström operation, mean RTP was **3.9 months** (RTP 83.3% at 4 months, **100% at 8 months**); no reoperations or major complications occurred [34].
- Across the pooled literature, **95% of patients return to any sport, 83% return to preinjury level, and 87% return to competitive sport** after anatomic lateral ankle stabilization, with a **mean RTS time of 12.45 weeks (95% CI 10.8–14.1)** [33]. Professional/competitive athletes returned at higher rates (93%) than recreational athletes (83%); age and BMI modestly increased failure risk [33].
- A systematic review of lateral ligament reconstruction in professional athletes (343 athletes, mean age 23) found **89% returned to preinjury sport** at a mean of **16 weeks**, with a 19.8% complication rate (numbness 7%, stiffness 5.2%, superficial infection 2.6%) [35].
- An ISAKOS systematic review (37 articles, 2,593 repairs) reported **86.6% RTP at mean 13.7 weeks**, with 78.9% returning to pre-injury activity level [36].
- **Predictors of RTS failure after MBG:** multivariate analysis of 62 patients under 35 identified pre-operative FAOS pain <90 (OR 8.66), recurrence of mechanical instability (OR 13.99), and inadequate dynamic postural control (OSI >1.8; OR 4.21) as independent risk factors for failure to return to preinjury sport [37].

#### Arthroscopic vs. Open Repair

- An RCT (40 patients) comparing open vs. arthroscopic Broström-Gould found **equivalent functional outcomes at 12 and 18 months**, with the arthroscopic group showing better early (6-month) VAS/functional scores and **earlier return to work (8.8 vs. 11.2 weeks)** [38].
- A comparative study of all-inside arthroscopic vs. open MBG found significantly **earlier painless walking** in the arthroscopic group (7.1 vs. 11.0 weeks) and better 6-week VAS/AOFAS, with no differences at 6 months [39].
- A 2023 systematic review/meta-analysis (11 studies, 686 patients) found no significant differences in talar tilt, anterior translation, complication rates, RTS time, or 2-year functional scores; arthroscopic repair allowed **earlier weight-bearing by ~1.3 weeks** and less perioperative pain [40].
- A mid-term (48-month) comparison likewise found equivalent outcomes and complication rates (16.3% arthroscopic vs. 14% open) [41].
- The Chinese guidelines rate open vs. arthroscopic repair as **equivalent short-term outcomes (1B)** [29]. A Singapore case series (39 patients) confirmed significant improvements in VAS (5.87→2.0), Karlsson (43.7→69.2), and AOFAS (62.5→83.8) with no recurrent instability [42].

#### Internal-Brace (Suture-Tape) Augmentation

- Suture-tape augmentation (internal brace) parallels the ATFL with high-strength tape; biomechanical studies report ultimate load-to-failure of 250–315 N vs. ~154 N for native ATFL [43].
- A multicenter RCT (119 patients) found **return to preinjury activity at 13.3 weeks vs. 17.5 weeks** with traditional repair (p = 0.001) [43].
- A 5-year RCT (Porter et al.) reported superior Foot and Ankle Outcome Scores and Tegner scores in the augmented group, with zero failures vs. two in traditional repair [43].
- A case series using one internal brace to augment both ATFL and CFL found mean RTS of **5.72 months** in 14 recreational athletes (12/14 returned) [44].
- Complication profiles differ: recurrence/revision is more common after traditional Broström; peroneal nerve/tendon irritation is more common with suture-tape constructs [43]. Augmentation is particularly useful in elite athletes, revision cases, generalized laxity, and poor tissue quality [43][45][46].

### 3.3 Direct Comparisons: Conservative vs. Surgical Management

**Critical caveat:** No high-level, non-retracted RCT directly randomizes athletes with established CAI to structured rehabilitation alone vs. operative stabilization. The only surgical-vs-conservative meta-analysis identified (Liu & Diao, 2019, in *International Wound Journal*) addressed **acute** lateral ankle ligament injuries and has been **formally retracted**; its findings must not be cited as valid evidence [47].

The established clinical framework is therefore a **stepped-care paradigm**:

1. **All patients — including those with recurrent LAS/CAI — begin with structured non-surgical management** (functional rehabilitation, bracing, neuromuscular training) for at least 3–6 months [29][30][31][32].
2. **Surgery is reserved for non-responders** — those with persistent mechanical laxity (positive anterior drawer/talar tilt), recurrent "giving way," or failure of sport-specific function despite adherence to rehabilitation [29][30].
3. **When surgery is performed**, pooled RTS benchmarks are: 83–95% RTS, mean 12.45–16 weeks [33][35][36]. For conservative management, published protocols expect RTS in ~11–16 weeks for a complete rehabilitation course [25], but this reflects a course following an acute sprain, not established CAI.

Because the two approaches are applied to different patient populations (conservative for all-comers; surgery for rehab-resistant cases), direct "which is superior" comparisons are not possible from the current evidence. The pragmatic interpretation is that **rehabilitation should be optimized and objectively tested before considering surgery**, and that young basketball players with confirmed mechanical laxity who fail rehabilitation have high RTS rates with surgical stabilization [33][34].

## Section 4: Return-to-Play Criteria and Prevention Protocols

### 4.1 RTS Decision-Making Frameworks

- **No standardized RTS paradigm exists.** A systematic review of expert opinions found consensus only on assessing sport-specific movement; there was no agreement on specific tests or cutoffs, and **no expert recommended time-from-injury as a criterion** [49]. A 2019 systematic review found **no studies using a fully criteria-based RTS process** for LAS [48].
- The **PAASS framework** (international multidisciplinary consensus, 2021) classifies RTS decisions into five domains: **P**ain (pain severity in sport and last 24 h), **A**nkle impairments (ROM, strength, endurance, power), **A**thlete perception (perceived stability, confidence, psychological readiness), **S**ensorimotor control (proprioception, dynamic postural control, landing in unpredictable situations), and **S**port/functional performance (hopping, jumping, agility, sport-specific drills, culminating in a full training session) [50].
- RTS should be treated as a **continuum**: return to participation → return to sport → return to performance [1]. Residual deficits at RTS are common — in one systematic review, 65.1% of patients still had pain and 86.2% had functional deficits when cleared [1].

### 4.2 Functional Testing Thresholds

#### Hop Tests and Limb Symmetry Index (LSI)

- Four tests are standard: single hop for distance, triple hop, crossover hop, and 6-m timed hop [51].
- **Recommended minimum LSI: ≥90%**; however, healthy physically active adults average **93.8% LSI** (range across tests 91.9–96.1%), and **100% of healthy subjects achieve at least 90%** — prompting recommendations that athletes should reach **≥95%, ideally 97–100%**, before RTS [52][53].
- The 6-m timed hop at **88% LSI** had 90% specificity for normal knee function (ACL literature), and the crossover hop at **95% LSI** had 88% sensitivity; the crossover hop was the strongest predictor of self-reported function, likely reflecting its multiplanar demands [54]. The crossover hop also explained 66% of the variance in psychological readiness (ACL-RSI) in NCAA Division I athletes [62].

#### Y-Balance Test / SEBT

- **Anterior reach asymmetry ≥4 cm:** OR 2.20 for injury [12]; in basketball players, this asymmetry predicts lower-extremity injury [31, reported in 55].
- **Composite score ≤94%:** ~3× increased risk (6.5× in girls) [55]; collegiate football cut point <89% (+LR 3.5) [55][56]; basketball players' composite norm is ~92.8 (lower than soccer 97.6), so sport-specific interpretation is required [12].
- Male collegiate athletes with **BMI ≥30.2 + anterior reach ≤54.4%** had 4.84× odds of ankle sprain [11].

#### Strength Testing

- Isokinetic ankle strength (30°/s torque, 120°/s power) with **LSI and muscular deficiency index (MDI) thresholds ≥90%** is proposed as an objective benchmark for return to activity [57].
- Reference agonist/antagonist ratios: **evertor/invertor ≈ 88.8%**; dorsiflexor/plantarflexor ≈ 36.1% [57].
- Isometric ankle eversion normative values for CAI patients: ~141–176 N (handheld dynamometry), with side-to-side differences ≥10% warranting intervention [58].

#### Range of Motion

- Weight-bearing lunge: normals **>9–10 cm** and **>40–42°**; clinically relevant asymmetry **>1.5 cm or >4.7°** [10]. Each 1 cm of toe-to-wall distance ≈ 3.6° of dorsiflexion [81].
- Minimum functional dorsiflexion: ~10° for gait, 20–30° for running [18].

#### Balance Tests

- Single-leg stance (firm surface): **proposed cutoff <3 errors** (ICC 0.93) [16].
- Foot lift test (30 s): **proposed cutoff <5 lifts** [16].

#### Self-Reported and Psychological Readiness

- **FAAM:** proposed cutoff **>90–95%** for ADL and Sport subscales (MCID 8–9 points) [16][25].
- **ALR-RSI (Ankle Ligament Reconstruction–Return to Sport after Injury):** proposed cutoff **55/120** in expert-opinion thresholds [16]; validation studies identify an **optimal cutoff of 76.7** (sensitivity 81%, specificity 75%) [59][60]. A short-form ALR-RSI is also validated [61]. Kinvent's protocol uses **ALR-RSI >70** for final RTP clearance [24].
- Psychological readiness correlates strongly with hop performance in young athletes — undertreated fear/kinesiophobia predicts delayed or failed RTS [62].

### 4.3 External Support Strategies (Bracing/Taping)

- **Ankle braces reduce first-time and recurrent injuries by ~69%** in basketball players, without significant impairment of sprint, jump, or change-of-direction performance, and without increased knee injury risk [63].
- In high school basketball specifically, lace-up braces reduced ankle injury incidence by **68%** regardless of prior injury history [64].
- A meta-analysis of 8 RCTs found bracing reduced sprain risk by **64% (RR 0.36)** [8]; a second review reported **69% reduction with braces and 71% with tape in previously injured athletes** [67].
- **Taping loses 40–50% of its support after ~1 hour of activity**, whereas semi-rigid braces maintain support [18][67].
- The NATA position statement recommends **prophylactic supports for all practices and games in athletes with prior sprains** (Evidence B) [2].
- Economic modeling: a single ankle injury costs €262–2,032 direct and €1,328–3,890 indirect; a seasonal team-wide bracing program costs under €100/player — bracing is roughly one-third the cost of taping with equivalent or better protection [63].
- Practical caveats: bracing is a **short-to-medium-term adjunct**, not a substitute for rehabilitation; braces may reduce proprioceptive challenge if used continuously during rehab, and some performance literature shows up to ~5% reduction in running/jumping performance [68]. Braces are most effective in athletes with a history of sprain [67][68].

### 4.4 Workload and Load-Management Strategies

- **Training load and injury risk in basketball:** A 2024 systematic review (14 articles) found significant (at least partially) relationships between training load and injury risk in 11 studies. Key thresholds: **≤3 decelerations (>2 m/s²) per game** and **≤1.3 miles covered per game** in professional male players were associated with higher injury risk, suggesting inadequate external-load exposure (undertraining) and player-specific workload profiles matter [76].
- **Acute:chronic workload ratio (ACWR):** a "sweet spot" of **0.8–1.3** is proposed to minimize injury risk; ratios <0.8 (undertraining) and >1.5 are associated with elevated risk. However, large RCTs have failed to show that ACWR-guided management reduces injuries, and the metric faces methodological criticism [77].
- **The 10% rule:** Avoid increasing total weekly training load by more than ~10% week-over-week; each 96 minutes of cumulative play was associated with a 2.87% increase in injury odds in one 2024 analysis [78].
- **Sleep:** adolescents sleeping <8 h/night have a 1.7× increased injury risk; basketball-specific research strongly supports sleep as a recovery pillar [77][78].
- **Return-from-injury load progression:** graduated load increments of ~10–30% across four phases (rehabilitation → reconditioning → full-intensity practice → competition) reduce re-injury risk [77].
- **Sport-specific context:** Elite basketball players cover ~73 m/min, ~3 m/min at high-speed running, ~4 accelerations/decelerations >2 m/s²/min, and ~0.2 jumps >3 G/min — so return programs must progressively restore these demands before full clearance [74].

### 4.5 Prevention Programs

#### Balance/Proprioceptive Training

- **Proprioceptive training reduces ankle sprain incidence by ~35% (RR 0.65), with NNT 13 for athletes with prior sprains** [19]. Balance-board programs specifically reduce **recurrences** [20].
- A 6-year balance-training program reduced acute ankle sprains by 81% in one landmark study; a multistation proprioceptive exercise program reduced sprain odds by ~65% (NNT 7) [8, reported in 8].
- The NATA recommends multi-intervention balance/neuromuscular programs of **at least 3 months** duration (Evidence A) [2].

#### Neuromuscular Warm-Up Programs

- **FIFA 11+:** A 2025 systematic review found FIFA 11+ reduced **ankle injury rates by 32%** (0.78 vs. 1.14 per 1,000 exposure-hours) in football; the older FIFA 11 program was not effective for ankle injuries [70]. FIFA 11+ has also demonstrated reduced injury rates in elite basketball players warmed up with the program [71]. Compliance is critical — the program must be performed ≥2×/week [71][72].
- **SHRed Injuries Basketball:** A neuromuscular training warm-up program demonstrated a **36% reduction in ankle and knee injury rates in youth basketball** [75].
- **Basketball-specific ankle-focused circuit:** A 2021 Spanish-position paper describes a 9-exercise neuromuscular warm-up (ankle mobility with elastic bands, closed-kinetic-chain peroneal/tibialis posterior activation, single-foot balance on unstable surfaces, lateral high-speed steps, coordination-ladder footwork, perturbation/decision-making partner drills) tailored to the demands of elite basketball [74].
- **General NMT effect sizes:** A meta-analysis of NMT in volleyball athletes (a comparable jumping sport) found moderate improvements in overall stability (SMD 0.63), with the strongest effects for upper-limb perturbation training in males and single-leg balance/neuromotor training in females; ≥8 weeks of training is recommended to consolidate adaptations [79].

## Section 5: Evidence Recency (2020–2026) and Quality Assessment

### 5.1 Key 2020–2026 Contributions

The 2020–2026 period has materially advanced the evidence base:

- **RTS frameworks and criteria:** Jiang et al. 2025 systematic review of RTS strategies [1]; PAASS consensus framework (2021) [50]; Kouvelis et al. 2022 functional-test thresholds for CAI [16]; Verschueren et al. 2019 critique of time-based RTS [48]; JSR 2020 lack-of-consensus review [49].
- **Surgical evidence:** AJSM 2024 meta-analysis of RTS after anatomic lateral ankle stabilization (95% RTS, 12.45 weeks) [33]; 2023 JOSR meta-analysis of arthroscopic vs. open Broström-Gould [40]; Wahib et al. 2026 RCT (arthroscopic vs. open) [38]; Lee et al. 2024 all-inside arthroscopic comparison [39]; Kim et al. 2025 predictors of RTS after MBG [37]; AAOS Now 2026 synthesis of suture-tape augmentation [43]; Sano et al. 2026 current concepts in arthroscopic CLAI management [31].
- **Rehabilitation evidence:** Skwiot et al. 2025 systematic review of CAI rehabilitation interventions (targeted exactly at 16–30-year-old athletes) [22]; Wang et al. 2021 RCT of resistance vs. balance training in recurrent sprain [7]; Bleakley 2025 rehabilitation chapter [23].
- **Prevention and bracing:** Medicina 2026 narrative review on ankle bracing in basketball [63]; Alhazmi et al. 2025 FIFA injury-prevention systematic review [70]; Chan et al. 2024 basketball training-load systematic review [76]; Yang et al. 2026 NMT meta-analysis [79]; Apunts 2021 basketball-specific warm-up [74].
- **Diagnostics and thresholds:** Plisky et al. 2021 YBT systematic review/meta-analysis [12]; Tourillon et al. 2025 dorsiflexion restoration framework [10]; World Journal of Orthopedics 2025 normative isokinetic ankle strength values with LSI/MDI ≥90% proposal [57]; Saliba et al. 2024 ALR-RSI validation with 76.7 cutoff [59][60]; Madsen et al. 2020 normative hop-test LSI data [52].

### 5.2 Evidence Gaps and Methodological Limitations

1. **No direct RCT comparing non-surgical rehabilitation vs. surgery for established CAI in athletes.** The only identified meta-analysis of surgical vs. conservative management was **retracted** [47]. The stepped-care paradigm (3–6 months of rehabilitation before surgery) is universally endorsed but not directly tested head-to-head [29][30].
2. **Basketball-specific data are sparse** for several dimensions (especially surgical outcomes in recreational/collegiate basketball players). Most surgical cohorts are mixed-sport; NBA-level data are epidemiological rather than interventional [3][4].
3. **No standardized RTS criteria exist**, and fewer than one-third of surgical studies report explicit RTP criteria; only ~5% use objective/quantitative thresholds [36][49].
4. **Outcome heterogeneity:** AOFAS, Karlsson, FAAM/FAOS, CAIT, VAS, and Tegner are used inconsistently, limiting comparability [29][36].
5. **Publication/quality issues:** Many rehabilitation trials have small samples and short follow-up [22]; a 2025 meta-analysis on training-load thresholds (ACWR) failed to find protective effects, questioning load-ratio dogma [77]; one prominent "conservative vs. surgery" meta-analysis was retracted [47].
6. **Heterogeneity of CAI definition:** International Ankle Consortium definitions require ≥2 "giving way" episodes in 6 months; many studies use looser criteria, complicating synthesis [22][6].

## Section 6: Clinical Synthesis and Recommendations

For a 16–30-year-old recreational or collegiate basketball player presenting with recurrent LAS and season-to-season instability, the evidence supports the following integrated approach:

1. **Comprehensive assessment** — quantify mechanical laxity (anterior drawer, talar tilt), functional instability (CAIT, FAAM), peroneal strength (isokinetic/hand-held, evertor:invertor ratio), dorsiflexion ROM (weight-bearing lunge), dynamic balance (YBT/SEBT), proprioception, foot alignment (cavovarus screen), and psychological readiness [6][10][29][50].
2. **First-line conservative rehabilitation (3–6 months minimum)** — progressive balance/proprioceptive training (wobble board, perturbation, stroboscopic/vibration adjuncts where available), eccentric peroneal strengthening, dorsiflexion restoration (mobilizations + stretching ≥1,200 s/week where impaired), NMT warm-up integration, and external support (lace-up or semi-rigid brace) for all practices/games [2][10][19][22][63].
3. **Return-to-sport gatekeeping** — clear athletes only after meeting objective thresholds: **≥90–95% LSI on hop testing, YBT anterior asymmetry <4 cm and composite within sport norms, weight-bearing lunge asymmetry <1.5 cm, FAAM >90–95%, ALR-RSI ≥~70**, and successful completion of sport-specific (basketball) progressive exposures including a full practice [16][24][25][50][52][53][59].
4. **Surgical consideration after failed rehabilitation** — in patients with confirmed mechanical laxity and persistent instability despite 3–6 months of structured care, modified Broström-Gould (open or arthroscopic) with or without internal-brace augmentation yields 83–95% RTS at ~12–16 weeks, with early accelerated protocols showing earlier weight-bearing and return to work [29][33][38][40][43]. Concurrent intra-articular pathology (synovitis, osteochondral lesions) and cavovarus alignment should be addressed [29][13][46].
5. **Prevention and load management** — continue balance/NMT programs 2–3×/week during the season [2][70]; maintain bracing for at least one full season post-return [2][63]; monitor workload (avoid >10% weekly jumps; consider deceleration/volume thresholds; ensure ≥8 h sleep) [76][77][78]; address psychological fear of re-injury explicitly [50][62].
