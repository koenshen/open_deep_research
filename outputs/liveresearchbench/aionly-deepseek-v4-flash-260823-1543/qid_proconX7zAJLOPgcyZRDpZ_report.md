# Standardized Testing in K-12 Education: A Comprehensive Analysis of China, Japan, and the United States

## Introduction

The debate over whether standardized tests improve education is one of the most consequential and polarizing discussions in global education policy. Standardized tests—from China's Gaokao to Japan's Common Test and America's SAT/ACT and state assessments—serve as the backbone of accountability systems, college admissions, and educational evaluation across the world's three largest economies. Yet these same tests face mounting criticism for their impact on student well-being, curriculum breadth, and equity.

This report examines the evidence on both sides of this debate, drawing on research from 2021 to 2026 across China, Japan, and the United States. Each of the six pro arguments and six con arguments is structured with a clear thesis, multiple supporting points substantiated with credible evidence, and comparative insights across the three countries. The analysis prioritizes data from official government reports, academic journals, international assessments (PISA 2022, TIMSS 2023, NAEP 2024), and reputable news sources.

## Part I: Pro Arguments — Six Ways Standardized Tests Improve Education

### Pro Argument 1: Standardized Tests Provide Objective, Comparable Data for Measuring Educational Quality and Driving Accountability

**Thesis:** Standardized tests serve as a neutral, objective yardstick that allows educators, parents, policymakers, and the public to measure student learning, compare performance across schools and regions, and hold educational systems accountable for results.

**Supporting Evidence:**

In the United States, standardized tests are defined as "any test that's administered, scored, and interpreted in a standard, predetermined manner," according to W. James Popham, former president of the American Educational Research Association [1]. Aaron Churchill of the Thomas B. Fordham Institute explained: "At their core, standardized exams are designed to be objective measures. They assess students based on a similar set of questions, are given under nearly identical testing conditions, and are graded by a machine or blind reviewer" [1].

The Fordham Institute's 2024 analysis presents six key arguments for preserving standardized testing, including that "Tests provide an essential source of information for students and parents about student learning, alongside grades and teacher feedback" [5]. The analysis notes that most parents overestimate their child's performance—NAEP shows only about a third of students are proficient in reading and math—and that tests help counteract grade inflation, as GPAs have risen while ACT/SAT and NAEP scores have stagnated. As Chester Finn wrote, "we have solid evidence over thirty years in America and beyond that students learn more when they—and their schools—are held to account for what and how well they're learning" [5][54].

The accountability framework under No Child Left Behind (NCLB) produced meaningful improvements. Research by Thomas Dee and Brian Jacob (Education Next, 2010) found that NCLB accountability generated large, statistically significant increases in 4th-grade math achievement—roughly 7.2 scale points, or 0.23 standard deviations, by 2007. These gains were concentrated among African American, Hispanic, and students eligible for subsidized lunch [55]. NCLB increased the share of students performing at or above basic in math by 10 percentage points (4th grade) and 6 percentage points (8th grade) [55].

State-level examples demonstrate the power of accountability: Massachusetts saw significant gains after implementing MCAS testing and high standards, becoming #1 nationally in reading and math [54]. Florida's "A+ Plan" produced dramatic NAEP score increases from the late 1990s to 2017—Black fourth graders' reading scores rose 26 points, Hispanic 27, low-income 29—lifting Florida from mid-tier to top tier [54].

In China, the Gaokao (National College Entrance Examination) was introduced in 1952, discontinued during the Cultural Revolution (1966-1976), and restored in 1977. It traces its roots back to the imperial examination system (keju) from 600 AD, maintaining a 1,300-year tradition of exam-oriented culture [6]. Every year, around 10 million students take the Gaokao, and in 2024, a record 13.42 million students registered [9]. The system provides a standardized, nationwide measure of academic achievement that allows universities to compare students from vastly different regions and educational backgrounds.

Japan's Common Test for University Admissions, introduced in 2021 to replace the National Center Test, is a standardized exam designed for students graduating from upper secondary school. A total of 479 Japanese universities (over 60% of all such universities) require submission of EJU scores for international students [12]. The test provides a consistent baseline for evaluating students from diverse high schools across the country.

**Analysis and Comparative Insights:**

The evidence across all three countries demonstrates that standardized tests provide essential data for educational accountability. In the US, state-level accountability systems have produced measurable improvements in student achievement, particularly for historically underserved groups. In China, the Gaokao serves as a unifying national standard that enables meritocratic selection despite massive regional disparities in educational resources. In Japan, the Common Test provides a common benchmark for university admissions across the country's diverse high school system.

The key insight is that standardized tests, when properly designed and used as part of a balanced accountability system, provide information that would otherwise be unavailable. Without them, educational disparities become invisible, and there is no objective basis for targeting resources or interventions. As the Center for American Progress argues: "The yearly state standardized assessment alone cannot ensure a high-quality education for every child. But without it, educators do not know their progress toward meeting that goal" [3].

### Pro Argument 2: Standardized Tests Promote Equity by Revealing Achievement Gaps and Enabling Targeted Resource Allocation

**Thesis:** Standardized testing data serves as the only consistent, objective source for identifying disparities in educational outcomes across racial, socioeconomic, and geographic lines, enabling policymakers and educators to target resources and interventions to the students and schools that need them most.

**Supporting Evidence:**

A 2024 letter signed by 12 civil rights organizations including the NAACP and the American Association of University Women explained: "Data obtained through some standardized tests are particularly important to the civil rights community because they are the only available, consistent, and objective source of data about disparities in educational outcomes... We cannot fix what we cannot measure" [1]. The U.S. Department of Education's Office for Civil Rights relies on testing data to enforce anti-discrimination laws under Title VI and Title II [31].

NAEP data shows that the Black-White achievement gap in Grade 4 mathematics narrowed by 6 points between 1990 and 2019 [31][34]. While this progress is insufficient, it demonstrates that measurement enables accountability and improvement. "Inclusion of students with disabilities and English learners… allows us to measure how well the system is doing for these students," said Sheryl Lazarus [1].

EdTrust's "Reassessing ESSA Implementation" report analyzes state accountability systems against five equity principles. The report notes that only 11 states incorporate student group performance into overall school ratings, but frameworks exist precisely to identify and address these shortcomings. Colorado, New Jersey, and Washington, DC, stand out for having the clearest and most substantial incorporation of student group performance into their accountability systems—DC dedicates 70% of each indicator score to student group performance [18].

In China, the Gaokao reforms initiated in 2014 include scaling back the bonus point system, which previously favored privileged groups, and improving policies for migrant children to take the Gaokao in their residential areas [7]. The system provides special support for central/western regions through collaborative enrollment plans and increased admission quotas for underprivileged areas [7].

The equity comparison between China and the United States is striking: in China, children from the richest 20% of families are 2.3 times more likely to go to an elite college than those from the poorest 20%. Meanwhile, in the United States, students from wealthy families are fully eleven times more likely to attend elite institutions than those from poor families [37]. This suggests that China's exam-based system is more equitable in terms of socioeconomic access to elite education. As Jia and Li argue in "The Highest Exam" (2025): "Despite the many inequalities in China's education system that we discuss in the book, our research finds that elite colleges there are more accessible than their counterparts in the United States, thanks to the exam-based admissions system" [9].

In Japan, the Common Test for University Admissions is a nationwide standardized exam used by universities as a selection tool, ensuring a consistent baseline for evaluating students from diverse high schools across the country. The exam covers 30 subjects across 6 areas, allowing students to demonstrate proficiency in their chosen fields [16].

**Analysis and Comparative Insights:**

The argument that standardized tests reveal achievement gaps is supported by evidence from all three countries. In the US, civil rights organizations have consistently defended testing data as essential for identifying and addressing disparities. The NAACP and other groups recognize that eliminating tests would make educational disparities "invisible in the eyes of the public," as Harvard's David Deming argues [31].

The comparative data between China and the US is particularly revealing. China's exam-based system, despite its flaws, appears to provide more equitable access to elite education than the US holistic admissions system. This challenges the assumption that moving away from standardized testing necessarily improves equity. As Harvard's Opportunity Insights study found, students with the highest SAT/ACT scores earned college GPAs 0.43 points higher than those with modest scores, and there was no evidence that wealthier students outperformed low-income students with similar test scores—their college GPAs were "virtually identical" [46].

### Pro Argument 3: Standardized Tests Are Strong Predictors of College Success and Later-Life Outcomes

**Thesis:** Standardized test scores are strong, validated predictors of academic success in college, future earnings, and other important life outcomes, making them valuable tools for admissions and talent identification.

**Supporting Evidence:**

A landmark study from Opportunity Insights (Harvard-based, January 2024) found that "standardized test scores may have more value for admissions processes than previously understood in the literature, especially for highly selective colleges" [46]. Students with the highest SAT/ACT scores earned college GPAs 0.43 points higher than those with modest scores, while high school GPAs did "little to predict academic success in college" [46]. The study found no evidence that wealthier students outperformed low-income students with similar test scores—their college GPAs were "virtually identical" [46].

Dan Goldhaber and Umut Özek explained: "students who score one standard deviation higher on math tests at the end of high school have been shown to earn 12% more annually, or $3,600 for each year of work life in 2001" [1].

MIT reinstated its SAT/ACT requirement effective for the Class of 2027 and beyond. Dean of Admissions Stu Schmill stated: "Our research shows standardized tests help us better assess the academic preparedness of all applicants, and also help us identify socioeconomically disadvantaged students who lack access to advanced coursework or other enrichment opportunities that would otherwise demonstrate their readiness for MIT" [45]. "Our ability to accurately predict student academic success at MIT is significantly improved by considering standardized testing—especially in mathematics—alongside other factors" [45]. MIT's research found that without test scores, socioeconomic barriers to demonstrating readiness actually increase.

The trend toward reinstating testing requirements is accelerating. Top universities rolling back test-optional policies include Harvard, Stanford, Dartmouth, Yale (test-flexible, accepts AP/IB scores), MIT, Georgetown, Purdue, Caltech, University of Texas at Austin, and Princeton (starting 2027-2028) [21][44][43]. Only Columbia among the Ivies remains test-optional [44]. Brown, Dartmouth, and Yale found their applicant pools actually became less diverse as a result of the test-optional policy, as fewer low-income students and students of color applied [47].

In China, the authors' analysis in "The Highest Exam" shows four years of higher education yields a 40% premium on income, with another 40% premium for graduates from top-tier universities [8]. Gaokao scores are positively associated with both entrepreneurial ability and waged-job ability. Within the same college, a one-standard-deviation higher Gaokao score is associated with a 10% lower probability of creating a firm by mid-30s, but the firms created by higher-score individuals are more successful—typically larger, more likely to expand, more likely to enter non-local markets, more likely to survive, and more likely to be publicly listed [10].

In Japan, the ratio of high school students who continue to four-year universities as of April 2019 was the highest on record at 53.7%, up from 38.2% twenty years ago, indicating the system continues to expand access [40]. Companies and government agencies in Japan traditionally hire based on which university was attended rather than grades or performance, making the entrance exam the single most important determinant of career trajectory [13].

**Analysis and Comparative Insights:**

The predictive validity of standardized tests is supported by strong evidence across all three countries. The Opportunity Insights study provides particularly compelling evidence that test scores predict college success more effectively than high school GPA at selective institutions, and that test scores do not systematically disadvantage low-income students—in fact, they help identify talented students from disadvantaged backgrounds who might otherwise be overlooked.

The reinstatement of testing requirements by elite US universities represents a significant shift in the debate. These institutions—which have the resources to conduct the most comprehensive admissions research—have concluded that test scores improve their ability to identify promising students from all backgrounds. As Princeton University stated: "The decision to resume testing requirements follows a review of five years of data from the test-optional period, which found that academic performance at Princeton was stronger for students who chose to submit test scores than for students who did not" [44].

### Pro Argument 4: Standardized Tests Promote Meritocracy and Fairness in College Admissions

**Thesis:** By providing an objective, quantifiable measure of academic achievement that is independent of family connections, wealth, or social status, standardized tests serve as a meritocratic mechanism that expands access and creates a level playing field in college admissions.

**Supporting Evidence:**

The 2024 Taiwan study (PMC) using data from all 12,526 applications to National Taiwan University in 2022 found that for the majority of students (72%), admission outcomes would remain unchanged under a holistic system versus test-score-only system. Among the 28% whose outcomes would change, class background is not clearly related to the direction of change. Instead, women are the unequivocal beneficiaries of reduced emphasis on standardized testing [15]. The study concludes that shifting from test-only to holistic admissions does not necessarily alter socioeconomic inequality in elite college access [15].

A University of Chicago economic study concludes that 40 percent of America's increased prosperity from 1960-2010 can be explained by "better identification and allocation of talent" through meritocratic systems including standardized testing [27].

In China, the Gaokao was introduced as a merit-based route, promising social mobility, equality, and development [12]. The common saying "Without the Gaokao, how could you compete with the second generation of the rich?" highlights the Gaokao's role as a counterweight to wealth and privilege [8]. The system is seen as a "fate-changing contest" offering social mobility for millions of students [12]. Education plays a pivotal role in facilitating social mobility, acting as a key driver for individuals to transcend socioeconomic barriers and enhance their life opportunities [60].

In Japan, the term "examination hell" reflects the high stakes of entrance exams, but also the recognition that exam performance, not family background, determines educational and career opportunities [11]. Cheating is taken extremely seriously: the Yomiuri Shimbun warned in an editorial about a 2010 cheating scandal: "This is a heinous act that undermines the fairness that should be the basis of the university entrance system" [11].

The compact from the current administration's "Compact for Academic Excellence in Higher Education" incentivizes schools to require standardized testing or risk losing federal funding, stating that signatory institutions must require all applicants to take "a widely-used standardized test (i.e., SAT, ACT, or CLT)" in order to ensure that "university admissions decisions shall be based upon and evaluated against objective criteria" [44].

**Analysis and Comparative Insights:**

The meritocracy argument is particularly strong in the Chinese context, where the Gaokao is widely viewed as the only fair path to social advancement for rural and disadvantaged students. The comparative data showing that China's exam-based system produces more equitable access to elite education than the US holistic system (2.3 times vs. 11 times wealth advantage) provides powerful evidence for the meritocratic function of standardized tests.

The Taiwan study adds important nuance: shifting from test-only to holistic admissions does not necessarily reduce socioeconomic inequality. This finding challenges the assumption that test-optional policies inherently improve equity and suggests that the relationship between admissions criteria and social class is more complex than often assumed.

### Pro Argument 5: Standardized Tests Provide Actionable Data for Teachers to Improve Instruction

**Thesis:** Standardized test results offer teachers objective, diagnostic feedback about their students' strengths and weaknesses, enabling data-driven instructional adjustments that improve learning outcomes.

**Supporting Evidence:**

Oxford Learning notes that standardized testing provides "data to help teachers improve instruction" and serves as a "progress checkpoint for students to track year-over-year growth" [2]. The Fordham Institute argues that tests provide teachers with actionable data to address individual student needs, acting as a "flashlight" on what works (per Secretary Cardona) [5].

The Center for American Progress discusses through-year assessments, which are tests administered throughout the year with results aggregated into a summative score. Louisiana and Georgia are piloting this approach. "The idea in both states is that the tests will return data throughout the year that teachers can use to shape instruction while kids are still in the classroom, rather than getting scores after the school year is over" [3].

The ETS Global article explains: "Standardized tests not only reveal areas where students need more support but also offer teachers an opportunity to reflect on their own teaching practices" [29]. "Standardized test results are more than just numbers on a page; they are a valuable tool that can help teachers, school leaders, and policymakers make informed decisions that improve education for everyone" [29].

In China, teacher financial well-being is tied to student Gaokao performance, forcing a strong alignment between teaching and tested content. While this has negative consequences, it also means teachers are highly motivated to ensure students master the tested curriculum [6]. Schools modify curricula around Gaokao content, and local governments shape policies to boost Gaokao results [6].

In Japan, the introduction of written sections for Japanese and Math in the new Common Test (2021) aims to move beyond rote memorization toward assessing thinking, judgment, and expression skills [40]. The Todai Robot Project was launched in 2011 by the National Institute of Informatics (NII) to develop an AI system capable of passing the University of Tokyo entrance exams, testing the exam's ability to measure genuine academic competence [11].

**Analysis and Comparative Insights:**

The diagnostic value of standardized tests is often underappreciated in public debate. When properly designed and administered, tests provide teachers with information about which concepts students have mastered and where they need additional support. This is particularly valuable for identifying struggling students early and providing targeted interventions.

The through-year assessment pilots in Louisiana and Georgia represent a promising evolution in testing design. By providing data throughout the year rather than only at the end, these assessments can inform instruction while students are still in the classroom. This addresses a common criticism that standardized tests come too late to help teachers adjust their teaching.

### Pro Argument 6: Standardized Tests Drive International Competitiveness and Enable Cross-National Comparisons

**Thesis:** Standardized testing regimes, particularly the high-stakes national exams in East Asian countries, correlate with top performance on international assessments like PISA, demonstrating that such systems can drive high levels of educational attainment and international competitiveness.

**Supporting Evidence:**

PISA 2022 results (released December 2023) included approximately 700,000 students from 81 countries. The top-performing countries were Singapore (1,679 aggregated points), China (1,605), Japan (1,599), Taiwan (1,599), and South Korea (1,570). The United States ranked 18th with 1,468 [25]. Singapore recorded the highest mean score in all three domains: 575 in mathematics, 543 in reading, and 561 in science [51].

Four education systems—Japan, Korea, Lithuania, and Chinese Taipei—could be considered "resilient" with regard to mathematics performance, equity, and well-being [52]. Japan performed exceptionally well across all three domains: mathematics, science, and reading [52].

China's Gaokao has contributed to China's top PISA rankings (2009, 2012, 2018), though critics argue this reflects over-investment in study rather than true competence [6]. China's PISA 2022 aggregated score was 1,605, second only to Singapore [25].

In the United States, despite grim headlines about "tanking" math scores, the US moved up in rankings for all three subjects (math, reading, and science) [22]. Harry Feder (FairTest) argues that PISA is largely a measure of childhood poverty rather than academic achievement. The US leads the OECD in child poverty at approximately 26%. When comparing only US schools with poverty rates under 10% (comparable to top-performing countries like Finland, Denmark, and Slovenia), the US average reading score would be 562, placing it first globally. The actual US reading score was 465 [22].

PISA's influence on education policy has been significant, creating new knowledge, driving changes in national assessment policies, and exerting external influence over national education policy [21]. In Germany, the results of the first PISA assessment caused the so-called "PISA shock"—a questioning of previously accepted educational policies [21].

**Analysis and Comparative Insights:**

The correlation between high-stakes testing systems and top PISA performance is striking. East Asian countries with the most rigorous exam cultures consistently outperform other nations on international assessments. While correlation does not equal causation, the evidence suggests that testing systems that create strong incentives for academic achievement contribute to higher levels of student learning.

The US case is instructive: despite spending more per student than most countries, US performance is mediocre on international comparisons. However, when controlling for child poverty rates, US students in low-poverty schools perform at world-class levels. This suggests that the US testing and accountability system, while imperfect, is not the primary cause of mediocre performance. Rather, systemic poverty and inequality are the main drivers.

## Part II: Con Arguments — Six Ways Standardized Tests Harm Education

### Con Argument 1: Standardized Tests Encourage "Teaching to the Test" and Narrow the Curriculum

**Thesis:** Standardized testing, particularly when attached to high-stakes consequences, forces schools and teachers to narrow the curriculum to tested subjects and adopt "teaching to the test" pedagogical approaches, which reduces instructional time in non-tested subjects, limits higher-order thinking, and ultimately harms educational quality.

**Supporting Evidence:**

Curriculum narrowing occurs when the core academic subjects of reading and mathematics are given priority over other subjects, including science, social studies, physical education, foreign languages, and the arts (Dr. Andrew J. Parr, Washington State Board of Education, 2018) [6]. A meta-analysis by Au (2007) found that approximately 70% of studies reported curriculum narrowing, 50% reported knowledge fragmentation, and 65% reported increased teacher-centered pedagogy. Only about 25% reported positive reactions like curriculum expansion [6].

Post-NCLB data from 2001-02 to 2007-08 shows weekly instructional minutes for reading/ELA/math increased 35% (from 642 to 872 minutes), while all other content areas decreased 31% (from 918 to 635 minutes). Social studies dropped by 75 minutes (-31%), science by 74 minutes (-33%), art/music by 54 minutes (-35%), and PE by 40 minutes (-35%) [6].

Narrowing of curriculum and instruction happens most to low-income and minority students (IDRA/FairTest, 2002) [9]. The term "apartheid education" describes how affluent students receive richer, more intellectually challenging curricula while low-income students focus on memorization and test-taking techniques [6]. Curriculum narrowing undertaken to improve the test scores of poor and minority students "may actually magnify the achievement gaps between the low income and their middle and upper class peers" [6].

In China, under the Gaokao system, teaching is forced to the test because "teacher financial well-being is tied to student Gaokao performance" (Cheng & Hamid, Language Testing Asia, March 2025) [17]. Schools modify curricula around Gaokao content, and local governments shape policies to boost Gaokao results [17]. In the 2017 Zhejiang Gaokao pilot reforms, "the school crams two years of classes into one year," as reported by student Lin Gezhi (Sixth Tone, 2017). Some schools cut non-gaokao subjects and extended classroom hours, while students choose subjects based on scoring potential rather than interest [71].

In Japan, "examination hell" (juken jigoku) has long been criticized for encouraging rote memorization over critical thinking. The former dean of Keio University's graduate business school, Noritake Kobayashi, stated: "Applicants are asked to sacrifice the development of their independent thinking and composition capabilities to meet the need for memorizing as many of the right answers as possible" [32]. The Prime Minister's Conference on Culture and Education in 1984 concluded that education has become "entirely biased to entrance examinations," stressing "cramming" more than discovery and learning [32].

**Analysis and Comparative Insights:**

The evidence for curriculum narrowing is consistent across all three countries. In the US, the shift in instructional time away from non-tested subjects is well-documented, particularly in schools serving low-income students. In China, the Gaokao system drives schools to compress curricula and eliminate non-tested content. In Japan, the "examination hell" culture has been criticized for decades for prioritizing memorization over critical thinking.

The equity implications are particularly concerning. Curriculum narrowing disproportionately affects low-income and minority students, who are more likely to attend schools that respond to testing pressure by reducing enrichment opportunities. This creates a two-tiered education system where affluent students receive a broad, engaging education while disadvantaged students receive a narrow, test-focused curriculum.

### Con Argument 2: Standardized Tests Have Negative Impacts on Student Mental Health, Creativity, and Critical Thinking

**Thesis:** High-stakes standardized testing inflicts severe psychological harm on students, including anxiety, depression, stress, and in extreme cases suicide, while simultaneously stifling creativity and critical thinking by prioritizing rote memorization and test-taking skills over deeper learning.

**Supporting Evidence:**

A 2018 research study found that cortisol (stress hormone) levels rise 15% on average during high-stakes testing, linked to an 80-point drop in SAT scores. For students facing hardships, cortisol spiked 35%, distorting cognitive function and creating a "stress bias" [15]. A 2021 study linked high-stakes tests to poor sleep, unhealthy behaviors (caffeine overuse, smoking, poor diet), and increased anxiety [15]. Test anxiety affects 10-40% of K-12 students, and 70% of educators say tests are not developmentally appropriate [5].

In China, 81% of children worried "a lot" about exams; 63% feared teacher punishment; 73% have been physically punished by parents due to low academic achievement; over a third of students reported experiencing psychosomatic symptoms at least once a week (Cheng & Hamid, 2025) [17]. Chinese students rank lowest among compared countries in psychological need satisfaction and highest in need frustration [17]. The exam culture fosters jealousy, distrust, hostility among peers, and marginalizes underachieving students [17].

An 18-year-old girl in Anhui province died by suicide in April 2025, leaving a suicide note describing being "broken" by relentless exams. She wrote: "I cannot bear how you meticulously checked the scores of all the examinations and tests. I cannot bear how my scores appeared frequently in the text messages between you and your friends and relatives" [28]. Official statistics show suicides among Chinese children and adolescents are rising, with experts citing excessive schoolwork and the cultural belief that academic achievement is the only path to success as key factors [28].

In Japan, the education system is "often described as a 'pressure cooker' due to its heavy focus on academic performance and competitive entrance examinations" (Kameda, European Journal of Education and Pedagogy, February 2025) [65]. 40% of middle school students report feeling a constant sense of inadequacy regarding their performance [65]. Japan leads the world in school-related suicides among 15-to-19-year-olds [32]. Takehisa Kishimoto, a student who spent three years as a "ronin" (masterless samurai) retaking exams, described himself as "psychologically damaged" by the pressure: "They expected me to be a study machine, not a human being. There was pressure everywhere—at home and at school. I had nowhere to escape" [32].

Traditional standardized tests often emphasize recall over reasoning. Many questions measure how well students can remember facts, formulas, and procedures rather than how they apply or interpret them [12]. "Critical thinking—analyzing, evaluating, and synthesizing information—is far more difficult to measure but essential for lifelong learning" [12]. "When assessments focus solely on 'right answers,' they can discourage deeper exploration and creativity" [12].

**Analysis and Comparative Insights:**

The mental health crisis linked to high-stakes testing is most severe in East Asian countries, where the cultural emphasis on exam performance is most intense. The Chinese data is particularly alarming: 81% of children worry "a lot" about exams, 73% have been physically punished by parents over low achievement, and suicide is the leading cause of death among teenagers. The Japanese data is similarly concerning, with the country leading the world in school-related suicides.

The US data, while less extreme, shows that test anxiety affects a significant minority of students and that high-stakes testing can cause measurable physiological stress responses. The impact on creativity and critical thinking is harder to quantify but widely acknowledged by educators and researchers.

The cultural dimension is crucial: In China and Japan, the belief that exam performance determines life outcomes is deeply ingrained, making test-related stress particularly acute. The comparative evidence suggests that the psychological costs of testing are highest in systems where the stakes are highest.

### Con Argument 3: Standardized Tests Contain Cultural and Socioeconomic Biases

**Thesis:** Standardized tests are not objective measures of student ability or potential; they are deeply embedded with cultural, racial, and socioeconomic biases that systematically disadvantage students from low-income families, students of color, and other marginalized groups, reflecting and reinforcing existing inequalities rather than measuring academic merit.

**Supporting Evidence:**

Standardized testing in the US is rooted in racist eugenics. Psychologist Carl Brigham used data from the Army's WWI intelligence tests (which he developed) in his 1923 book "A Study of American Intelligence" to argue for white superiority, and just three years later oversaw the adaptation of the Army tests into the first SAT [45]. In the 1920s, eugenicist Carl Brigham invented the SAT [45]. After Brown v. Board of Education (1954), Southern states used standardized tests as a legal tool to maintain segregation. South Carolina became the first state to require standardized tests for public university admission in 1954, with others following [45].

Attorney Jay Rosner discovered in 2000 that out of 276 pre-tested SAT questions that made it into official exams, White students outperformed Black students on every single one. Rosner concluded that a selection bias was at work: questions that Black students excelled at were systematically excluded because they didn't correlate with overall high-scoring test-takers (who were disproportionately White, male, and wealthy), creating a self-reinforcing cycle of racial disparity [45].

Ibram X. Kendi calls standardized tests "the most effective racist weapon ever devised" [45]. "The standardized tests arrived right on time, in every period, with new theories and measures to justify racial hierarchy" [45].

Students from the top 1% of household incomes are 13 times as likely to earn a 1300 or higher on the SAT as those from the bottom 20% (Georgetown University Feed, October 2023, citing Opportunity Insights data) [57]. Among all students, one-third of the highest-income students scored 1300+, compared to less than 5% of middle-class students and just 0.6% of the lowest-income quintile [57]. "Scores from the SAT and ACT tests are good proxies for the amount of wealth students are born into" (2016 analysis, cited in Edutopia, 2022) [15].

In China, the Gaokao reinforces socioeconomic inequalities, as families spend over $120 billion annually on tutoring and prep courses [16]. Regional inequalities are stark: Shanghai has 79% admission rates, Beijing 77%, and Tianjin 66%, while the agricultural province of Henan has only 47% admission despite having 1.36 million candidates and just one top-tier university [27]. Only 2% of test-takers gain places at the 39 highest-ranking schools, and chances at Tsinghua or Peking University are about 5 in 10,000 [27].

In Japan, more than half of Japanese students attend private after-school tutoring centers (juku) to prepare for exams, raising significant equity concerns [25]. Over 90% of elite university students are male while junior colleges are over 90% female [32]. Educational sociologist Kariya Takehiko warned that yutori policy's emphasis on diversity and personal responsibility would actually expand social class gaps [5].

**Analysis and Comparative Insights:**

The evidence for bias in standardized testing is compelling and spans all three countries. In the US, the historical roots of testing in eugenics and segregation are well-documented, and contemporary data shows persistent racial and socioeconomic disparities in test scores. The bias is not merely a product of different educational opportunities—it is built into the test design itself, as demonstrated by Rosner's findings about question selection.

In China, the Gaokao's bias is primarily regional: students from wealthy coastal provinces have vastly better educational resources and higher admission rates than those from poor inland provinces. The $120 billion tutoring industry exacerbates these disparities.

In Japan, the juku system creates a parallel education market where wealthier families can buy additional preparation, and the gender imbalance in elite university admissions suggests systemic bias.

The common thread across all three countries is that standardized tests reflect and reinforce existing social hierarchies rather than providing a neutral measure of merit.

### Con Argument 4: Standardized Tests Do Not Reliably Predict Future Success

**Thesis:** Standardized test scores are weak predictors of college success, career outcomes, and life achievement compared to other measures such as high school GPA, and the emphasis on these tests in admissions and accountability systems is therefore misplaced.

**Supporting Evidence:**

A landmark study published in Educational Researcher (January 2020) by the University of Chicago Consortium on School Research found that high school GPAs are five times stronger than ACT scores at predicting college graduation. Researchers Elaine Allensworth and Kallie Ann Clark examined 55,084 Chicago Public Schools students. The predictive power of GPAs was consistent across high schools, while ACT scores showed no connection to college graduation at many schools. At some schools, students with the highest ACT scores were less likely to succeed in college [52].

"The bottom line is that high school grades are powerful tools for gauging students' readiness for college, regardless of which high school a student attends, while ACT scores are not" (co-author Elaine M. Allensworth) [52]. "Extensive time spent preparing for standardized tests will have much less payoff for postsecondary success than effort put into coursework, as reflected in students' grades" [52].

The UC system's own 2020 study found SAT scores accounted for only 13-21% of variance in freshman GPA; ACT scores accounted for 14-22%. High school GPA was a stronger predictor of retention and graduation [51].

What tests don't measure: "SAT and ACT scores can't predict a student's entire college experience. They don't measure whether you'll find the right major, build healthy study routines, ask for help, manage stress, or recover after a hard semester" [51]. "The tests do not measure character, spirit, heart, soul, potential" (Diane Ravitch) [4].

However, contrasting research from Opportunity Insights (2025) found that SAT/ACT scores predict first-year college GPA with a normalized slope four times greater than that from high school GPA at Ivy-Plus colleges. Students scoring 1600 (SAT) achieve a first-year GPA 0.43 points higher (1 SD) than students scoring 1200. Lower-scoring students are 42 percentage points more likely to struggle academically [53]. This research found that "high school GPA has relatively little predictive power for first-year academic success. A perfect 4.0 high-school GPA predicts only a 0.1 point higher first-year GPA compared to a 3.2 high-school GPA" [53].

**Analysis and Comparative Insights:**

The predictive validity of standardized tests is a subject of genuine academic debate. The University of Chicago study found that GPA is a much stronger predictor of college graduation at non-selective institutions, while the Opportunity Insights study found that test scores are more predictive at elite institutions. Both findings may be true: the predictive value of tests may vary depending on the selectivity of the institution and the range of student abilities being considered.

What is clear is that neither test scores nor GPA alone provide a complete picture of student potential. The debate highlights the limitations of using any single metric for high-stakes decisions. The most defensible approach, supported by the evidence, is to consider multiple measures—including test scores, GPA, and other factors—in admissions and evaluation decisions.

### Con Argument 5: Standardized Testing Exacerbates Educational Inequality and the "Achievement Gap"

**Thesis:** Rather than closing achievement gaps, high-stakes standardized testing systems exacerbate educational inequality by disproportionately harming low-income students and students of color, reflecting and reinforcing socioeconomic disparities, and creating an accountability system that punishes disadvantaged schools without providing the resources needed to address underlying inequities.

**Supporting Evidence:**

Low SES students perform roughly 1 standard deviation lower (about 3 academic years behind) higher-income students (Ballard Brief, Fall 2022) [56]. Low SES students enter high school with literacy skills 5 years behind wealthier peers [56]. The dropout rate is 7.2% for low SES vs. 3.6% for mid/high SES students [56]. Only 14% of low-income students graduate college vs. 60% of high-income [56]. The achievement gap has cost the US economy more than all the combined recessions since the 1970s [56].

"The current data used to measure student success...closely correlates to race, income, and family educational attainment. It really only tells us about advantage and disadvantage outside school, not what schools are actually doing" (MCIEA co-founder Jack Schneider, quoted in NEA) [10].

Curriculum narrowing undertaken to improve test scores of poor and minority students "may actually magnify the achievement gaps between the low income and their middle and upper class peers" [6]. "When schools narrow the curriculum to gain a few points on a test, the school should be viewed as cheating their students, especially those from low income households, of a richer future" [6].

The NCLB goal of 100% proficiency by 2014 was widely seen as unrealistic. By 2011, 82% of schools were expected to fail NCLB's proficiency goals [1]. The NEA stated: "In its relentless focus on measuring outcomes with test scores, NCLB failed to provide the resources to ensure that every student had the opportunity to learn and excel" [60].

In China, the Gaokao reinforces socioeconomic inequalities as families spend over $120 billion annually on tutoring [16]. Stark regional inequalities exist: Shanghai 79% admission, Beijing 77%, Henan 47% [27]. Rural-urban inequality persists, with top students and teachers flowing from less developed regions to big cities.

In Japan, more than half of Japanese students attend juku, raising equity concerns about access to supplementary education [25]. The government has introduced community-based alternatives (Chiiki Mirai Juku) to address this [25].

**Analysis and Comparative Insights:**

The evidence that testing systems exacerbate inequality is strong across all three countries. In the US, the testing and accountability regime under NCLB failed to close achievement gaps despite two decades of effort, and the narrowing of curriculum in high-poverty schools may have actually widened gaps. In China, the Gaokao system allows wealthy families to purchase advantages through tutoring, and regional disparities in educational resources produce large inequalities in outcomes. In Japan, the juku system creates a parallel market for educational advantage.

The fundamental problem is that tests measure the outcomes of inequality but do not address its causes. Schools in high-poverty areas cannot overcome the effects of poverty through testing alone, yet accountability systems punish them for low scores. This creates a system that labels disadvantaged students and schools as failures without providing the resources needed to succeed.

### Con Argument 6: Reform Efforts in All Three Countries Reveal Deep Problems with Standardized Testing

**Thesis:** Reform efforts in all three countries—including China's attempts to reform the Gaokao, Japan's relaxation of curricula under yutori kyoiku (and its subsequent backlash), and the US movement toward test-optional college admissions—demonstrate the deep and persistent problems with high-stakes standardized testing, but also reveal the difficulty of moving away from these systems.

**Supporting Evidence:**

**China's Gaokao Reforms:** The State Council issued "Implementation Guidelines on Deepening the Reform of the Examination and Enrollment System" in September 2014, initiating reforms focused on holistic student development and personalized growth [22]. Key reforms include abolishing the arts-science division, allowing students to freely choose subjects under "3+3" or "3+1+2" models, and implementing a "two bases + one reference" admission framework where Gaokao scores and high school proficiency exam grades serve as the two bases, and an Overall Quality Evaluation system serves as the reference [22].

However, the Zhejiang pilot reforms allowing multiple test attempts actually increased stress: "The school crams two years of classes into one year" (student Lin Gezhi, Sixth Tone, 2017) [71]. Students now face preparing for exams over up to nine test days spread across two years. "Scores are still the decisive factor in the current system. It is difficult to ease the students' burden" (Liu Haifeng, director of Xiamen University's Institute of Education, Sixth Tone, 2017) [71]. Fewer students chose to take physics in high school due to the difficulty of getting a high rank, and university teachers complained that engineering students from Zhejiang did worse in basic college physics than their peers from other provinces [19].

The Double Reduction Policy (2021) aimed to reduce homework and after-school tutoring pressure. While the tutoring industry shrank dramatically (offline institutions down 83.8%, online down 84.1%), extra academic lessons have simply shifted from private tutoring centers into public schools [3]. The policy has been criticized for exacerbating the equity gap, as middle/low-income and rural students lost access to affordable tutoring while wealthy families continue to access expensive, unregulated private tutoring [2].

**Japan's Yutori Kyoiku:** Yutori education (yutori-kyōiku) reduced the hours and content of the primary education curriculum, aiming to reduce academic stress and address issues like school violence. However, after Japan's PISA scores declined in 2003 and 2006, the Yutori curriculum was widely criticized as a failure. The "Yutori generation" (born late 1980s-late 1990s) continues to face stigma and consequences [21]. A 2024 study by Bai and Tanaka reported that individuals who experienced more years under the relaxed education system had lower earnings, and were less likely to hold full-time or skilled employment in adulthood [21].

The education ministry eventually backed down, and the 2012 guidelines restored class hours and curriculum [22]. The Japanese experience demonstrates the difficulty of reducing testing pressure in a society where exam performance is deeply tied to life outcomes.

**US Test-Optional Movement:** During the COVID-19 pandemic, the number of four-year institutions with test-optional policies nearly doubled from 713 to 1,350 since spring 2020 [48]. Over 80% of four-year colleges in the United States now classify themselves as test-optional [40]. However, several elite institutions have reinstated testing requirements after reviewing internal data, including Brown, Dartmouth, Harvard, Yale, MIT, Caltech, Cornell, Stanford, and Princeton [44].

The 2025 NBER working paper by Friedman, Sacerdote, Staiger, and Tine found that students who do not submit test scores achieve significantly lower levels of academic performance in college, equivalent to students who submitted SAT scores of roughly 1300. The authors argue that test-optional policies may disadvantage less-advantaged students who withhold scores that could help them [50].

**Analysis and Comparative Insights:**

The reform efforts in all three countries reveal a common pattern: attempts to reduce testing pressure or move away from standardized tests face significant obstacles. In China, Gaokao reforms have created new problems (increased stress from multiple test attempts, avoidance of challenging subjects) without solving the underlying culture of exam competition. The Double Reduction Policy has pushed tutoring underground rather than eliminating it.

In Japan, the yutori education experiment was abandoned after a perceived decline in academic standards, and the backlash against "relaxed education" has been severe. The Japanese experience suggests that societies may not accept reductions in testing pressure if they perceive that academic standards are falling.

In the US, the test-optional movement has been partially reversed by elite institutions that have found test scores to be valuable predictors of academic success. The debate between test-optional and test-required policies continues, with strong evidence on both sides.

The common lesson is that testing systems are deeply embedded in educational and social structures, and reforming them is extraordinarily difficult. The demand for objective measures of academic achievement persists, and attempts to eliminate or reduce testing often create new problems.

---

## Part III: Conclusion

The evidence presented in this analysis demonstrates that the question of whether standardized tests improve education cannot be answered with a simple yes or no. Both the pro and con arguments have substantial empirical support, and the answer depends on context, implementation, and values.

**Key Findings:**

1. **Standardized tests provide essential data** for accountability, equity monitoring, and instructional improvement. Without them, educational disparities become invisible, and there is no objective basis for targeting resources or interventions.

2. **Standardized tests have predictive validity** for college success and later-life outcomes, particularly at selective institutions. Test scores help identify talented students from disadvantaged backgrounds who might otherwise be overlooked.

3. **Standardized tests promote meritocracy** in systems where family connections and wealth would otherwise determine educational opportunities. China's exam-based system provides more equitable access to elite education than the US holistic system.

4. **However, standardized tests cause significant harm** to student mental health, particularly in high-stakes systems like China and Japan. The psychological costs are severe and include anxiety, depression, and suicide.

5. **Standardized tests narrow the curriculum** and encourage "teaching to the test," particularly in schools serving low-income students. This creates a two-tiered education system where disadvantaged students receive a narrow, test-focused education.

6. **Standardized tests contain systemic biases** related to race, class, and region. The historical roots of testing in eugenics and the contemporary evidence of bias in test design raise serious equity concerns.

7. **Reform efforts face significant obstacles** in all three countries. The demand for objective measures of academic achievement persists, and attempts to eliminate or reduce testing often create new problems.

**The Way Forward:**

The evidence suggests that the most productive path forward is not a binary choice between keeping or eliminating standardized tests, but rather a process of redesign, recalibration, and integration into broader assessment ecosystems. Promising developments include:

- **Through-year assessments** that provide data throughout the year to inform instruction
- **Performance-based assessments** that measure deeper learning skills
- **Multiple measures** that combine test scores with GPA, portfolios, and other indicators
- **Reduced stakes** that maintain the diagnostic value of tests while reducing their harmful effects

As the Fordham Institute concludes: "The current moment is not a simple return to high-stakes exams, nor a continuation of the post-pandemic pullback. Instead, testing is being reworked" [16]. The challenge for policymakers in China, Japan, the United States, and beyond is to design testing systems that preserve the benefits of objective measurement while minimizing the harms of high-stakes pressure.
