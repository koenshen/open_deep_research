# The Transformation of American Retail: Mall Closures, E-Commerce, and Reinvention (2005–2026)

## Overview

American retail has undergone a structural transformation over the past two decades. What began as a slow erosion of department stores in the mid-2000s accelerated into a full-blown "retail apocalypse" in the 2010s and reached an inflection point during the COVID-19 pandemic of 2020. The pandemic compressed roughly five years of e-commerce adoption into a few months [10][94], pushed mall vacancy to record highs, and forced communities across the country to confront what to do with millions of square feet of vacated retail space.

Yet the story is not simply one of decline. Since 2022, physical retail has staged a partial comeback: e-commerce penetration has stabilized at roughly 17% of retail sales (Census Bureau definition), meaning about 83 cents of every retail dollar is still spent in stores [54][67]; foot traffic has recovered to near pre-pandemic levels at many surviving shopping centers [66][67]; and a wave of creative reuse—from Amazon fulfillment centers to medical campuses to apartment conversions—has begun to remake the landscape. Retail still supports more than 1 in 4 American jobs (55 million workers) and contributes $5.3 trillion to annual U.S. GDP, per the National Retail Federation [98], but the sector's center of gravity has shifted decisively toward discount, grocery, experience-driven, and online-integrated formats [43][79].

This report examines four dimensions of this transformation: (1) community and economic impacts, (2) the fate of vacant retail space, (3) the evolution of shopping habits, and (4) creative reuse solutions and case studies.

## 1. Community & Economic Impact

### 1.1 Retail Employment: A Sector in Structural Decline

Retail trade remains one of the largest private-sector employers in the United States—roughly 15.44 million workers as of July 2026 [1]—but its trajectory has been flat-to-declining for a decade. Employment grew from a Great Recession low of 14.4 million in 2010 to a peak of 15.8 million in 2017, then lost about 200,000 jobs from 2017 to 2019 as e-commerce competition, thin margins, and technological disruption took their toll [2]. The pandemic then delivered a shock: retail lost almost 800,000 jobs in 2020, with clothing stores accounting for 38.6% of the sector's losses [2]. The BLS Current Employment Statistics series recorded a decline of 459,000 retail trade jobs in 2020 [3]. The recovery has been partial: by mid-2026 retail employment stood at 15.44 million, still below its 2017 peak [1], and monthly job reports in early 2026 showed renewed retail job losses—the Revelio Labs February 2026 report found the U.S. economy lost 16,600 jobs, driven primarily by declines in retail trade and leisure/hospitality [95].

The losses are concentrated in specific segments. General merchandise stores and clothing stores each accounted for roughly 29% of the 2017–2019 contraction; sporting goods, hobby, book, and music stores added 19.3% [2]. Department stores have been in freefall for two decades: they have lost 18 times more workers than coal mining since 2001 [4], cut more than 200,000 jobs in 2012 alone, and eliminated roughly 80,000 more jobs between 2013 and 2017 even as online retailers added about 100,000 [5]. Between 2012 and 2016, roughly 75% of U.S. counties lost department store jobs, and four-fifths of those counties lost out on net (department stores shed more jobs than e-commerce added locally) [5].

The jobs that replace retail are different in kind and location. Courier/messenger employment grew 54.4% and warehousing/storage grew 86.1% from 2010 to 2019 [2]. The average online retail job paid slightly over $50,000 in 2012 (exceeding $59,000 by 2016) versus about $20,500 for department store jobs [5]; fulfillment centers pay about 26% better than general retail [4]. But these jobs are concentrated in a few logistics hubs rather than spread across every county—King County, Washington (home to Amazon) saw nonstore employment grow from 1.4% to 2.8% of county employment between 2012 and 2016 [5].

The BLS projects the decline will continue. Retail trade is projected to lose ~587,000 jobs (4.0% of 2020 employment) over 2020–2030, the largest projected decline of any sector [2]. The January 2026 industry projections show retail trade is one of only four sectors (along with mining, government, and manufacturing) expected to lose jobs over 2024–2034, shedding 181,900 jobs (-1.2%), with sales and related occupations declining by 297,800 jobs [8].

### 1.2 The Uneven Burden: Women, Wages, and Geography

The retail job losses have fallen disproportionately on women. The Institute for Women's Policy Research found that women lost 160,300 retail jobs between October 2016 and October 2017 while men gained 106,000—women shouldered the entirety of the losses, concentrated in general merchandise stores where women fell from 64% to 60.4% of workers [6]. During the pandemic, women made up 48.7% of the retail workforce but accounted for 65.2% of retail job losses between February and June 2020; 2.4 million retail jobs were lost between February and April 2020, 1.4 million of them held by women [7]. Women in retail earn $34,000 annually vs. $42,000 for men, and 32.5% work part-time [7].

Geographically, the losses concentrate in exactly the places that depend most on retail. New York City's retail sector lost 27% of its employment from February to April 2020 (vs. 15% nationally), with clothing stores losing 25,000 jobs and department stores 5,600 [10]. Manhattan—home to 46% of city retail jobs and two-thirds of clothing store employment—bore 59% of the February-to-June decline [10]. Long-term NYC trends mirror the nation: food/beverage store jobs rose 27% and drug stores 28% from 2009–2019, while e-commerce employment more than doubled; clothing and electronics stores fell 8% each from 2014–2019 [10].

Seasonal retail hiring—a critical source of first jobs and holiday income—has also shrunk. The holiday buildup averaged 605,000 employees during 2018–2021 but only 475,000 during 2022–2024, and retailers now retain far fewer seasonal workers after the holidays [9].

### 1.3 Small Business Survival Rates

The pandemic was devastating for small retailers. Yelp data found 163,735 business closures between March 1 and August 31, 2020, with 60% (97,966) permanent; retail/shopping accounted for 30,374 closures, of which 17,503 (58%) were permanent [11][12]. Restaurants lost 32,109 businesses (61% permanent) [11]. An earlier Yelp report found ~140,000 closures by June 15, 2020, with more than 20% of businesses closed in April having reopened [96]; by the fourth quarter of 2020, restaurant openings had recovered to near pre-pandemic levels, but retail openings remained 25% below 2019 levels [97].

Academic studies quantify the asymmetric impact. A national survey of more than 5,800 small businesses (March 28–April 4, 2020) found 43% had temporarily closed and employment had fallen 39% on average; the median firm had only about two weeks of cash on hand [13]. Using confidential administrative data covering all California sales-tax filers, Fairlie et al. found small business closure rates jumped from 4.6% in 2019Q2 to 8.5% in 2020Q2, while large business closures rose from 1.3% to 2.7%—a 3.3 percentage-point COVID-induced increase for small firms vs. 1.3 for large firms. The disproportionate closing of small businesses led to increased market concentration among larger firms that only partially reversed [14]. Federal Reserve Board research found economy-wide "excess" establishment exits were likely below 200,000 in the pandemic's first year—with full-service restaurants experiencing a 13.5% cumulative exit rate (about 70% above normal) and "other services" (barbershops, nail salons) seeing ~123,000 excess exits—while grocery and electronics stores saw lower-than-normal exits [15].

At the same time, entrepreneurship surged: 2020 saw the highest number of new business applications since the series began in 2004, with applications in non-store (internet) retail rising over 200% from early-2020 lows [16]. The Census Bureau's Small Business Pulse Survey found 74% of businesses reported decreased revenue in the first week, and a K-shaped recovery emerged—accommodation/food services remained badly depressed while other sectors recovered [16].

### 1.4 Local Tax Revenue and Municipal Budgets

Retail is not just jobs—it is the fiscal lifeblood of many American municipalities. The International Council of Shopping Centers (ICSC) estimates that malls and shopping centers provide **$400 billion in local tax revenue annually** [17]. Mall owners are often the largest taxpayers in their communities: PREIT (21 malls) pays over $65 million annually in real estate taxes and estimates its Pennsylvania and New Jersey malls support about 17,000 jobs; Simon Property Group pays over $60 million in property taxes on Long Island properties alone [17]. Even at-risk malls matter: average C- and D-rated malls generate $90–145 million in annual sales, producing $1.8–3 million per year in local sales tax at a 2% rate—and there are roughly 730 B-, C-, and D-rated malls in the U.S. [17].

The Lincoln Institute of Land Policy notes property taxes account for 72% of local tax revenue, and retail is about one-quarter of commercial property value; in some communities dependent on malls, they can make up 20–30% of the tax base [18]. When malls fail, the consequences are direct:

- **Bangor, Maine (Bangor Mall):** The mall's assessment fell roughly 25% per year from $60.9 million (2017) to $34.6 million (2019), and its tax bill dropped below $1 million for the first time in two decades. It sold at auction in February 2019 for $12.6 million—less than half its assessed value—after Simon Property Group defaulted on an $80 million loan [18].
- **Sheboygan, Wisconsin (Memorial Mall):** The city lost $1.3 million in annual tax revenue when the mall successfully challenged its assessment; the mall closed in 2017 [18].
- **Franklin, Tennessee (CoolSprings Galleria):** The city tapped budget reserves because of lost mall revenue, with the city administrator comparing the situation to the 2008 recession [17].
- **Crestwood, Missouri (Crestwood Court):** More than half vacant; sales taxes made up about half of the city's $14 million in 2008 revenue, and collections fell nearly 10% the prior year [19].
- **Onondaga County, New York (ShoppingTown Mall):** Assessment dropped from $53 million (2008) to $36 million (2014); the owner owed $9.7 million in back taxes [18].

Tennessee's experience illustrates the broader pattern: local option sales taxes provided about 11% of county revenues on average (up to 33% in Sevier County), and during and after the Great Recession, inflation-adjusted local sales tax revenues fell in 75 of Tennessee's 95 counties [20]. During the pandemic, consumer spending fell 35% in restaurants/hotels, nearly 69% in entertainment/recreation, and nearly 65% in transportation [20].

Not all communities suffer equally. Bangor offset its mall's decline with a thriving downtown including a $22 million bank campus and new apartments [18]; Sheboygan benefited from Acuity Insurance's corporate expansion. As Lincoln Institute Fellow Ron Rakow put it: "In some communities dependent on malls, they can make up 20 to 30 percent of their tax base... If the mall isn't doing as well, the community is either going to have to reduce services or increase taxes for others" [18].

### 1.5 Community, Social, and Property-Value Impacts

Malls were never merely shopping venues—they were third places. A 2023 survey of 1,005 Americans found 50% miss going to the mall; 61% want a revival of traditional shopping malls; 62% believe mall closures negatively impacted their local economy; and 1 in 10 say mall closures have impacted their social life [21]. The survey also found 68% of Americans live within an hour of a "dead mall" and 2 in 5 live near two or more [21].

Research on anchor store closures shows a "domino effect": when an anchor closes, co-tenancy clauses allow other tenants to vacate, foot traffic to remaining businesses drops, and municipal tax bases shrink [17][91]. A George Mason University study of a major electronics retailer that closed 200+ stores found net losses far exceeded the sales of the shuttered locations—an average net monthly loss of roughly $209,317 per closed store—because closures sent a negative signal about the brand and drove consumers to competitors [23]. Conversely, a Harvard Business School study of 1,196 malls found anchor quality matters enormously: Apple's entry increases the profit/entry probability of upscale department stores while decreasing that of discount stores, establishing Apple as the "ultimate anchor store" [22].

On residential property values, the evidence is nuanced. A Georgia State University study of King County, Washington found negative externalities from retail extend only about 700–950 feet; beyond roughly 235 feet, proximity to retail actually enhances home values by up to ~$9,800, peaking at ~560 feet—suggesting that well-planned retail is an amenity, not a nuisance [25]. But a shuttered mall is different: neighborhoods near dead malls face crime, blight, and stigma [24]. The decline of anchor stores has also been linked to the broader shrinkage of the middle class, as warehouse clubs (Costco, Sam's Club) and supercenters (Walmart) grew more than online shopping itself from 1999 to 2013 [24].

## 2. The Fate of Vacant Retail Space

### 2.1 National Vacancy Statistics

The U.S. has roughly 1,000 enclosed malls—down from about 2,500 in the 1980s—and Moody's Analytics tracks about 700 of them [26][37][38]. Their vacancy rate hit a **record 11.4% in Q1 2021**, up from 10.5% in Q4 2020—a 90-basis-point quarterly jump that was the largest ever recorded by Moody's commercial real estate division, surpassing the previous record spike of 80 basis points in Q1 2009 [26]. "Malls are absolutely still on the ropes. They were on the ropes even before Covid," said Moody's Victor Calanog [26]. For context, mall vacancy had previously peaked at 10.3% in Q1 2010 after the Great Recession, fallen to a six-year low of 8.0% by Q4 2015 [34], then climbed again to 9.1% in Q3 2018—the highest since 2011—as Sears and Bon-Ton closings hit [27].

Different data providers measure differently. The mall-specific vacancy rate has since improved: Colliers reported mall vacancy of 8.9% in Q1 2025, improving to 8.5% in Q4 2025 [28][29]. The broader "shopping center" vacancy rate tracked by Moody's (including strip centers) has been remarkably stable at 10.3–10.4% from Q4 2024 through Q1 2026 [30][31], while Cushman & Wakefield's national retail vacancy measure stood at 6.0% in Q2 2026, well below the 7.4% historical average [32]. The differences reflect methodology: Moody's tracks a broader inventory of older shopping centers, while CoStar/Colliers/C&W track all retail property types including single-tenant net-lease buildings, which have vacancy rates as low as 2.7% [29][33]. What all sources agree on is that the U.S. is dramatically over-retailed: the retail stock totals roughly 8.5 billion square feet—the majority of it general retail space such as freestanding drugstores and grocery stores—or 24.5 square feet per capita, vs. 4.5 in Europe and 2.8 in China [18][49][50]. Retail vacancy has also bifurcated by property quality: 75% of national vacancy sits in Class B or C product [34].

### 2.2 Mall Closures and Anchor Store Departures

The anchor store exodus has been the primary driver of mall distress. Between 2016 and 2020, 360 mall-based department stores closed [90]. The bankruptcy wave includes:

- **Sears:** From 3,500+ stores and 355,000 employees in 2006 to bankruptcy in October 2018 with 68,000 employees [35]
- **J.C. Penney:** Chapter 11 in May 2020, closing hundreds of stores [35]
- **Bon-Ton, Toys "R" Us (2018), Forever 21 (2019):** Forever 21's bankruptcy closed 200+ stores; its 2025 "Chapter 22" liquidation shuttered all 354 U.S. stores [40][43]
- **2020 pandemic bankruptcies:** Neiman Marcus, Lord & Taylor, Brooks Brothers, J.Crew, Pier 1 Imports, Tuesday Morning, GNC, Stage Stores, Stein Mart [94][35]
- **Bed Bath & Beyond (2023), Rite Aid (2023/2025), Joann (2025), Party City (2024), Big Lots (2024/2025):** The 2025 liquidations of Joann (~800 stores), Rite Aid (~1,240), Party City (~700), Big Lots, and Forever 21 drove more than half of all 2025 closure square footage—about 127 million square feet [43]
- **Macy's:** Announced in 2024 it will close 150 underperforming stores through 2026; closed 66 in 2025 and announced 14 more in January 2026 [41]

Coresight Research's store closure tracking shows the waves: 2019 saw 9,302 announced closures (a record at the time); 2020 was forecast at 20,000–25,000 [94]; the pandemic year produced 9,698 tracked closures [103]; 2024 saw 7,325 [103]; 2025 saw 8,270 closures vs. 5,270 openings (a net loss of ~3,000) [43][103]. Retail Dive reported closure plans up over 300% year over year in early 2025 [83]. For 2026, Coresight projects ~7,900 closures and ~5,500 openings [79][101]. As of early May 2026, more than 1,200 closures had already been announced [101][102].

Predictions of mass mall mortality have been frequent: Credit Suisse predicted in 2017 that over 25% of malls (200+) would close by 2022 [35][90]; Coresight estimated in 2020 that 25% of America's roughly 1,000 malls would close within three to five years [39]; Capital One Shopping projects only 900 of ~1,200 malls will remain by 2028 [37]. Yet the count has proven stickier than predicted—the roughly 1,000–1,150 malls have stayed relatively consistent since 2019, and ICSC's Tom McGee observed, "If the pandemic didn't cause a dramatic decline in the number of these centers, then almost nothing will" [90]. What has happened instead is a sharp bifurcation. Some analysts argue the "retail apocalypse" narrative is overhyped: Cambridge Associates notes roughly 4,100 stores were actually opened on a net basis in 2017 despite closure headlines [93], and the Cato Institute cites NBER research using smartphone geolocation data showing the number of general merchandise stores actually increased during the 2010s as dollar stores and discounters replaced traditional department stores [92].

### 2.3 The Great Divide: Class A vs. B/C Malls

The mall market has split into winners and losers:

- **Class A malls** (with sales exceeding $500/sq ft) had vacancy of just 5.6% in 2026; **Class C malls** (below $300/sq ft) had 13.3% vacancy [47]
- Coresight found top-tier malls average 95.5% occupancy vs. 89% for lower-tier centers [46]; by 2022, top-tier occupancy was 95.1%, with minimum base rents growing in the mid-teens [45]
- Green Street Advisors identifies roughly 380 C- and D-rated malls that are "not viable retail centers long term"; an A++ mall can generate up to $1,000/sq ft vs. ~$320 for a C+ mall [39]
- Simon Property Group, owner of the highest-quality portfolio, ended 2025 at 96.4% mall/outlet occupancy with rents up 4.7% [43]
- Placer.ai found top-tier mall traffic was up 12% in 2022 vs. 2019, driven by long-distance visitors, while weaker malls closed and redistributed traffic to survivors [45][65]

The "survival of the fittest" dynamic means the headline vacancy rate understates the problem: nearly 40% of available retail space is rated two stars or below, and less than a quarter was built after 2000 [52]. The losers are concentrated in the Midwest, Northeast, and parts of California, Florida, Ohio, New York, and Michigan [43].

### 2.4 Total Retail Square Footage and the Construction Collapse

The U.S. retail stock peaked at roughly 8.5 billion square feet in 2019 [49]. Since then, the market has been rationalizing through demolition and conversion: more than 400 million square feet of retail space was demolished since 2009 [48], with 165+ million square feet demolished since 2018 [53]. New construction has collapsed from ~300 million square feet per year in the early 2000s to ~81 million annually since 2020—and only ~21 million square feet of new first-generation space is available for lease, less than 5% of total availability [29][53]. Retail under construction is at its lowest level since 2011 [28]. New development deliveries fell to 19.6 million square feet in 2025, on pace to be the third weakest year this century [51]. One broader measure puts total U.S. retail space at 11.8 billion square feet including all commercial building types [99].

This supply discipline is why overall retail vacancy has remained historically low (4.1–4.3%) even as demand shifted: since end of 2019, occupied retail space has grown only 1.3%, yet monthly sales per square foot reached a record $24.96 in the first half of 2025—more than 30% above pre-pandemic levels [52][53]. Retail vacancy rose from 4.0% to 4.1% in 2024, then jumped to 4.3% by Q3 2025, with negative net absorption of 10.6 million square feet in the first nine months of 2025 [51].

### 2.5 The Post-COVID Squeeze (2024–2026)

The 2024–2026 period saw a wave of "Chapter 22" bankruptcies—companies that emerged from prior Chapter 11 filings only to fail again—dominated by private-equity-owned retailers: 71% of the largest consumer discretionary bankruptcies of 2025 involved private equity ownership at filing [43]. The 2025 liquidations (Joann, Rite Aid, Party City, Big Lots, Forever 21, plus Saks Global's January 2026 Chapter 11) dumped ~127 million square feet onto the market [43]. Yet landlords absorbed much of it: Kimco re-leased ~90% of its Party City/Joann/Big Lots/Rite Aid space at 30–40% releasing spreads; Simon re-tenanted Saks Off 5th at a +67% mark-to-market spread; Brixmor posted 38.7% new-lease cash spreads for 2025 [43]. Discount retailers, grocers, and fitness operators absorbed the boxes: Aldi (~180 new stores in 2025), Dollar Tree (+388), Dollar General (+196), Burlington (104 net new stores, over 40% from bankrupt-tenant recaptures), Planet Fitness (181 new clubs), and Crunch Fitness (91 clubs, 4.27 million square feet) [43].

The result is a market that is tight nationally but deeply uneven locally. The most stressed markets in 2025: New Orleans (+240 bps to 6.3% vacancy), Buffalo (+240 bps to 10.8%), Pittsburgh (+200 bps), Providence (+170 bps), Milwaukee (+170 bps). The strongest: Charlotte (3.4% vacancy, +7.6% rent growth), Raleigh-Durham (3.1%), Phoenix (+964,553 sq ft of absorption) [43]. Western U.S. urban retail vacancy stood at 7.3% in Q2 2026, up ~330 basis points since 2019, while suburban vacancy rose only ~60 basis points to 5.0%—a direct consequence of remote work emptying downtowns [53].

## 3. Evolution of Shopping Habits

### 3.1 E-Commerce's Share of Retail Sales, 2005–2026

The U.S. Census Bureau's quarterly e-commerce series tells the story of a slow burn followed by a sudden leap. E-commerce accounted for just 0.19% of retail sales in 1998 and 3.6% in 2008 [61][62]. By 2013 it had reached 5.9%; by 2018, 9.7% [59]. Annual e-commerce volume grew from roughly $91 billion in 2005 to $338 billion in 2015 to $569.4 billion in 2019 [59][60]. Since 1999, e-commerce has grown every year; the only quarters with year-over-year declines were Q1–Q2 2009 during the financial crisis [62]. Then came COVID-19.

In Q1 2020, e-commerce was 11.9% of retail sales; in Q2 2020, it surged to 16.3%—the largest quarterly jump in the series' history [58]. Coresight Research estimated the pandemic accelerated the shift to online retailing by about five years [10][94]. Annual volumes: $807.1 billion in 2020 (+41.7%), $950.5 billion in 2021 (+17.8%), and $1.012 trillion in 2022—the first year e-commerce crossed the trillion-dollar mark [59]. The penetration rate dipped slightly in 2022 (the only annual decline on record) and then resumed its climb: roughly 15% (2023), 16.0% (2024), 16.4% (2025), and **17.1% in Q2 2026** on a seasonally adjusted basis [54][55][56][59]. On a not-seasonally-adjusted basis, Q4 (holiday) quarters run much higher—18.3% in Q4 2025—because of the seasonal concentration of online holiday shopping [57].

It is important to note measurement differences. The Census Bureau's 17% figure excludes spending categories that rarely sell online (restaurants, gas, autos) and, since the April 2025 benchmark revision, excludes nonemployer firms. Digital Commerce 360, which excludes categories that don't typically sell online, calculates e-commerce at 23.1% of 2025 retail sales [60]. Even by the most generous measure, however, roughly three-quarters of retail spending still occurs in physical stores—and by the Census measure, about 83 cents of every retail dollar is spent in stores [54][67]. E-commerce penetration varies sharply by category: nonstore retailers are 70.7% online, clothing 6.9%, sporting goods/books 6.0%, furniture 3.3%, food and beverage 2.9% [63].

### 3.2 Foot Traffic: Collapse and Recovery

Physical store visits collapsed in spring 2020: year-over-year foot traffic fell 26% in March, 45% in April, and 30% in May 2020, remaining down 13–18% monthly through the rest of the year [64]. Apparel sales fell 53% in March, 89% in April, and did not post a single month of year-over-year growth after March 2020 [64]. Mall visits had already been declining for years—mall visits fell 50% between 2010 and 2013 [35]—but the pandemic hollowed out the weakest properties.

The recovery has been uneven but real. By Q1 2024, open-air shopping centers were the first format to exceed pre-pandemic visitor levels; by Q2 2025, indoor mall visits were just 1.1% below Q2 2019 levels, their strongest performance since 2020 [66][67]. In the first half of 2025, indoor malls led with visits up 1.8% year-over-year, open-air centers grew 0.6%, and outlet malls declined 0.8% [66]. The 2025 holiday season saw open-air centers up 1.7% year-over-year, and in the first half of 2026 all three mall formats grew visits: open-air +4.7%, indoor +1.9%, outlets +1.0% [67]. Visit durations have lengthened—indoor malls posted a 3.3% dwell-time increase in H1 2025—suggesting more purposeful trips and greater engagement with dining and entertainment [66]. PassBy's analysis of 36.9 billion modeled mall visits found community centers led growth at +1.22% while outlet centers declined 3.24% [68]. Abakus Analytics notes footfall in 2022 rose just 1% year-over-year and has never fully returned to pre-pandemic levels at many properties [71].

However, the recovery is not universal. Q1 2025 visits to indoor malls were still 7.4% below Q1 2019 [65]. Office-centered urban retail remains depressed: nationwide office visits were still down 27.4% vs. June 2019 as of mid-2025 [105], and San Francisco and Seattle have seen the slowest retail recoveries due to remote work [53]. One analysis identified a sharp foot-traffic decline beginning in June 2025—a 10%+ drop in weekly pedestrian traffic in the second half of 2025 after four years of gains—attributed primarily to Hispanic consumers avoiding public spaces amid immigration enforcement fears [70]. Behavioral shifts also persist: Thanksgiving-day mall visits have essentially ended, and the share of mall visits occurring on weekdays rose from ~60% (2019) to over 70% (2022) as remote workers adopted malls as de facto offices and gyms [69].

### 3.3 Omnichannel: BOPIS, Curbside, and Stores as Fulfillment Nodes

The most durable behavioral change of the pandemic era is omnichannel shopping—the blurring of online and physical retail. Buy-online-pick-up-in-store (BOPIS) has become mainstream: **97.2 million Americans (34.2% of consumers) regularly use BOPIS**, making it the third most-used fulfillment method among online shoppers [74]. U.S. click-and-collect sales grew from $35 billion in 2019 to $154 billion in 2025, and are projected to reach $292 billion by 2030 [74]. During the pandemic, BOPIS grew 554% year-over-year in May 2020 [76]; 68% of U.S. shoppers used BOPIS at least once in 2024, up from 57% in 2022 [75]. Critically, BOPIS drives incremental sales: 85% of BOPIS shoppers make an additional purchase while collecting their order [75].

Curbside pickup, nearly nonexistent before 2020 (only about 4% of the 500 largest retailers offered it), was adopted by 55.1% of the top 1,000 retail chains by 2021 [76][77]. It has since been rationalized—only 24.8% of the top 1,000 chains still offered it in 2024—but it remains a permanent feature for grocery and big-box retailers, with ~72 million Americans using curbside in the past year and $54.3 billion in 2025 sales [77]. Virtually all major grocers and big-box chains (Target, Walmart, Kroger, Home Depot) maintain the service; grocery curbside usage doubled from 6% (2022) to 12% (2024) [77].

The strategic significance is that stores have become fulfillment nodes. Target's same-day ecosystem (Drive Up, in-store pickup, rapid delivery) generated more than $14 billion in sales and roughly two-thirds of Target's digital revenue in fiscal 2025, and Target's 2026 stores will fulfill 95% of its online orders [75][79]. Walmart's store-fulfilled models contributed to a $2.5 billion monthly e-commerce run rate by late 2024 [77]. Academic research found that adding curbside pickup extends a store's competitive reach from 1.5–2 miles to up to 8 miles, and that consumers will order from stores as far as 33 miles away [78]. Mobile devices now influence 70% of in-store purchases [72].

### 3.4 The Back-to-Brick-and-Mortar Movement

Since 2022, there has been a genuine—if selective—return to physical stores. Retailers opened more stores than they closed in 2022 (net +1,575) and 2023 (net +295), the first net-positive years since 2016 [42]. E-commerce growth slowed from 41.7% (2020) to single digits, and by 2024–2025 the penetration rate had essentially plateaued [54][59]. Placer.ai's analysis of Walmart, Target, Costco, and Dollar General found the big-box giants' combined share of physical retail traffic rose from 16.8% (2019) to 17.5% (Q1 2026); visits per store in 2025 exceeded pre-pandemic levels by 18.1% for Costco and 10.2% for Dollar General [73].

The 2025–2026 wave of closures (8,270 in 2025; ~7,900 projected in 2026) is best understood not as the death of physical retail but as "right-sizing" and a shift toward value and experience formats [43][79][82]. Coresight's Deborah Weinswig: consumers "have no patience for stores that are constantly disorganized, out of stock, and that deliver poor customer service" [82]. The retailers opening stores in 2026 are predominantly discount and grocery chains: Dollar General (450 new stores), Aldi (180+ new stores, toward a 3,200-location goal), Tractor Supply (~100), Ollie's Bargain Outlet (75), Barnes & Noble (60, a notable brick-and-mortar turnaround), Target (30+ new stores plus a $5 billion investment, on the way to 300 new locations by 2035), Costco (26–28 net new), BJ's Wholesale Club (25–30), Nordstrom Rack (13+), Uniqlo (11), and L.L. Bean (8) [79][80][81]. Even Starbucks, which closed hundreds of stores in its 2025 restructuring, plans 150–175 net new U.S. company-operated locations in 2026 [80]. Core retail sales were up 4.72% year over year in July 2026, marking the ninth consecutive month of sales growth per the CNBC/NRF Retail Monitor [84].

The data support the "physical is back" narrative in relative terms: e-commerce penetration has been roughly flat at 16–17% (Census) since 2021 [56], foot traffic has recovered to within a few points of pre-pandemic levels at surviving centers [66][67], and per-square-foot sales productivity reached record highs [52]. As the GrowthFactor analysis notes, "the 'retail apocalypse' story is now two years out of date" [67].

### 3.5 Generational and Behavioral Shifts

Younger consumers are driving the physical retail revival. Circana data shows shoppers aged 18–24 bought 62% of their total general merchandise in stores (vs. 52% for those 25+), and 60% of Gen Z and 53% of Millennials visited a mall in the past three months [40]. Online-only brands like Edikted have opened physical mall locations [40]. Gen Z and Millennials also lead BOPIS adoption (39.1% and 35.9% of users, respectively) [74].

The secondhand/resale market has become a major force reshaping apparel retail. ThredUp's 14th Annual Resale Report found the global secondhand market reached $257 billion in 2025 (up 13.1%) and is projected to hit $393 billion by 2030; the U.S. secondhand market grew 13.3% to $55.5 billion—nearly four times faster than the broader retail clothing market [85]. Gen Z and Millennials will drive more than 70% of market growth through 2030; 62% of Gen Z shopped secondhand in 2025, and 67% consider resale value before buying new apparel (up from 47% the prior year) [85][86]. Nearly 60% of retailers believe not having a resale presence creates a structural competitive disadvantage [85].

## 4. Creative Reuse Solutions & Case Studies

As dead and dying malls accumulate, American cities and towns have turned to an extraordinary range of adaptive reuse strategies. The economics are unforgiving: converting a shuttered mall to a warehouse or residential complex can reduce its value by 60–90% [89], and closed malls sit empty for ~4 years on average, selling at 43% below their acquisition price [47]. But the examples below demonstrate that creative reuse can work—and in some cases, produce more tax revenue and community benefit than the mall ever did.

### 4.1 Housing and Mixed-Use Redevelopment

**Crosstown Concourse, Memphis, Tennessee** — The most celebrated example of mall-to-community conversion. This 1.5-million-square-foot former Sears facility was converted into a "vertical urban village" containing 265 apartments, a YMCA, a high school, a community college satellite, offices, health providers, theaters, and restaurants. The project was made possible by 30 forms of public, private, and philanthropic funding [17][87]. It has become a national model for mixed-use reuse.

**Northgate Station, Seattle, Washington** — Simon Property Group is pairing retail with 234 apartments at this redeveloped center, with housing opening in 2026—part of a broader trend of adding residential density to surviving malls [67].

**Collin Creek Mall, Plano, Texas** — Purchased for $10 million, this dead mall is being redeveloped into $1 billion in commercial development including 15–20 restaurants and housing, aided by a property tax incentive [18].

**Smith Haven Mall, Long Island, New York** — Simon Property Group has been redeveloping this property with new non-retail uses including residential and entertainment components [65].

### 4.2 Fulfillment Centers and Industrial Conversion

The e-commerce boom has made dead malls attractive to logistics operators—their large floor plates, ample parking, and highway access are ideal for last-mile fulfillment.

**Randall Park Mall, North Randall, Ohio** — Once the largest mall in the country (opened 1976), it closed in 2009 and was demolished in 2015; Amazon subsequently built a distribution center on the site [88].

**Rolling Acres Mall, Akron, Ohio** — Closed and demolished with nearly $500,000 in city-funded demolition costs; Amazon bought the site for a distribution center [24][88].

Amazon, FedEx, DHL, and UPS have converted numerous dead mall anchors and entire malls into fulfillment centers across the country [36]. Moody's identified five markets where retail-to-warehouse conversion makes the most sense: Central New Jersey, Northern New Jersey, Long Island, Memphis, and Detroit [39]. The tradeoff is stark: warehouses typically employ far fewer workers than the malls they replace, and property values fall 60–90% [89]—but they generate stable industrial rent and put otherwise dead land back on the tax rolls.

### 4.3 Healthcare and Medical Conversions

**100 Oaks Mall, Nashville, Tennessee** — Vanderbilt University Medical Center converted this struggling mall into an outpatient clinic campus, the most prominent "mall-to-medicine" conversion in the country [36][90]. The conversion kept the property occupied and provided accessible medical services.

**University of Rochester Medical Center, Rochester, New York** — The medical center converted a vacant mall into clinical and research space, a model replicated by health systems nationwide seeking cheap, accessible, single-story space [36].

Healthcare systems have become major buyers of dead mall space because it offers large floor plates, abundant parking, and central locations. The Richmond Fed notes that converting vacant department store space into "experiential" uses—including medical offices, gyms, libraries, and entertainment venues—has become the dominant adaptation strategy for surviving malls [90].

### 4.4 Education, Civic, and Community Uses

**Highland Mall, Austin, Texas** — This enclosed mall was converted into the Highland Campus of Austin Community College (ACC), which now anchors the redeveloped property alongside retail and office uses [36]. The conversion made ACC one of the largest community college campuses in the country and preserved a major tax base.

**Windsor Park Mall, San Antonio, Texas** — Converted into the global headquarters of Rackspace Technology (a cloud computing company), an early and influential example of office/tech reuse [36].

**University Place, Chapel Hill, North Carolina** — This shopping center was transformed with a children's museum, health club, CrossFit studio, and specialty food store, supported by flexible zoning and local government partnership [18].

**Crosstown Concourse (see above)** — Includes a public high school and college satellite, demonstrating educational reuse at scale [17][87].

Religious congregations and community organizations have also moved into vacant retail spaces—former big-box stores and mall anchors offer the large, open, affordable spaces that churches and nonprofits need [87]. Other notable conversions include a former Sears that became a Xerox call center in Florida and Ford offices that moved into a former Lord & Taylor in Michigan [17].

### 4.5 Entertainment, Experiential, and Hospitality

The most successful malls are reinventing themselves as entertainment and hospitality destinations.

**Phipps Plaza, Atlanta, Georgia** — Simon Property Group's redevelopment added a Nobu Hotel and Restaurant, a Life Time athletic club, Citizens Market food hall, and an office tower to the upscale mall, demonstrating the "experiential anchor" strategy [45].

**The Mall at Green Hills, Nashville, Tennessee** — Taubman's $200 million renovation (completed 2019) added 132,000 square feet of inline retail, a new Dillard's, and a 70,000-square-foot RH (Restoration Hardware) gallery [45].

**Southdale Center, Edina, Minnesota** — The nation's first enclosed mall (1956) now hosts Life Time Work coworking space, part of a wave of fitness/coworking conversions [27].

Fitness has been one of the strongest backfill categories: fitness space in malls is up 70% since 2013 [90]; Planet Fitness opened 181 new clubs in 2025 and Crunch Fitness leased 4.27 million square feet [43]. Dick's Sporting Goods opened 16 new House of Sport and 20 Field House experiential locations in 2025 [43]. Entertainment uses—movie theaters, arcades, climbing walls, and upscale dining—have "moved from add-on to anchor" [67].

**Dillard's acquisition of Longview Mall, Longview, Texas** — In an unusual move, department store chain Dillard's purchased the struggling mall outright to control its own destiny—a sign that some retailers see value in mall ownership [40].

### 4.6 The Retail-to-Retail Reinvention

Not all reuse involves abandoning retail. The strongest malls are densifying with new retail formats:

- **Off-price and discount retail** has been the biggest growth story: Burlington, Ross, and TJ Maxx collectively opened 339 stores in 2024; Dollar General, Five Below, Ulta, and Sephora lead expansion [92][26]
- **Experiential tenants** (restaurants, gyms, theaters, arcades, libraries) now fill former department store spaces in A-tier malls [90]
- **Successful super-regional malls** continue to thrive: Houston's Galleria (30+ million annual visitors), King of Prussia (22 million visitors, 450 stores), Mall of America (40+ million visitors), and Aventura Mall in Florida (ranked #1 in USA Today's 2025 10Best) [47]
- **Simon Property Group** ended 2025 at 96.4% occupancy with rents up 4.7% [43]

### 4.7 What Works: Lessons from the Case Studies

The most successful conversions share common elements:

1. **Location matters.** Malls in growing Sun Belt metros (Plano, Austin, Nashville) attract redevelopment capital; malls in shrinking Rust Belt cities often end up as warehouses or demolition sites [18][43].
2. **Institutional anchors work.** Hospitals, universities, and tech companies have the balance sheets to finance large conversions (Vanderbilt, ACC, Rackspace, Crosstown Concourse) [36][17].
3. **Mixed-use density is the goal.** The highest-value redevelopments add housing, offices, and civic uses rather than trying to replicate retail (Crosstown Concourse, Northgate Station, Collin Creek) [17][67][18].
4. **Public-private partnership is essential.** Crosstown Concourse required 30 forms of funding; Collin Creek used property tax incentives; North Randall and Akron used public demolition funds [17][18][88].
5. **Policies that support transition**—flexible zoning, density bonuses, tax incentives—are key to local retail health. As Lincoln Institute fellow Marc Moffitt put it: "Malls as a property type are dead. It is not if, it's when they go under and are ready for redevelopment... Investors view malls as mixed-use redevelopment opportunities better able to serve the community, and they are going to provide a much more robust sales and property tax base" [18].

## 5. Conclusion

The American retail landscape in 2026 looks very different from 2005. The enclosed mall—once the third place of American suburbia—has been reduced from roughly 2,500 properties to about 1,000, and only a few hundred of those are truly healthy [37][38][47]. E-commerce captured the growth of the past two decades but has stabilized at roughly 17% of retail sales (Census definition), leaving physical stores with 83% of spending [54][67]. The pandemic was not the cause of the retail apocalypse but its accelerant: it compressed five years of e-commerce adoption into months [10][94], pushed mall vacancy to a record 11.4% [26], and delivered a body blow to small businesses and municipal budgets from which many communities are still recovering [11][13][14].

The long-run picture is one of rationalization rather than extinction. The U.S. built two to five times more retail space per capita than any comparable country [18][49], and the market is slowly working off that overhang through demolition, conversion, and the reallocation of space to discount retailers, grocers, fitness operators, and non-retail uses [43][48]. The communities that have fared best are those that treated dead malls not as lost retail but as development opportunities—turning Sears warehouses into urban villages, dead malls into medical campuses, and empty boxes into fulfillment centers [17][36][87].

For the communities still grappling with a shuttered mall, the evidence offers a clear playbook: embrace the transition, assemble public-private partnerships, allow density and mixed uses, and recognize that the mall's highest and best use may no longer be shopping at all [18][90].
