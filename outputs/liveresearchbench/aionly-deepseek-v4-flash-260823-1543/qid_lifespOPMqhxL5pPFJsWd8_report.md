# High-Altitude Pulmonary Edema (HAPE) in Ski Mountaineers at Altitudes ≥3,000 m: Pathophysiology, Documented Cases, Treatment, and Prevention

## 1. Epidemiology and Risk Context in Ski Mountaineering

High-altitude pulmonary edema (HAPE) is a life-threatening, non-cardiogenic pulmonary edema that typically occurs in lowlanders who ascend rapidly to altitudes above 2,500–3,000 m. It is the most common cause of death related to high-altitude exposure, and it is completely reversible if recognized early and treated properly [1][4]. HAPE usually manifests within 2–4 days of ascent, most frequently after the second night at altitude, and is characterized by progressive dyspnea, cough, weakness, chest tightness, and eventually pink frothy sputum in severe cases [2][14].

Incidence varies dramatically with altitude and ascent rate:

- **~2,500 m:** 0.01%–0.1% [3]
- **3,500–5,500 m (very high altitude):** 0.6%–6% [32]
- **4,500 m:** 0.2% (gradual ascent over 4 days) vs 6% (ascent over 1–2 days); 1.7% overall among those ascending to ~4,000 m [7][64]
- **5,500 m:** 2.5% (trekking) vs 15.5% (airlifted soldiers) [7]
- **Himalayan trekkers/Alpine climbers:** ~4% with ascent >600 m/day [4]
- **Mount Everest trekkers:** ~1.6% [3]
- **Colorado ski resort visitors:** ~1 in 10,000 skiers; 0.01%–0.1% [2][4]

Ski-specific exposure data are particularly relevant. Many U.S. and Canadian ski resorts have base elevations at or above the 2,500 m (8,200 ft) threshold where HAPE risk begins, and a Colorado ski area at 2,928 m documented 150 HAPE cases over a 39-month period [5][6]. Arapahoe Basin, for example, holds the highest summit of any U.S. ski area at 13,050 ft (~3,978 m). Colorado hosted a projected 14 million skier visits in 2023–24, making altitude illness a substantial public health issue in the ski industry [8][60].

Key risk factors include: prior history of HAPE (the strongest predictor; recurrence risk 60% with rapid ascent above 4,500 m), male sex (84% of ski-resort HAPE cases were male), rapid ascent rate, cold exposure, heavy physical exertion, high sleeping altitude, patent foramen ovale (PFO), and pre-existing cardiopulmonary disease [2][3][5][7]. Physical fitness is not protective; the biggest risk factor is ascending too quickly [6]. Untreated HAPE carries a mortality of up to 50%, and treated mortality in resource-limited settings can reach 11%, although prompt treatment with descent and oxygen typically yields complete recovery with no permanent lung damage [2][3].

## 2. Predominant Pathophysiological Mechanisms

### 2.1 Hemodynamic Mechanisms: Exaggerated Hypoxic Pulmonary Vasoconstriction and Capillary Stress Failure

The central and best-established mechanism in HAPE is an **exaggerated hypoxic pulmonary vasoconstriction (HPV)** leading to excessive pulmonary artery pressure (PAP), elevated pulmonary capillary hydrostatic pressure, and mechanical "stress failure" of the alveolar-capillary barrier [10][11][12][13]. In HAPE-susceptible individuals, mean pulmonary artery pressure at altitude reaches 36–51 mm Hg, and pulmonary capillary pressure averages ~22 mm Hg — levels sufficient to produce protein- and erythrocyte-rich edema fluid [12]. Right-heart catheterization studies at the Capanna Margherita (4,559 m) confirmed elevated capillary pressure with normal left atrial (wedge) pressure, proving the edema is hydrostatic and non-cardiogenic [10][12][23].

A critical refinement of the paradigm is that HPV is **non-uniform** across the pulmonary vascular bed. Areas with weaker vasoconstriction receive over-perfusion and high pressure, producing the characteristic patchy, often right-sided or bilateral radiographic opacities, while the edema results from high flow plus high pressure exceeding the structural capacity of capillaries in those regions [10][14]. Microsphere studies in swine and MRI studies in humans support this "overperfusion" mechanism [10][14].

The hemodynamic paradigm is supported by the landmark randomized trial showing that prophylactic nifedipine (a pulmonary vasodilator) reduced HAPE incidence from 63–64% to 10% in susceptible mountaineers [13][44]. This remains the strongest clinical evidence that elevated PAP is causally central to HAPE [13].

**Exercise and cold — specific modifiers in ski mountaineers.** Heavy physical exertion after rapid ascent causes greater increases in PAP and is a key precipitant in ski mountaineering [14][19]. A Doppler-echocardiography study found that the change in pulmonary arterial systolic pressure (PASP) from baseline to hypoxic exercise discriminated HAPE-susceptible from HAPE-resistant mountaineers with no overlap (19.2–30.4 mm Hg vs 6.7–18.5 mm Hg, cut-off ~19 mm Hg) [16]. HAPE-susceptible individuals also show greater increases in pulmonary artery pulse pressure with exercise, indicating increased proximal artery stiffness [17]. Cold exposure independently elevates PAP: short-term cold exposure increased pulmonary arterial pressure in Kyrgyz highlanders, and cold weather is consistently listed as a HAPE risk factor [18][19][20]. Together, the ski-mountaineering scenario — rapid ascent, intense leg exercise at altitude, and cold air — is a "perfect storm" of hemodynamic stressors; the documented Sierra Nevada case (Section 3.3) directly illustrates this [32].

### 2.2 Genetic Susceptibility Factors

HAPE susceptibility is partly heritable, but no single variant determines risk and genetic testing has no established clinical role [3][22][23]. Evidence from candidate-gene studies includes:

- **ACE I/D polymorphism:** A case-control study in Indian males found the DD genotype in 55% of HAPE-susceptible subjects vs 27.5% of controls (D allele frequency 65% vs 38.75%, p=0.005), and a meta-analysis of published/unpublished data supported increased HAPE risk in D-allele carriers [21][23]. However, the association has **not** been replicated in Caucasians (Dehnert et al. 2002), indicating population-specific heterogeneity [22]. The ACE I allele is thought to confer adaptation; the D allele increases risk [23].
- **eNOS (NOS3) Glu298Asp (G/T):** The TT (mutant) genotype was found in 22.5% of HAPE-susceptible vs 7.5% of controls, with the G allele predominating in controls (80% vs 56.25%, p=0.002), consistent with reduced nitric oxide bioavailability as a susceptibility mechanism [21][23].
- **Endothelin-1 (EDN1) G(8002)A:** The AA genotype was higher in HAPE-susceptible subjects (42.5% vs 17.5%), and the A-3A haplotype was associated with increased risk [21].
- **VEGF C(936)T and C(-634)G:** No significant association in the Indian study [21].
- **EPAS1 and EGLN1:** Gene-gene interaction studies in Japanese populations and high-altitude-adapted populations (Sherpas/Tibetans with protective EPAS1 haplotypes, Andeans with EGLN1 mutations) support a role for the hypoxia-sensing pathway in susceptibility and resistance [23][30]. HSP70 genotypes and variations in EPAS1, VEGFA, BNIP3L, and ANGPTL4 are also associated with susceptibility [30].
- **Mitochondrial DNA:** Haplogroups D4 and B4b confer resistance; B4c increases susceptibility [23].

Overall, the genetic evidence is low-to-moderate quality (small case-control studies, population-specific heterogeneity, few replication studies), and no genetic test is currently recommended for risk stratification [3][22].

### 2.3 Endothelial Dysfunction: Nitric Oxide, Endothelin-1, and Sympathetic Activation

A consistent physiological signature of HAPE susceptibility is **reduced pulmonary nitric oxide (NO) bioavailability** combined with **excess endothelin-1 (ET-1) activity**:

- Measurements of exhaled NO, BAL nitrite/nitrate, and forearm NO-dependent endothelial function all point to reduced NO availability in hypoxia as a major cause of the excessive PAP rise in HAPE-susceptible individuals [10].
- HAPE-susceptible mountaineers show augmented sympathetic activation (muscle sympathetic nerve activity) during hypoxia and at altitude, and exaggerated endothelin release; endothelin release can be inhibited by NO, linking the two systems [24].
- Lower exhaled NO correlates with higher systolic PAP at altitude (R = −0.50), and NO levels fall during the first days of acclimatization [25].
- HAPE-susceptible subjects have reduced exhaled NO, elevated ET-1 after 1–2 days at altitude, and higher resting and hypoxic-exercise PASP [14][25].
- Patent foramen ovale, found >4x more frequently in HAPE-prone than HAPE-resistant mountaineers, can worsen hypoxemia and create a vicious cycle of hypoxemia and pulmonary hypertension [25][33].

Inflammation in HAPE is now understood as a **secondary consequence, not the primary cause**. In incipient HAPE, catheterization/BAL studies demonstrate leakage of high-molecular-weight proteins and erythrocytes across the alveolar-capillary barrier "in the absence of any evidence of inflammation" [10]. In established HAPE, BAL shows marked increases in high-molecular-weight proteins, erythrocytes, and leukocytes (mostly alveolar macrophages), with elevated inflammatory mediators — consistent with a secondary inflammatory response to the protein-rich edema and mechanical injury [26]. A 2002 JAMA study explicitly concluded that inflammation is not an etiologic factor [3][15].

### 2.4 Impaired Alveolar Fluid Clearance and Sodium Transport

A second, complementary pathophysiological defect in HAPE is **impaired alveolar fluid clearance (AFC)** due to reduced transepithelial sodium transport. Hypoxia reversibly inhibits epithelial sodium channels (ENaC) and Na,K-ATPase, the molecular engines of AFC [27][28]. Mechanistic studies show that short-term hypoxia triggers mitochondrial ROS → Ca²⁺ entry via CRAC channels → CAMKK-β → AMPK-α1 → PKC-ζ → phosphorylation and rapid endocytosis of the Na,K-ATPase α-subunit, while sustained hypoxia down-regulates mRNA and protein of both Na,K-ATPase and ENaC [27]. Hypothermia (relevant in ski mountaineering) further impairs AFC [25].

The pivotal clinical evidence for this mechanism is the randomized, placebo-controlled trial by Sartori et al. (2002): prophylactic inhalation of the β₂-agonist **salmeterol decreased HAPE incidence by more than 50%** in susceptible subjects, supporting the concept that stimulating sodium-driven alveolar fluid clearance protects against HAPE [29]. Experts now view HAPE as requiring two co-existing defects: (1) defective pulmonary vascular function (uneven, exaggerated HPV with capillary leaking) and (2) defective alveolar fluid clearance; exaggerated pulmonary hypertension alone is not sufficient to trigger HAPE [25]. Oxidative stress also appears contributory: plasma VEGF and malondialdehyde (MDA) increased in response to hypoxic exercise only in the HAPE-susceptible group in one physiological study [16]. Subclinical pulmonary dysfunction (reduced FVC and alveolar volume) has also been observed in HAPE-susceptible non-mountaineers, suggesting some susceptibility reflects occult lung disease [31].

## 3. Documented Cases Across the Historical Record

### 3.1 Historical Milestones

HAPE was recognized in the 19th century but initially misattributed to "high-altitude pneumonia." Key historical landmarks include [2][14]:

- **1891:** Dr. Jacottet died on Mont Blanc; the autopsy is considered the first documented HAPE case.
- **1913:** Ravenhill described "puna" of the cardiac type in South America.
- **1960:** Charles Houston's landmark NEJM paper, "Acute pulmonary edema of high altitude," and Hultgren & Spickard's contemporaneous report identified HAPE as acute pulmonary edema without heart disease, distinguishing it from pneumonia.
- **1991:** The International Hypoxia Symposium in Banff established the first standardized AMS diagnostic criteria (Lake Louise Consensus, updated 2018).

### 3.2 Case Series in Ski Resort and Ski-Mountaineering Populations

**Hultgren et al. — Colorado ski resort (2,928 m).** Medical records of 150 HAPE patients over 39 months at a Colorado Rocky Mountain ski area showed: mean age 34.4 years; 84% male; mean time to symptom onset 3 ± 1.3 days after arrival; common symptoms were dyspnea, cough, headache, chest congestion, nausea, fever, weakness; rales in 85%; pulmonary infiltrates in 88% (most commonly bilateral or right-sided); mean SpO₂ on presentation was 74% (range 38–93%); and 14% had concurrent cerebral edema symptoms. With oxygen treatment and/or descent, there were **no deaths or complications** [5].

**Honigman et al. — Summit County, Colorado (1983).** Twenty-nine cases among visitors to resorts at 2,743–3,354 m: all male, mean age 37.8 years, all residing below 915 m. This series proposed two HAPE types: Type 1 "nonresident-ascent" HAPE (adult male visitors ascending above ~2,439 m) and Type 2 "resident-reascent" HAPE (high-altitude residents, often children, returning after a sojourn at low altitude) [35].

**Uriza — 10-case series at 2,600 m.** Radiographic features showed airspace consolidation most frequently involving the upper zones, resolving 48–72 hours with oxygen therapy alone; pulmonary capillary pressure exceeded 20 mm Hg in susceptible individuals [23].

### 3.3 Individual Case Reports Directly Relevant to Ski Mountaineering

**Sierra Nevada ski station (Archivos de Bronconeumología).** A 40-year-old male athlete ascended to 3,300 m in under 3 hours and skied intensively for 48 hours before developing HAPE with dyspnea, cough, tachycardia, hypoxemia, and low-grade fever. Chest imaging showed bilateral reticular cotton-wool infiltrates and ground-glass opacities; echocardiography revealed pulmonary hypertension with estimated sPAP ~70 mm Hg. He improved within 24 hours on oxygen and supportive care, was discharged asymptomatic after 3 days, and had a normal one-year follow-up [32]. This case is the clearest published illustration of the ski-mountaineering risk scenario: rapid ascent + intense exercise + cold.

**Himalayan trekker (Shrestha et al., 2014).** A 55-year-old woman trekking in the Khumbu region continued ascending through an intercurrent respiratory infection despite progressive symptoms, taking acetazolamide 250 mg twice daily. She required helicopter evacuation; echocardiography showed PAP of 50 mm Hg. She recovered with bed rest, oxygen, and sustained-release nifedipine 20 mg twice daily. The authors emphasized that acetazolamide does not prevent HAPE, descent is the golden rule, and intercurrent illness increases risk [33].

**Native highlander (IJMS).** A 41-year-old Nepali trekking porter of Rai ethnicity, born and raised in the Himalaya, developed HAPE at ~5,000 m after a 2-week hiatus at 3,420 m, becoming unconscious (GCS 3/15, undetectable SpO₂). He recovered after ICU care with mechanical ventilation. The case demonstrates that even native highlanders with prior HAPE history remain susceptible — "re-entry" HAPE risk applies to high-altitude residents as well [34].

**Experienced mountaineer (Whitlow & Davis, 2014).** A mountaineer with extensive experience above 2,500 m and no prior HAPE developed HAPE ascending to 3,200 m, illustrating that prior tolerance does not guarantee protection and that rapid ascent plus genetic predisposition can overcome experience [36].

**Auto-PEEP vs oxygen (Tannheimer & Lechner, 2022).** Cross-case comparison of two HAPE patients: a 55-year-old at Mera Peak (5,450 m) treated with oxygen (SpO₂ rose from ~64% to 97% within 2 minutes) and a 33-year-old at Denali (4,380 m) treated with a 30-minute Auto-PEEP breathing maneuver (2-second inspiration, 8-second expiration against pursed-lips resistance generating ~55 cm H₂O), which raised SpO₂ from <65% to ~80%, then slowly to 95%, with sustained effect for ~120 minutes. The Denali patient summited 4 days later without medication [37].

## 4. Acute and Long-Term Treatment

### 4.1 Non-Pharmacological Acute Treatment

**Descent** is the single best treatment. Guidelines recommend descending at least 1,000 m or until symptoms resolve, minimizing exertion during descent [15][39][47]. Symptomatic improvement can occur with as little as 500–1,000 m of descent [2][3].

**Supplemental oxygen** targeting SpO₂ >90% is the most effective and reliable adjunct when descent is delayed or impossible [15][39][47]. In the ski-resort series, oxygen alone (2–4 hours) was sufficient for most patients [5]. In the Mera Peak case, 3 L/min raised SpO₂ from ~64% to 97% within 2 minutes [37].

**Portable hyperbaric chambers (Gamow bag)** simulate a descent of ~1,500 m and are a temporizing measure when descent is impossible; the Gamow bag is the only FDA-approved soft-sided chamber for altitude illness, operating at ~2 PSI with the patient inside for 1–2 hours [41][42][43]. The first reported use by non-physician EMS personnel (park rangers) was in Sequoia and Kings Canyon National Parks for HAPE and HACE [41]. The UIAA emphasizes that chamber treatment buys time and is not a substitute for descent [43].

**CPAP.** The 2025 HAPER CAPER randomized controlled trial — conducted in three Colorado ski-resort emergency departments (Keystone 2,828 m, Breckenridge 2,926 m, Copper 2,950 m) — compared high-flow oxygen plus CPAP (10 cm H₂O) vs high-flow oxygen alone in 64 adults with HAPE (mean SpO₂ 67%). Both therapies were highly effective, with clinical resolution in ~158 vs ~178 minutes (p = 0.297); CPAP conferred **no additional benefit** over high-flow oxygen alone, and the authors suggested that routine CPAP use for standard HAPE cases is not warranted. The 2024 WMS guidelines make only a weak recommendation for CPAP as an adjunct in refractory cases [39][40].

**Auto-PEEP** (pursed-lips expiratory resistance) is an emerging, equipment-free rescue technique that improved SpO₂ comparably to oxygen in a 2022 case comparison; experts suggest performing it every 2 hours for 30 minutes when HAPE is suspected [37].

### 4.2 Pharmacological Treatment

**Nifedipine.** The landmark 1991 NEJM trial by Bärtsch et al. demonstrated that prophylactic nifedipine reduced HAPE incidence from 63–64% to 10% in susceptible mountaineers [13][44]. For acute treatment, however, a prospective cross-sectional study of 110 Indian soldiers with HAPE found that adding oral nifedipine to descent + oxygen + bed rest provided **no additional benefit** for time to SpO₂ normalization, hospital stay, or radiographic resolution (P > .05 for all) [38]. The WMS therefore recommends nifedipine (30 mg extended-release q12h) as adjunctive therapy only when descent is delayed and oxygen/chamber are unavailable — not as routine monotherapy [15][39][47]. Nifedipine does not prevent AMS [64].

**PDE5 inhibitors (sildenafil, tadalafil).** In the landmark Maggiorini 2006 RCT at 4,559 m, prophylactic **tadalafil 10 mg twice daily** reduced HAPE incidence to 1/8 subjects vs 7/9 with placebo, while **dexamethasone 8 mg twice daily** prevented HAPE in all 10 subjects [45]. However, a systematic review of PDE5 inhibitors and dexamethasone for HAPE concluded that neither class significantly alters outcomes in established HAPE, and one high-altitude trial (Bates et al., 5,200 m) found sildenafil 50 mg three times daily did not significantly reduce pulmonary artery systolic pressure and was associated with more severe AMS symptoms on day 2 [42][46]. WMS 2024 lists tadalafil 10 mg q12h or sildenafil 50 mg q8h as alternatives to nifedipine for prophylaxis, not primary treatment [39][47].

**Dexamethasone.** Best evidence is for prophylaxis: 0/10 treated subjects developed HAPE vs 7/9 placebo in Maggiorini 2006, and a 2025 narrative review of 13 RCTs concluded dexamethasone is most effective at altitudes >4,000 m at doses of 8–16 mg/day, with no serious adverse events in short-term use [45][48]. It is not routinely recommended for isolated HAPE treatment, but is required when HAPE coexists with HACE [47].

**Acetazolamide.** Not recommended for HAPE prevention or treatment in the 2024 WMS guidelines; it facilitates acclimatization and prevents AMS but is "an ineffective substitute for pulmonary vasodilators in HAPE-susceptible individuals" [3][47]. The Shrestha trekker case demonstrated HAPE occurring despite acetazolamide 250 mg twice daily [33]. It may be considered for re-entry HAPE prevention in those with a history [39][47].

**Salmeterol.** Prophylactic inhaled salmeterol (125 µg twice daily) reduced HAPE incidence by >50% in the Sartori 2002 RCT and supports the alveolar-fluid-clearance mechanism, but it is no longer recommended by WMS 2024 (side effects, inferior efficacy); Pennardt 2013 graded it 2B as a supplement to nifedipine only [15][29][47][53].

**Agents to avoid.** Diuretics/furosemide are discouraged (patients are often volume-depleted); morphine and nitrates are not recommended [2][15][47]. Furosemide was nevertheless used successfully in the Sierra Nevada case, but this is not guideline-supported [32].

### 4.3 Emerging Therapies (2020–2026)

- **SWIFTARC trial (NCT06040268):** An ongoing University of Colorado phase 1/2 trial testing **Advair HFA (salmeterol/fluticasone 126/270 µg twice daily)** to prevent HAPE and enhance exercise performance under simulated high altitude, based on the salmeterol mechanism plus anti-inflammatory effects [50].
- **Inhaled nitric oxide (iNO):** Reverses hypoxic pulmonary vasoconstriction selectively in the pulmonary circulation without systemic vasodilation; mechanistically plausible for HAPE but not yet studied as a HAPE treatment [51].
- **Inhaled fasudil (Rho-kinase inhibitor):** In PAH patients, inhaled fasudil 30 mg significantly reduced mean PAP comparably to iNO, with a different mechanism; no HAPE-specific trials yet [52].
- **CPAP/oxygen refinement:** The HAPER CAPER trial (2025) is the first RCT of CPAP for HAPE and effectively de-escalated routine CPAP use [40].
- **Auto-PEEP:** Equipment-free self-administered treatment requiring validation [37].

## 5. Acclimatization and Prevention Protocols

### 5.1 Graduated Ascent Schedules

Gradual ascent is the primary prevention strategy for all altitude illness, backed by strong recommendations in the WMS 2019 and 2024 guidelines [36][39][49][53]. The consensus rules for ski mountaineers and trekkers above 3,000 m:

- Do not increase sleeping elevation by more than **500 m/day** above 3,000 m; take a **rest day every 3–4 days** ("climb high, sleep low") [36][39][49][53].
- More conservative options: max 300–350 m/day (Pennardt 1C) or 300 m/day with a rest day every third day [15][65].
- The CDC recommends avoiding a single-day ascent to a sleeping altitude ≥2,750 m, then ≤500 m/night above 3,000 m, with an extra night for every 1,000 m gained [54].
- The UIAA advises 300–500 m/night above 2,500–3,000 m, with two nights at the same altitude after every 2–4 days of ascent [63].
- A 2022 multicenter proposal, the **"400 m rule,"** suggests limiting sleeping-altitude gain to ~400 m/day after 2,700 m, combined with a clinico-physiological score and an acetazolamide decision tree for individuals at risk of severe altitude illness [56].
- Never ascend with symptoms ("don't go up until symptoms go down"); descend if symptoms worsen [3][66].
- Avoid alcohol, sleeping pills, and heavy exertion in the first days above 3,000 m [1][4][19].

For ski tourists specifically, acclimatizing at intermediate altitude (e.g., an overnight in Denver at 1,600 m before proceeding to resorts >2,800 m), sleeping at lower elevations, and choosing lower-altitude resorts reduce risk [60].

### 5.2 Pre-Acclimatization Strategies

**Staged ascent and pre-acclimatization** at 2,200–3,000 m for ~1 week reduces AMS risk and is recommended when feasible; short intermittent hypoxic exposures (15–60 min) are unlikely to help, while longer daily exposures (>8 h/day for >7 days) are more likely to confer benefit [9][53].

**Hypoxic tents and intermittent hypoxic exposure (IHE):** The WMS 2024 guidelines issued a **non-recommendation for hypoxic tents** (strong recommendation, moderate-quality evidence), because no placebo-controlled studies demonstrated significant benefit [62]. However, a participant-blinded RCT (Treml et al. 2020) found that just **1 hour/day of normobaric hypoxia (FiO₂ 0.11) for 7 days** reduced Lake Louise Scores on later re-exposure, with a number-needed-to-treat of 2.7 (superior to acetazolamide's NNT of 8), and the benefit carried over for at least one week [58]. A 2026 meta-analysis of seven controlled studies estimated that cumulative pre-acclimatization of ~200 hours at simulated altitude (2,200–5,000 m) may reduce subsequent AMS risk to nearly zero, with effects scaling with total exposure time [57]. For extreme-altitude sojourns, ~300 hours of intermittent hypoxic conditioning may be optimal, and exposures should not be terminated earlier than 1–2 weeks before departure [56].

### 5.3 Pharmacological Prophylaxis

For individuals with a history of HAPE who must ascend rapidly, WMS 2024 recommends [39][49][54]:

- **First line:** Nifedipine extended-release 30 mg q12h, started the day before ascent and continued 4–7 days at altitude (or until descent). This is based on the landmark RCT reducing incidence from 63–64% to 10% [44].
- **Second line:** Tadalafil 10 mg q12h, or sildenafil 50 mg q8h [39][45][47].
- **Third line:** Dexamethasone 8 mg q12h (short stays <5 days; 0/10 developed HAPE in the Maggiorini trial) [45][47][48].
- **Acetazolamide** (125 mg q12h) is first-line for AMS prevention (reducing AMS incidence ~50%), but is not recommended for HAPE prevention except possibly for re-entry HAPE [3][39][47][54][55].
- **Ibuprofen** 600 mg q8h is a weak alternative for AMS prevention in those intolerant to acetazolamide/dexamethasone [39][55].
- **Salmeterol** is no longer recommended for HAPE prophylaxis (WMS 2024) [39][53].
- Competitive athletes should note that acetazolamide, dexamethasone, and several other prophylactics are **WADA-prohibited**, limiting their use in ski-mountaineering competition [9].

### 5.4 Risk Stratification Tools

**Prior history is the single strongest predictor** of altitude illness, and prior HAPE carries a 60% recurrence risk with rapid ascent above 4,500 m [3][7][56]. Other validated tools include:

- **WMS risk classification (2024):** Low risk = no prior history + gradual ascent; moderate/high risk = history of altitude illness, sleeping ≥2,800 m on day 1, or ascending ≥500 m/day above 3,000 m [36][39].
- **Canouï-Poitrine Severe High-Altitude Illness (SHAI) score:** A 10-variable pre-acclimatization risk score (range 0–12, cut-off 5) predicting severe altitude illness in sea-level residents [59].
- **Richalet hypoxic exercise test:** Measures desaturation, ventilatory response, and cardiac response to hypoxia at rest and exercise; used to predict severe altitude illness. In a 2022 systematic review of 8 studies (3,558 subjects), severe desaturation (≥22%), low ventilatory response (HVRe <0.78 L/min/kg), and low cardiac response (HCRe <0.84 beats/min/%) were independent risk factors [60].
- **Stress Doppler echocardiography:** HAPE-susceptible individuals show exaggerated PASP responses to both hypoxia and exercise; Grünig et al. (2000) proposed this as a noninvasive screening method, and Mounier et al. (2011) found a PASP increase >19 mm Hg with hypoxic exercise discriminated susceptible from resistant mountaineers with no overlap [16][61]. Individuals with mean PAP >35 mm Hg or systolic PAP >50 mm Hg should avoid altitudes above 2,500 m [15].
- **Pulmonary function testing:** HAPE-susceptible subjects had lower FVC and alveolar volume, and 16/25 had abnormal PFTs in one series, suggesting that some "susceptibility" reflects subclinical lung disease that should be investigated in recurrent cases [31].
- **Genetic screening:** No established clinical role; susceptibility is partly genetic, and testing is not recommended [3].
- **Lung ultrasound** comet-tail scores at altitude distinguish HAPE from healthy controls and can detect subclinical edema [15].

### 5.5 Recurrence Prevention and Long-Term Management

Recurrence is the central concern after a first HAPE episode: ~60% recurrence with the same rapid ascent profile. Prevention of recurrence combines all of the above: slow ascent (<500 m/day sleeping gain), staged acclimatization, and pharmacological prophylaxis (nifedipine or tadalafil) for any rapid ascent [3][7][15][39][54]. Additional long-term measures:

- After a HAPE episode, especially at relatively low altitude or with recurrence, perform **echocardiography and pulmonary function testing** to exclude PFO, intracardiac shunts, pulmonary hypertension, and subclinical lung disease [3][15][31].
- Avoid altitude travel during or immediately after respiratory infections [15][33].
- Avoid altitudes >2,500 m if resting mean PAP >35 mm Hg or systolic PAP >50 mm Hg [15].
- Acclimatized residents returning to altitude after >2 weeks at low altitude (re-entry HAPE) should consider acetazolamide (per WMS 2024) and staged re-ascent [39][47].
- For high-altitude residents, children, and re-entry scenarios, nifedipine or tadalafil prophylaxis may be appropriate [12][47].
- There is no permanent lung damage after prompt treatment; complete recovery is the rule, and most patients can safely return to altitude with appropriate preventive strategies [2][3][32].

## 6. Conclusion

In ski mountaineers operating at ≥3,000 m, HAPE is best understood as a hydrostatic, non-cardiogenic pulmonary edema driven by exaggerated and non-uniform hypoxic pulmonary vasoconstriction, with crucial contributions from endothelial dysfunction (low NO, high endothelin-1), sympathetic overactivation, and impaired alveolar fluid clearance. Genetic susceptibility is real but polygenic and not yet clinically actionable. The ski-mountaineering context adds two potent precipitating factors — intense exercise and cold exposure — both of which augment pulmonary artery pressure, as documented in the Sierra Nevada case and Colorado ski-resort case series (150 patients, zero deaths with prompt oxygen/descent). Treatment rests on descent and oxygen, with nifedipine/PDE5 inhibitors as adjuncts when descent is delayed; CPAP adds no benefit over high-flow oxygen alone (2025 RCT). Prevention is the cornerstone: graduated ascent (≤500 m/day sleeping gain above 3,000 m, rest days), pre-acclimatization (~200 hours if using simulated altitude), risk stratification by prior history and, where available, hypoxic exercise testing, and nifedipine/tadalafil prophylaxis for susceptible individuals. The evidence base is strongest for the hemodynamic paradigm (RCT-grade prophylaxis data), moderate for the alveolar-clearance mechanism (one positive RCT), and limited for most genetic associations.
