# Climate Disclosure Regulation in 2026: SEC Climate Disclosure Rule, EU CSRD, and California SB-253/SB-261/SB-219 — A Comparative Analysis

## Executive Summary

As of August 2026, the global climate disclosure landscape is defined by profound divergence among the three regimes examined in this report:

- **The U.S. SEC climate disclosure rule** (adopted March 2024) **never took effect**. It remains stayed pending litigation, the SEC voted in March 2025 to end its defense, and on May 29, 2026, the SEC formally proposed to **rescind the rule in its entirety**. A final rescission decision is unlikely before late 2026 or early 2027. The SEC's 2010 interpretive guidance on climate risk disclosure remains in effect. [1][2][3]

- **The EU Corporate Sustainability Reporting Directive (CSRD)** is in force and was substantially amended by the **Omnibus I Directive (EU) 2026/470**, in force since March 18, 2026. The scope has been narrowed to the largest companies only (more than 1,000 employees **and** more than €450 million net turnover), with first reports under the new scope due in 2028 for financial year 2027. Double materiality, limited assurance, and ESRS-aligned reporting remain core features. [4][5]

- **California's climate disclosure suite — SB 253 and SB 261, as amended by SB 219** — is **operative and being implemented**. The California Air Resources Board (CARB) approved initial implementing regulations on February 26, 2026, then deferred the first SB 253 Scope 1/2 reporting deadline from August 10 to **November 10, 2026**. SB 261 enforcement is currently **paused by a Ninth Circuit injunction** pending a constitutional challenge, but SB 253 obligations continue. [6][7]

For multinational enterprises, the practical consequence is that **voluntary and mandatory reporting obligations are expanding even as the SEC rule recedes**, and companies above the California and EU thresholds face overlapping, partially aligned, but not identical compliance obligations. This report provides a full comparison across timelines, assurance, materiality, enforcement, costs, and strategic implications.

## Section 1: Current Legal and Regulatory Status (as of August 2026)

### 1.1 SEC Climate Disclosure Rule — Never Effective, Formal Rescission Proposed

The SEC adopted the climate disclosure rule (Release No. 33-11275) on March 6, 2024, in a 3-2 vote. It received over 24,000 comment letters — the most in SEC history — and created new Subpart 1500 of Regulation S-K and Article 14 of Regulation S-X. [15]

Within weeks of adoption, litigation was consolidated in the **Eighth Circuit** (*Iowa v. SEC*), and the SEC voluntarily stayed the rule's effectiveness in April 2024. [1] In **March 2025**, Acting Chair Mark Uyeda directed staff to end the SEC's defense of the rule; the Commission voted 2-1 to do so on March 27, 2025. [4]

The critical development in 2026:

- **May 4, 2026**: The SEC submitted a proposed rule to rescind the climate disclosure rules to OIRA for review.
- **May 29, 2026**: The SEC formally proposed **rescission in its entirety** (Release No. 33-11421; 91 FR 33296, published June 3, 2026). The comment period closed **August 3, 2026**. [2][3][5]
- The rescission proposal cites two independent grounds: (1) the rules exceeded the SEC's statutory authority (including under the major questions doctrine), and (2) the rules were unnecessary and detrimental to capital formation and materiality-based disclosure.
- The underlying Eighth Circuit litigation remains in abeyance; the court has asked the SEC to resolve the rules' fate through notice-and-comment rulemaking or renewed defense. [1]
- **The rules have never been in effect; no filings have ever been due under them.** The likely outcomes are formal repeal, court-ordered vacatur, or both. The SEC estimates the rescission would generate approximately **$4.9 billion in annualized cost savings** over ten years. [2][3]
- **Critically, the SEC's 2010 interpretive guidance (Release No. 33-9106) remains in effect**, requiring public companies to disclose material climate risks under existing Regulation S-K items. [1][53]

### 1.2 EU CSRD — Amended by Omnibus I, Scope Significantly Narrowed

The CSRD (Directive (EU) 2022/2464) entered into force on January 5, 2023, replacing the Non-Financial Reporting Directive (NFRD). The European Commission's February 26, 2025 **Omnibus simplification package** proposed sweeping changes, driven by the goal to cut administrative burdens by at least 25% for all companies and 35% for SMEs. [41][54]

The legislative outcome:

- **Directive (EU) 2025/794** ("Stop-the-Clock") delayed CSRD reporting by two years for Waves 2 and 3 (published April 16, 2025).
- **Directive (EU) 2026/470** ("Omnibus I") was adopted on February 24, 2026, published February 26, 2026, and entered into force **March 18, 2026**. [4][7]
- Member states must transpose the amended CSRD by **March 19, 2027**. [4][8]

The new scope thresholds are the most consequential change: only companies exceeding **both** 1,000 average employees **and** €450 million net annual turnover are in scope (for FY beginning on/after January 1, 2027). [4][5] The Commission estimates roughly 80% of previously in-scope companies (approximately 35,000–42,000 entities) fall out of scope, leaving approximately 5,000–6,000 large undertakings. [8][21]

The revised (simplified) ESRS were published as a delegated act on **July 3, 2026** and apply from FY2027, reducing mandatory datapoints by over 60% and total datapoints by over 70%. [6][42] The requirement to adopt sector-specific ESRS was removed, and the planned transition to **reasonable assurance was permanently removed** — limited assurance remains the standard. [4][6]

### 1.3 California — SB 253 and SB 261 Operative; SB 261 Enforcement Paused; SB 219 as the Amending Statute

California's climate disclosure framework is a two-statute package amended by a third:

- **SB 253 (Climate Corporate Data Accountability Act)** — requires US entities "doing business in California" with annual global revenues over **$1 billion** to disclose Scope 1, 2, and 3 GHG emissions to CARB.
- **SB 261 (Climate-Related Financial Risk Act)** — requires entities with revenues over **$500 million** to publish biennial TCFD-aligned climate financial risk reports.
- **SB 219** (signed September 27, 2024) amended both laws: it retained the 2026 start for Scope 1/2 reporting; allowed consolidated parent-level reporting; gave CARB flexibility on Scope 3 timing; made third-party contracting optional; and separated fee due dates from filing deadlines. [11][12][17]

As of August 2026:

- **CARB rulemaking is largely complete for 2026.** CARB unanimously approved the initial implementing regulations on February 26, 2026, then withdrew and modified them, deferring the first SB 253 reporting deadline from August 10 to **November 10, 2026** (contingent on OAL approval). [6][13][7]
- **SB 253 is fully operative.** Companies must prepare for the November 10, 2026 deadline for Scope 1 and 2 reporting (FY2025 data for most entities). [6][7]
- **SB 261 enforcement is paused.** On November 18, 2025, the Ninth Circuit granted an injunction pending appeal in *Chamber of Commerce v. Sanchez* (No. 25-5327), halting enforcement of SB 261's January 1, 2026 deadline. Oral argument was heard January 9, 2026; no decision has been issued. CARB will set an alternate reporting date after the appeal resolves and has opened a voluntary reporting docket. [6][10][51]
- CARB's preliminary list identifies **over 3,100 companies** potentially subject to the laws. [51]
- The lawsuit also challenges SB 253, but the Ninth Circuit declined to enjoin SB 253; SB 253 compliance obligations continue. [6][10]

## Section 2: Plain-Language Descriptions of Each Framework

### 2.1 SEC Climate Disclosure Rule

The SEC rule would have required US-listed public companies (domestic and foreign registrants) to include climate-related disclosures in their registration statements and annual reports (Form 10-K/20-F). Under Regulation S-K Items 1501–1506, affected companies would have disclosed: (1) climate-related risks reasonably likely to have a material impact on business, strategy, or financial condition; (2) board and management oversight of climate risks; (3) transition plans, scenario analysis, and internal carbon prices (if used); (4) Scope 1 and 2 GHG emissions (for large accelerated and accelerated filers, if material); (5) climate targets and goals; and (6) financial statement footnote disclosures (Article 14 of Regulation S-X) covering severe weather impacts above a **1% threshold** and carbon offset/REC costs. Scope 3 emissions disclosure was dropped from the final rule. All disclosures would have been tagged in Inline XBRL. [15][16][24]

Because the rule is stayed and proposed for rescission, no company currently faces obligations under it. However, the SEC's 2010 interpretive guidance still requires material climate risk disclosure under existing rules. [1][53]

### 2.2 EU CSRD

The CSRD is the EU's landmark sustainability disclosure law. Under the post-Omnibus framework, **very large companies** (over 1,000 employees and over €450 million net turnover) must publish sustainability information in their management reports according to the European Sustainability Reporting Standards (ESRS). Non-EU parent groups with over €450 million EU turnover and an EU subsidiary/branch above €200 million turnover must report from FY2028.

Five core requirements define the regime:
1. **Integration** — sustainability disclosures within the annual management report; 
2. **ESRS alignment** — disclosure per 12 standards (ESRS 1/2, E1–E5, S1–S4, G1), now simplified;
3. **Double materiality assessment** — companies must assess both how sustainability issues affect the business (financial materiality) and how the business affects people and planet (impact materiality);
4. **External assurance** — limited assurance by a statutory auditor or independent assurance services provider;
5. **Digital tagging** — Inline XBRL machine-readable tagging. [16][21]

The CSRD was designed to fix the NFRD's "accountability gap" — insufficient, incomparable, and untrustworthy sustainability reporting — and to support the EU's sustainable finance agenda, including the SFDR and EU Taxonomy. [16]

### 2.3 California SB 253/SB 261/SB 219

**SB 253** is a GHG emissions disclosure law: US companies (public or private, including US subsidiaries of foreign parents) doing business in California with over **$1 billion** in global annual revenues ("gross receipts," per California Revenue and Taxation Code § 25120(f)(2)) must annually disclose Scope 1, 2, and 3 emissions calculated per the GHG Protocol. Scope 1/2 reporting begins with the November 10, 2026 deadline; Scope 3 begins in 2027 (initially limited to 5 of 15 GHG Protocol categories). [7][17][11]

**SB 261** is a climate risk disclosure law: entities with over **$500 million** in annual revenues (excluding insurers) must publish biennial reports aligned with the TCFD framework (or equivalents such as IFRS S2 or CSRD reporting), covering governance, strategy, risk management, and metrics/targets, with an emphasis on physical and transition risks and mitigation measures. First reports were statutorily due January 1, 2026, but enforcement is paused pending litigation. [7][19]

**SB 219** is the 2024 technical amendment that made the regime workable for covered entities: it allows consolidated parent-level reporting (relieving subsidiaries), extended CARB's guidance deadline, gave CARB flexibility on Scope 3 timing, and eliminated advance fee payments. It did **not** delay the first reporting year. [11][12][17]

## Section 3: Detailed Comparison by Dimension

### 3.1 Reporting Timelines and Phased Implementation

#### SEC Climate Rule (Original Schedule — Now Stayed/Proposed for Rescission)

The original compliance dates, based on fiscal years beginning (FYB) in a given calendar year, were phased by filer status [20][24]:

- **Large Accelerated Filers** (public float ≥ $700M): most disclosures for FYB on/after January 1, 2025 (first filings in 2026); GHG emissions for FYB on/after January 1, 2026; limited assurance for FYB on/after January 1, 2029; reasonable assurance for FYB on/after January 1, 2033.
- **Accelerated Filers** (float $75M–$700M): most disclosures FYB on/after January 1, 2026; GHG emissions FYB on/after January 1, 2028; limited assurance FYB on/after January 1, 2031; no reasonable assurance required.
- **Smaller Reporting Companies, Emerging Growth Companies, Non-Accelerated Filers**: most disclosures FYB on/after January 1, 2027; exempt from GHG emissions and assurance.

**None of these dates are operative** because the rule was stayed in April 2024 and never became effective. [1]

#### EU CSRD — Post-Omnibus Timeline

Under the amended directive [4][7][21]:

- **Wave 1** (large public-interest entities with 500+ employees, previously under NFRD): already reporting — FY2024 reports were published in 2025. Member states may exempt Wave 1 entities now below the new thresholds from FY2025–2026 reporting.
- **Wave 2** (other large companies): reporting for FY beginning on/after **January 1, 2027**, with first reports published in **2028** (delayed two years by the Stop-the-Clock directive). The simplified ESRS apply.
- **Wave 3** (listed SMEs, small credit institutions, captive insurers): **excluded from mandatory scope** under Omnibus I.
- **Wave 4** (non-EU parent groups with >€450M EU turnover and EU subsidiary/branch >€200M turnover): reporting for FY beginning on/after **January 1, 2028**, first reports in **2029**. The Commission has not yet adopted the non-EU ESRS (nESRS); expectations for completion have slipped [4][21][8].
- Member state transposition deadline: **March 19, 2027**. Revised ESRS apply from FY2027, with voluntary early application possible for FY2026. [6][42]
- The EU limited assurance standard deadline was postponed from October 1, 2026 to **July 1, 2027**. [4][6]

#### California — Operative Deadlines

- **SB 253 Scope 1/2 first reports**: The statutory start was 2026. CARB's initial regulation set an **August 10, 2026** deadline, but on June 24, 2026, CARB withdrew the package from OAL review and proposed a three-month deferral. The **Modified Regulations (July 27, 2026)** set the first-year deadline at **November 10, 2026** — contingent on the 15-day comment period and OAL approval. Most entities report FY2025 data. [6][7][13]
- **SB 253 Scope 3**: Reporting begins **2027**, with CARB proposing an initial phase-in limited to 5 of 15 GHG Protocol categories (Purchased Goods and Services; Fuel- and Energy-Related Activities; Waste Generated in Operations; Business Travel; Employee Commuting). [6][17]
- **SB 261**: Statutorily due January 1, 2026; **enforcement paused** by the Ninth Circuit injunction; CARB will set an alternate reporting date after the appeal resolves. [6][10]
- **2027-and-beyond**: CARB will undertake separate rulemaking to set reporting dates beyond 2026, reporting content/format, and assurance details. A July 21, 2026 workshop previewed an annual November 10 reporting deadline and fiscal-year-based reference dates. [6][17]

### 3.2 Data Verification and Assurance Standards

#### SEC Climate Rule

The rule would have required:

- **Independent attestation providers** (qualified third parties meeting independence requirements) for Scope 1 and 2 GHG disclosures — but only for Large Accelerated Filers and Accelerated Filers, and only where emissions were material.
- **Large Accelerated Filers**: limited assurance (FY2029) escalating to **reasonable assurance (FY2033)** — a six-year escalation.
- **Accelerated Filers**: limited assurance only (FY2031); no reasonable assurance.
- SRCs, EGCs, and non-accelerated filers: exempt from GHG emissions and assurance entirely.
- Financial-statement disclosures (S-X Article 14) were subject to the existing financial statement audit and ICFR regime; all non-financial disclosures were subject to disclosure controls and procedures, with CEO/CFO certifications under Exchange Act Rule 13a-14. [24][20]

The SEC noted a **potentially limited supply of GHG attestation providers** with appropriate qualifications — a capacity concern that also materialized in EU and California contexts. [20]

#### EU CSRD

- **Limited assurance is mandatory from the first year of reporting** for all in-scope companies — a historic first: "For the first time in EU history, sustainability reports must be independently verified, just like financial statements." [26]
- Assurance must be provided by a **statutory auditor or an independent assurance services provider (IASP)**, depending on member state transposition. Auditors must pass an exam and complete at least eight months of practical training in sustainability. [26]
- **The planned transition to reasonable assurance has been permanently removed** under Omnibus I; the Commission's mandate to adopt reasonable assurance standards was deleted. [4][6]
- The EU limited assurance standard (based on ISSA 5000, effective December 2026) is due by **July 1, 2027**. In the interim, CEAOB's September 2024 guidelines govern, aiming to prevent fragmentation across member states. [25][26]
- Assurance covers ESRS compliance, the double materiality assessment process, EU Taxonomy Article 8 disclosures, and digital tagging. [26]

#### California

- **SB 253**: CARB has confirmed that **limited assurance is NOT required for 2026 submissions** (first-year relief). Limited assurance is required for Scope 1 and 2 beginning **2027**, escalating to **reasonable assurance by 2030**. Scope 3 limited assurance is required no earlier than **2030** (CARB may set it earlier). [7][23][6]
- A **safe harbor** protects Scope 3 disclosures made in good faith through 2030. [7][22]
- Assurance providers must be independent third-party verifiers accredited to standards including **ISAE 3000 (Revised), ISAE 3410, ISSA 5000, ISO 14064-3:2019, AICPA AT-C 210/205, and AA1000AS v3**. [6][17]
- **SB 261**: **No third-party assurance or verification requirement**. Reports must be published and linked to CARB's docket; entities may complete reports "to the best of their ability." [19][18]

### 3.3 Materiality Thresholds and Definitions

| Framework | Materiality concept | Definition / test |
|---|---|---|
| SEC | **Financial materiality only** | Supreme Court standard from *TSC Industries* and *Basic v. Levinson*: a matter is material if there is "a substantial likelihood that a reasonable investor would consider it important" or its omission "significantly altered the total mix of information." Scope 1/2 GHG disclosure required only "if material." Financial statement severe weather disclosures used a quantitative 1% threshold. [28][15] |
| CSRD | **Double materiality** | Two lenses: **impact materiality** (how the company affects people and environment — "inside-out") and **financial materiality** (how sustainability affects the company — "outside-in"). A topic is material if it meets either threshold. Retained in full by Omnibus I; the 2026 ESRS revision simplified the mechanics but preserved the concept. [29][6] |
| California | **No materiality concept** | Pure revenue-threshold applicability: SB 253 applies to entities with >$1B global gross receipts (R&T Code § 25120(f)(2)); SB 261 to entities >$500M. Materiality of climate risk is irrelevant to applicability — but not to report content (SB 261 requires disclosure of material climate-related financial risks). [7][19] |

Practical implications for the double materiality assessment (DMA) under CSRD: ESRS 2 requires companies to disclose their process (IRO-1) and the list of covered disclosures (IRO-2). EFRAG's first-year reviews found that **over 40% of reviewed undertakings lacked a robust DMA**, with common errors including treating the DMA as a checklist, aggregating impact and financial scores, inadequate threshold documentation, and omitting value-chain impacts. Under the simplified ESRS, the DMA becomes more top-down, starting from business model and strategy. ESRS E1 (Climate) is treated as "material by default" by regulators such as BaFin; declaring it non-material requires specific justification. [29][42]

### 3.4 Enforcement Mechanisms

#### SEC Climate Rule

- **Enforcement body**: The SEC Division of Enforcement (and state AGs, in a supporting coalition now defending the rule in litigation).
- **Enforcement theories** would have included: disclosure controls/procedures failures (Rule 13a-15); books and records violations (Exchange Act § 13(b)(2)(A)); fraud under § 17(a) of the Securities Act and § 10(b)/Rule 10b-5; and management certification failures (Rule 13a-14). [24]
- **Penalties**: The SEC does not set fixed penalties in rules; penalties are imposed under securities law tiers, inflation-adjusted. As of the January 2025 adjustment (still applicable in 2026 — the July 2026 Federal Register notice confirmed no 2026 adjustment), maximum civil penalties for violations after November 2, 2015 range from $10,360 (natural person, standard tier) to **$1,035,909** (other person, fraud with substantial losses). [30]
- **Enforcement record on climate/ESG**: The SEC's Climate and ESG Enforcement Task Force (2021–2024) brought actions against BNY Mellon ($1.5M), Goldman Sachs ($4M), Activision Blizzard ($35M), JPMorgan ($18M), and Deutsche Bank ($19M for greenwashing), among others. The Task Force was **disbanded** in 2024, with the SEC stating the expertise now resides across the Enforcement Division. [31]
- **Current context**: FY2025 enforcement statistics show 456 total actions (lowest in ~20 years) and reduced monetary relief outside one large ponzi case; climate/ESG enforcement is not a current priority. [3]

#### EU CSRD

- **Enforcement bodies**: Member State competent authorities (typically national securities regulators or audit oversight bodies) have primary enforcement authority; **ESMA** coordinates supervision of listed issuers through its Sustainability Reporting Working Group and enforces via its **Guidelines on Enforcement of Sustainability Information (GLESI)**, effective January 1, 2025. [32]
- **Penalties**: The CSRD requires penalties that are "effective, dissuasive, and proportionate," with amounts set at national level. Fines for non-compliance have been reported at up to **€10 million** in some analyses [14]; by comparison, the related CSDDD caps penalties at 3% of net worldwide turnover (for far larger companies). [8]
- **First-year enforcement activity**: ESMA's 2025 report (published May 2026) found approximately 2,000 issuers' sustainability statements were in scope; enforcers took **enforcement actions toward 109 issuers** in 2025. Most actions related to ESRS E1 (climate change, 40%) and ESRS 2 (general disclosures, 36%), followed by ESRS S1 (own workforce, 8%). Enforcers almost exclusively required **corrections in future statements**, acknowledging the "learning curve." [33]
- **Transposition gaps**: Nine member states had not fully transposed the CSRD as of September 30, 2025 (Germany, Spain, Luxembourg, Malta, Netherlands, Austria, Portugal, Cyprus, Czech Republic), creating uneven enforcement intensity. [21]

#### California

- **Enforcement body**: CARB administers both statutes, with the California Attorney General involved in penalty enforcement. [34]
- **Penalties — SB 253**: Administrative penalties up to **$500,000 per reporting year** for non-filing or late filing. [7][22]
- **Penalties — SB 261**: Up to **$50,000 per entity per year** for inadequate or missing reports. [7][22]
- **First-year enforcement discretion**: CARB will exercise enforcement discretion in 2026 — no penalties for entities demonstrating good-faith compliance efforts. First-year relief allows entities to submit data possessed/collected on or before December 5, 2024, or a letterhead statement if no such data exists. [7][23]
- **Fee enforcement**: Daily penalties for nonpayment of fees (each day counts as a separate violation); CARB may seek injunctions; entities must retain records for five years. [44]
- **Ongoing litigation**: The Ninth Circuit injunction suspends only SB 261 enforcement; the constitutional challenge (compelled speech, preemption, dormant Commerce Clause) could ultimately narrow, invalidate, or uphold all or part of both statutes. ExxonMobil's separate suit is held in abeyance pending the Ninth Circuit decision. [6][10][51]

## Section 5: Real-World Enterprise Compliance Challenges and Cost Estimates

### 5.1 SEC Climate Rule — Cost Analysis and Challenges

**Government/regulatory estimates:**

- The SEC's own economic analysis for the final rule estimated per-registrant annual compliance costs **ranging from under $197,000 to over $739,000** per filer per year over ten years, depending on reporting scope. [36]
- The SEC's 2026 rescission proposal estimates approximately **$4.9 billion in annualized cost savings** from rescission across all affected registrants — effectively the SEC's own estimate of the rule's annual compliance burden. [2][5]
- The earlier 2022 proposal estimated first-year costs of $640,000 per non-SRC registrant ($180,000 internal, $460,000 external) and ~$530,000 annually thereafter. [47]

**Independent and industry critiques:**

- **Competitive Enterprise Institute**: projected compliance costs rising from $3.8 billion to **$10.2 billion annually**; average firms paying ~$865,000 extra per year; roughly 39 million additional work hours; and a potential **$25 billion GDP loss** by the late 2020s. [37]
- **Dr. Matthew Winden (University of Wisconsin-Whitewater), SEC comment letter**: estimated incremental direct costs of **$6.37 billion** — 165% of then-current total SEC compliance costs — and modeled ~$25 billion in annual GDP foregone and 200,000 fewer jobs once fully implemented. [38]
- **National Association of Manufacturers**: argued the rule would impose at least **$4.9 billion in annual compliance costs** on US companies. [39]
- **U.S. Chamber of Commerce** (with former SEC Chief Economist James Overdahl): argued compliance costs would exceed even **Sarbanes-Oxley implementation**, that the cost-benefit analysis was "incomplete and substantially underestimates compliance costs," and that the rules would discourage companies from entering or remaining in US public markets. [40]
- **Commissioner Hester Peirce** projected the rule would "increase the typical external costs of being a public company by around 21 percent." [24]

**Compliance challenges identified** (now largely moot at the federal level, but instructive): GHG data collection systems (spreadsheets insufficient at scale), materiality determinations, climate governance documentation, internal control readiness for future assurance, and attestation provider availability. [20][24] Deloitte's pre-adoption survey found 57% of executives cited data availability/quality as their greatest ESG disclosure challenge; only 21% had an ESG council; 82% said they needed additional resources. [7]

### 5.2 EU CSRD — Cost Analysis and Challenges

**Government/Commission/EFRAG estimates:**

- EFRAG estimated administrative costs of approximately **€740,000 on average for large listed companies**, plus **€430,000 in initial investments**, and a total system-wide cost of **€23.3 billion per year** across all originally in-scope companies. [45]
- The Commission's Omnibus package was projected to reduce administrative costs by approximately **€4.4 billion annually**, with one-off savings of ~€1.6 billion (CSRD/ESRS) and ~€0.9 billion (Taxonomy). [41]
- EFRAG's post-Omnibus analysis projects cumulative savings of **€4.7 billion for FY2027–2032** (a 44% reduction, though critiqued as optimistic); a later EFRAG-commissioned study estimated **€3.7–4.1 billion** in savings over 2027–2031, depending on supply-chain effects. [42][45]
- The revised ESRS (July 2026) are expected to reduce reporting costs per company by over 30%, with mandatory datapoints cut by over 60% and total datapoints by over 70%. [42]

**Independent industry estimates and experience:**

- **Socious** (citing PwC 2025 CSRD Readiness Survey, Deloitte, KPMG, and other sources) estimates: [43]
  - **Large enterprises (10,000+ employees): €500,000–€2 million+ first-year setup costs**; recurring annual costs €200,000–€800,000.
  - Mid-size companies (1,000–10,000 employees): €150,000–€500,000 first-year.
  - "A 2025 PwC survey found **60% of European multinationals exceeded their initial CSRD budget by 30% or more**."
  - Data collection across the value chain consumes **40–60% of total effort**; KPMG found it averages 800–1,500 person-hours for a mid-size multinational, largely manual spreadsheet work.
  - Limited assurance costs: €50,000–€150,000 for large corporates; €150,000–€500,000+ for complex multinationals.
- **One Earth** argues the EFRAG cost figures are unrealistically low, estimating the true cost at **€39 billion per year**, roughly double the EFRAG system-wide figure. [45]
- **German Business Panel** (University of Mannheim): at the end of 2024, **67.6% of companies rated the new reporting rules negatively**; only 12.6% believed the CSRD actually promotes sustainability; more than half of surveyed companies cancelled planned investments due to bureaucratic burden. After the Omnibus reform, over 50% rated the reform positively. [45]
- **PwC's Global Sustainability Reporting Survey 2025** (496 executives, 40 countries): ~40% of planned CSRD reporters said they would delay by two years per the Stop-the-Clock directive; ~40% said they would report voluntarily on the original timeline; over 60% increased investment in resources and senior leadership time; use of AI in sustainability reporting nearly tripled from 11% to 28%. [44]

**Practical compliance challenges**: Scope 3 data across value chains; cross-team coordination between finance and sustainability; audit trail readiness; value-chain data gaps; assurance provider capacity; the need to start assurance readiness at least six months early; and consequences of qualified/adverse assurance opinions (regulatory scrutiny, reputational damage, director liability). [43][26]

### 5.3 California — Cost Analysis and Challenges

**State fiscal data:**

- Implementation of the California laws was estimated to cost the state roughly **$16 million in the first year**, with CARB needing **$9 million in FY2024-25 and $2 million the following year** for SB 253, and **$13.7 million over two fiscal years** for SB 261. [46]
- CARB's proposed fee schedule includes a one-time **$20.7 million repayment** of temporary funding from the Greenhouse Gas Reduction Fund, plus recurring annual program costs of approximately **$13.9 million** — funded by flat annual fees on covered entities. [48]
- Proposed fees: **~$3,106–$3,316 per entity under SB 253** and **~$1,403–$1,443 per entity under SB 261** (some analyses cite a combined $2,000–$7,000 range). Entities over $1B pay both fees; fees are cumulative. [6][43][48]

**Industry estimates:**

- In the Chamber of Commerce litigation, plaintiffs argued that **"Scope 3 reporting alone could exceed $1 million per year per company"** and that the laws create a "patchwork of inconsistent state-by-state regulatory regimes." [47]
- The Newsom administration's 2024 attempt to delay both laws cited "concerns about cost, timeline and the entirely new and significant workload for the state and the entities covered." The proposal was not enacted. [47]
- The largest cost driver is **Scope 3 verification**, which relies on estimates, supplier data, and assumptions of varying quality. UL Solutions and others recommend internal audits of reporting processes, early engagement with assurance providers, and pre-assurance mock audits to avoid capacity bottlenecks. [50]
- Suppliers not directly regulated by SB 253 will still face data requests from covered customers — creating a **value-chain ripple effect** across the California economy. [16][22]
- Entities must verify applicability entity-by-entity (revenue per the lesser of the prior two fiscal years, using California "gross receipts" definitions broader than financial statement revenue), and parent companies may file consolidated reports, including for foreign parents reporting for US subsidiaries. [19][6]

### 5.4 Comparing Cost Estimates — A Note of Caution

The three regimes' cost signals are not directly comparable because they cover different populations and scopes. The SEC's $4.9 billion annualized figure and the CSRD's €4.4 billion savings figure are official regulatory estimates that critics (industry, academics, and NGOs) have repeatedly argued understate real compliance costs by factors of two to ten (e.g., the CEI's $10.2 billion and One Earth's €39 billion figures). The California cost figures are dominated by fee-based cost recovery plus the largely uncosted internal burden of GHG data collection and assurance. For budget planning purposes, multinationals should assume **first-year costs of $1–5 million per large reporting entity** across overlapping regimes, with recurring annual costs of $500,000–$2 million.

## Section 6: Strategic Implications for Multinational Enterprises

### 6.1 The SEC Rescission Does Not Mean Deregulation

The SEC's proposed rescission removes a federal disclosure obligation, but it does **not** reduce the overall regulatory surface. As one law-firm analysis puts it, the SEC proposal "would not eliminate the growing network of climate-related disclosure requirements imposed by state governments, foreign jurisdictions and international standard-setting bodies." [53] Multinationals face: (1) California SB 253 for US entities over $1B revenue; (2) CSRD for EU entities (or non-EU parents with EU groups) above the new thresholds; (3) ISSB-based standards in 36+ jurisdictions; (4) the SEC's 2010 interpretive guidance requiring material climate risk disclosure; and (5) voluntary frameworks (CDP, TCFD/IFRS S2, GRI) increasingly demanded by investors and supply chains. [51][49]

### 6.2 The Overlap Problem: Who Faces What

A typical multinational enterprise with global revenues above $1 billion faces a compliance matrix:

- **US parent doing business in California**: SB 253 (Scope 1/2 by November 10, 2026; Scope 3 from 2027) and, if revenue exceeds $500M, SB 261 (currently paused but potentially reinstated by the Ninth Circuit).
- **EU subsidiary or group with >1,000 employees and >€450M turnover**: CSRD reporting for FY2027 (first report 2028), including ESRS and double materiality.
- **US public parent with ≥$700M float**: no SEC climate rule filing due, but must continue evaluating material climate risk disclosure under the 2010 interpretive guidance and existing S-K requirements.
- **Private equity-owned and private companies**: not subject to SEC requirements but captured by California and, if they have EU operations, by CSRD.

For each of these, the **same underlying data** — a GHG inventory, a climate risk assessment, a transition plan — must be repackaged to meet different materiality tests, assurance standards, and reporting formats.

### 6.3 Strategic Recommendations for Multinationals

**1. Build once, report many (data architecture).** The single most important strategic response is to construct a **unified climate data platform** that can feed EU ESRS, California SB 253, ISSB/IFRS S2, and voluntary frameworks from one auditable source. The GHG Protocol remains the common denominator: California mandates it, CSRD's revised ESRS align its GHG boundaries with IFRS S2 and permit operational control or equity share options, and ISSB references it. Companies that invested in CSRD readiness can reuse much of that infrastructure for SB 253 and vice versa. [43][6][44]

**2. Treat the double materiality assessment as the analytical core.** The CSRD's DMA is the gateway to ESRS disclosures and remains in place after Omnibus I. Simultaneously, SEC's 2010 guidance requires financial materiality analysis, and California requires threshold-based reporting regardless of materiality. A rigorous, documented DMA that produces both impact and financial materiality conclusions can satisfy the EU requirement while also informing SEC materiality judgments and California risk reporting (SB 261). [29][28]

**3. Plan for assurance escalation now.** Both the EU (limited assurance from first year) and California (limited assurance from 2027, reasonable assurance by 2030) require independent third-party assurance. The assurance provider market is constrained; companies should engage providers early, build audit trails, implement internal controls (segregation of duties, reconciliations, version control), and conduct dry runs. Choosing providers accredited across ISAE 3000/ISSA 5000 and ISO 14064-3 maximizes flexibility. [43][26][50]

**4. Manage litigation and rulemaking uncertainty through scenario planning.** The Ninth Circuit's SB 261 decision could arrive at any time and could narrow, invalidate, or uphold the California laws. The SEC's rescission comment period closed August 3, 2026, with a final rule possible in late 2026 or early 2027 — but the 2010 guidance will remain. Companies should maintain "warm" reporting capabilities: even if SB 261 reports are not currently enforceable, preparing them now avoids a scramble if the injunction lifts, and the reports can double as CSRD/ISSB input. [6][1][51]

**5. Expect value-chain data demands regardless of direct applicability.** California's Scope 3 phase-in and the CSRD's value-chain reporting requirements mean that **suppliers and business partners far below the reporting thresholds will receive data requests**. The CSRD's "value-chain cap" limits what in-scope companies may demand from protected undertakings (≤1,000 employees) to the forthcoming voluntary VSME standard — but private companies above the cap but below the CSRD thresholds have no such protection from customer demands. The same dynamics apply to California's five-category Scope 3 phase-in. [52][42]

**6. Align with ISSB as the interoperability layer.** The revised ESRS explicitly align with IFRS S1/S2, and CARB is designing its 2027 framework with "interoperability with IFRS S2 and EU CSRD" in mind. [6][42] Companies reporting under ISSB standards (or voluntarily under IFRS S2 via CDP) will find that ESRS and California disclosures require incremental rather than duplicative work. However, the CSRD retains double materiality, so a pure-ISSB financial-materiality approach is insufficient for EU purposes. [29]

**7. Monitor and participate in the rulemaking pipeline.** Both CARB (2027-and-beyond rulemaking, Scope 3 categories, assurance details) and the EU (revised ESRS scrutiny by Parliament/Council, nESRS for non-EU groups, VSME) have active pipelines through 2027. The EU's next Omnibus iterations may adjust scope further; California's fee and reporting schedules are still in flux. Multinationals should participate in public comment windows (e.g., CARB's August 2026 hearing and 15-day comments; SEC rescission comments closed August 3, 2026) to shape the operational details of the rules that will govern them. [6][1][42]

**8. Governance: one interconnected program.** Because climate disclosures increasingly flow into audited financial statements (EU CSRD integration into management reports; California's GHG data informing financial risk assessment; SEC's 2010 guidance on MD&A and Risk Factors), companies should integrate climate governance into the **finance function** — not leave it in a CSR silo. This includes board-level oversight, executive certifications, internal controls over GHG data, and alignment with the enterprise risk management framework. [20][24][26]

---

## Conclusion

As of August 2026, the three major climate disclosure regimes examined here are at very different points of evolution. The **SEC rule** is in the process of formal rescission, a remarkable reversal of the March 2024 adoption — though the underlying 2010 interpretive guidance preserves a federal floor for material climate disclosure. The **EU CSRD** has been significantly narrowed by Omnibus I, with the scope limited to roughly 5,000–6,000 of the largest companies, but the surviving regime retains its distinctive features: double materiality, ESRS-based reporting, limited assurance, and digital tagging, with first reports under the new scope due in 2028. **California's SB 253 and SB 261** are the most immediately operative in the US, with SB 253's November 10, 2026 deadline now the critical near-term milestone and SB 261 suspended pending Ninth Circuit review.

For multinational enterprises, the strategic imperative is clear: invest in **unified, assurance-ready climate data infrastructure** that can serve multiple jurisdictions; maintain flexible, scenario-based compliance plans that can rapidly adapt to litigation outcomes and rulemaking changes; and recognize that, despite political shifts and deregulatory currents in Washington, the substantive reporting burden of climate disclosure — driven by California, the EU, and the global ISSB movement — continues to expand.
